## Principle 8: Match Regularization and Checkpoint Frequency to the Generalization Regime

*Consensus: 6/7 BELKA writeups reported rapid non-shared overfitting, separate checkpoint policies, or strong sensitivity to depth and training duration. At least 10/14 MoA writeups used early stopping, label smoothing, weight decay, shallow networks, or per-target stopping.*

Tune stopping cadence, capacity, smoothing, and weight decay against the regime that matters. Novel-chemistry validation may peak within a fraction of an epoch, while shared-identity performance can continue improving; multi-label targets may also peak at different epochs.

**Why this matters:** Large models first learn broad patterns and then increasingly exploit identity-specific shortcuts. Sparse labels amplify confidence errors, so infrequent validation can skip the useful checkpoint entirely, and one global epoch can overfit common targets while underfitting rare ones.

**When to use:** Validate frequently when an epoch traverses millions of examples, when grouped CV peaks early, or when target-wise loss curves diverge. Use mild label smoothing and weight decay for log-loss tasks, and consider per-target checkpoint selection if it is implemented without peeking at test outcomes.

**When NOT to use:** Do not stop an interpolation model at the extrapolation optimum when repeated identities dominate its intended use. Conversely, do not intentionally overfit simply because shared-CV rises if the final score is mostly novel chemistry. Avoid excessive smoothing under pure ranking metrics when it weakens ordering without reducing variance.

**How to reason about it:**
Plot metric versus updates separately for each regime and target. Set validation intervals in optimizer steps, not epochs. Choose a checkpoint rule before final training, repeat it across folds, and reject hyperparameters whose apparent optimum moves wildly with tiny seed or interval changes.

best = {"seen": (-float("inf"), None), "unseen": (-float("inf"), None)}
