## Principle 10: Ensemble Residual Diversity, Not Model Names

*Consensus: 5/7 BELKA solutions used multi-model or multi-checkpoint ensembles, while the winner and 8th-place solution showed that a strong single Transformer can remain competitive. Essentially all substantive MoA writeups used blending or stacking, often with MLP, TabNet, ResNet, or CNN diversity.*

Select ensemble members using leakage-free OOF quality and residual/prediction correlation, not architecture labels alone. Prefer a compact blend of strong, complementary representations, validation regimes, or output parameterizations over many near-duplicate seeds.

**Why this matters:** A weaker model can improve a blend if it captures different biology or chemistry, while highly correlated strong models contribute little. DeepInsight CNNs materially helped MoA because their errors differed from shallow NNs; BELKA ensembles gained stability across sequence, graph, and fingerprint views under uncertain non-shared CV.

**When to use:** Blend when OOF predictions share the same folds and the candidate improves optimized or simple-average OOF performance across regimes. Useful diversity sources include local versus global molecular encoders, random versus grouped CV, multi-label versus label-powerset heads, and raw versus engineered assay features.

**When NOT to use:** Do not stack on in-sample predictions or tune weights repeatedly against a public leaderboard. Stacking failed in several BELKA and MoA attempts, and a single well-pretrained Transformer won BELKA. Keep one model when added members are highly correlated, uncalibrated, or operationally too expensive for negligible OOF gain.

**How to reason about it:**
Start with the best robust single model. Add the candidate with the largest validated marginal gain, considering worst-regime performance and inference cost. Optimize nonnegative weights on OOF predictions, compare against equal weights, and use nested validation if optimizing many weights or class-specific blends.
