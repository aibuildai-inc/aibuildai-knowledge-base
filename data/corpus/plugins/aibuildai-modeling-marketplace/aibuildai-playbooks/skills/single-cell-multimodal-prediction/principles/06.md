## Principle 6: Compress Targets Only When Their Effective Rank Justifies It

*Consensus: At least 6/8 top multimodal solutions compressed the 23,418-dimensional Multiome target; only 1/5 top perturbation solutions used target SVD in its final system, while another explicitly found full-target regression better.*

Use target SVD/PCA when thousands of outputs lie near a stable low-rank subspace and sample-level signal matters more than rare gene-specific residuals. Otherwise predict the full response, use a gene-factorized scalar model, or combine a low-rank head with a residual head.

**Why this matters:** Target compression drastically reduces parameters and captures co-regulated programs, but reconstruction discards low-variance directions. That trade-off was favorable for sparse ATAC-to-RNA prediction and less consistently favorable for signed perturbation significance profiles, where individual genes and extreme magnitudes strongly affect rowwise RMSE.

**When to use:** Inspect singular-value decay, out-of-fold reconstruction error, and metric loss from an oracle reconstruction. Use compression when a modest rank preserves almost all attainable score and allows more robust models or faster ensembles.

**When NOT to use:** Avoid it when the oracle inverse transform already loses substantial metric value, when rare gene responses matter, or when the number of training rows makes the estimated subspace unstable. Full-output neural heads and long-form gene-factor models were competitive alternatives in perturbation prediction.

**How to reason about it:**
Before training a regressor, compare `metric(Y, inverse_transform(transform(Y)))` at several ranks. This is an upper bound on any compressed model. Pick rank from metric retention and fold stability, not explained variance alone; optionally train with more latent components and reconstruct with fewer only after validating the effect on ensembles.
