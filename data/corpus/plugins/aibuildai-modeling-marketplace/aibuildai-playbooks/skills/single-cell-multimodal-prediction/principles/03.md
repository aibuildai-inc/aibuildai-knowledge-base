## Principle 3: Normalize According to the Assay, Not by Habit

*Consensus: 7/8 top multimodal solutions explicitly relied on assay-aware normalization or multiple normalized views; perturbation leaders mostly modeled the provided aggregate response instead of reprocessing raw counts.*

For measured modalities, create representations that respect count depth, compositionality, and sparsity: candidates include library-size normalization plus log1p for RNA, TF-IDF/LSI or BM25 for ATAC, CLR, row standardization, and binary accessibility. Keep preprocessing as a cross-validated model choice rather than assuming the organizer transform is optimal.

**Why this matters:** Technical variation in sequencing depth and batch can dominate biological variation. ATAC peaks are sparse incidence-like observations, RNA counts are compositional, and protein targets may contain negative normalized values; the same transform therefore changes signal-to-noise differently in each assay.

**When to use:** Use assay-aware transforms when raw or lightly processed molecular measurements are available. Fit every data-dependent transform inside the training fold when it uses target information or could reveal batch composition, and retain multiple views only when they add OOF diversity.

**When NOT to use:** Do not automatically return to raw single-cell counts for an aggregate differential-expression task. In the perturbation competition, most strong leaderboard solutions used the compact `de_train` table; optional raw and baseline multiome data added complexity and upstream nuisance effects without consistent gains.

**How to reason about it:**
Check row totals, zero fractions, feature prevalence, batch separation, and whether zero means absence or dropout. Compare transforms on hard folds, especially the latest day or held-out cell types. A transform is useful only if it improves those folds rather than merely making random-fold reconstruction easier.
