## Principle 2: Make Validation Reproduce the Hidden Experimental Axis

*Consensus: 13/13 top-eight writeups described a deliberate validation scheme; at least 8/13 explicitly held out donor, day, cell type, compound, or adversarially test-like rows.*

Construct validation folds around the factor absent from training at test time, rather than around randomly selected rows. Evaluate more than one plausible shift when the private split combines axes, such as unseen day plus unseen donor or held-out compounds within rare cell types.

**Why this matters:** Cells from the same donor, day, perturbation, or batch share strong technical and biological structure. Random folds leak that structure and can rank feature sets in the opposite order from a future-day or cross-cell-type test; this caused large public-to-private leaderboard reversals in both competitions.

**When to use:** Use grouped or blocked validation whenever metadata or the experimental design identifies the test missingness. For future-time prediction, validate on the latest available day and on donor–day blocks. For perturbation completion, hold out the scored compounds inside one cell type while leaving only the same sparse support that will be available for test cell types.

**When NOT to use:** A maximally hard split can discard so much data that model comparisons become noisy. Several solutions therefore used a hard, test-like CV for hypothesis selection and ordinary K-fold only to train the final fold ensemble; target-cluster stratification was another compromise when several shifts interacted.

**How to reason about it:**
Build a validation matrix, not a single score. Compare random, entity-grouped, and exact test-simulation folds. Reject gains that occur only on easy folds, inspect per-day and per-cell-type errors, and prefer features whose direction of improvement is stable across the plausible shifts.
