## Principle 8: Validate Under the Scientific Shift, Not Just Random Rows

*Consensus: 12/14 writeups discussed cross-validation, temporal holdouts, source/pH grouping, duplicate control, or a train-test distribution mismatch.*

Construct validation around the mechanism that creates the hidden test set: time-forward annotation gain for prospective function tasks, and held-out parent proteins or study conditions for mutation-property tasks. At minimum keep identical sequences and tightly related variants in one fold.

**Why this matters:** Random folds can leak protein identity, close homologs, study-specific assay baselines, or duplicated sequences. This creates excellent local scores that fail on future annotations or on a single unseen enzyme; the stability writeups repeatedly observed high generic CV that transferred poorly to the competition protein.

**When to use:** For CAFA, use historical database snapshots when possible and group identical or highly similar sequences. For mutation assays, group by wild-type protein, study/source, and relevant conditions such as pH; report both within-family and leave-family-out performance if the test regime is uncertain.

**When NOT to use:** Simple K-fold can remain a useful secondary diagnostic when the prospective benchmark is too small or unstable—two high-ranking CAFA systems found it correlated better with the leaderboard than attempted alternatives. Do not mistake that pragmatic fallback for proof of future generalization.

**How to reason about it:**
Write down the expected hidden-test generation process, then prevent information paths that bypass it. Compare random, identity-grouped, similarity-clustered, temporal, and source-grouped scores. Large gaps are information about deployment risk, not a reason to choose the easiest split.
