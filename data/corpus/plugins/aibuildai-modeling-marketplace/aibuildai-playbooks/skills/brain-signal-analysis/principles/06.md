## Principle 6: Ensemble Diverse Representations and Frequency Ranges for Complementary Strengths

*Consensus: Most source solutions ensemble multiple models (the Melbourne seizure prediction winner rank-averaged 11 models). Parkinson's FOG: the 6th-place ensemble combined spectrogram, wavelet, and 1D conv models; spectrograms good at StartHesitation/Turn, wavelets good at Walking.*

Different representations capture different aspects of the signal. In the FOG 6th-place solution, spectrograms were strong on turn and start-hesitation events, wavelets on walking, and 1D conv models added fine temporal boundaries. Models trained on different feature sets, different resampling rates, or different frequency bands add diversity.

**Why this matters:** One FOG solution: spectrograms scored 0.43/0.37, wavelets 0.39/0.35, 1D conv 0.37/0.29. Ensemble: 0.37/0.46. Spectrograms were strong on StartHesitation and Turn (frequency-structured events), weak on Walking (variable, transient). Wavelets were the opposite. Combining them covered all event types. For EEG, the Melbourne seizure prediction winner blended 11 subject-specific models built on different feature sets (band power, correlations, and other spectral features) with XGBoost and other classic learners, and averaged their ranked predictions to reduce overfitting. Diversity in inputs (feature sets, frequency bands, resampling rates) prevents all models from making the same mistakes.

**When to use:** Always ensemble at least 3-5 diverse models in brain signal tasks (small data, high noise). Prioritize input diversity (1D sequences, spectrograms, wavelets, different hand-crafted feature sets) over many variants of one architecture. Add band and resampling diversity for cheap gains. Rank-average when the models' output scales differ. Use learned ensemble weights (optimize on CV, neural net blend, or GP minimize) rather than uniform average.

**When NOT to use:** Do NOT ensemble 10 nearly identical models (same architecture, same preprocessing, different seeds)—diminishing returns after 3-5. Do NOT ensemble models trained on different data splits if you suspect distribution shift (e.g., one model trained on tdcsfog, one on defog, but test is all defog—weight the defog model higher). Do NOT use uniform weights if models have very different CV scores—downweight weak models.

**How to reason about it:**
Step 1: Train at least one model per major representation (1D raw, spectrogram, wavelets, or hand-crafted feature sets). Step 2: Add diversity within each representation (different frequency bands; different learners; different resolutions, e.g., resampling to 32Hz, 64Hz, and 128Hz as one FOG solution did). Step 3: Optimize blend weights on CV using scipy.optimize.minimize, sklearn Ridge on OOF predictions, or a small neural net (1 linear layer per model + bias term per class). Step 4: Check if certain models are strong on specific classes (e.g., wavelet models for Walking)—if yes, use class-specific weights or multi-head stacking.

# Ensemble with learned weights (optimize on CV)
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def cv_score(weights, oofs, targets):
    blend = sum(w * oof for w, oof in zip(weights, oofs))
    return metric_loss(targets, blend)  # the competition metric, lower is better

# Initialize with equal weights, optimize
w0 = [1/len(oofs)] * len(oofs)
result = minimize(cv_score, w0, args=(oofs, targets), 
                  method='L-BFGS-B', bounds=[(0, 1)] * len(oofs))
weights = result.x / result.x.sum()  # normalize

# Or simple neural net blend
class BlendNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_models, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc = nn.Linear(n_models, 1, bias=False)
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n_classes))
    def forward(self, x):  # (batch, n_classes, n_models)
        return self.fc(x).squeeze(-1) + self.bias
