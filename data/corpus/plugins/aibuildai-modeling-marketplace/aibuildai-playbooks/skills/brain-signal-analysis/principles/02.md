## Principle 2: Choose Frequency Bands for the Task

*Consensus: Seizure prediction solutions computed power in standard EEG bands from delta up to high gamma (up to 180Hz); several Melbourne models applied no filtering, others a wide Butterworth or 60Hz notch filter. The useful frequency range is task-specific.*

Raw EEG contains power line interference (50/60Hz), slow drift, and high-frequency noise, but the task's signal can sit in any band. Decide which frequency range carries the signal before you filter anything away, and describe that range with band features that do not depend on each recording's overall gain.

**Why this matters:** A cutoff that is too low removes real signal: seizure prediction solutions extracted features from bands well above 30Hz (low and high gamma). A narrow filter chosen by habit can therefore delete the most useful information, and one Melbourne solution computed its features with no filtering, de-noising, or re-referencing at all. Recordings also differ in overall amplitude; a Melbourne solution averaged the power spectrum in each band, divided by the total power, and took the logarithm, so that the features describe how power is distributed rather than how loud one recording is.

**When to use:** Always check the frequency content of the classes before choosing a filter: compare average power spectra of positive and negative samples, and keep the bands where they differ. Use relative log band power when recordings or subjects differ in gain. Add a notch at the local line frequency only when that frequency is inside the range you keep.

**When NOT to use:** Do NOT bandpass accelerometer data the same way—movement sensors have different frequency profiles. Do NOT apply a narrow low-pass by default: tasks that depend on fast oscillations need the high bands. Do NOT use relative band power when the absolute power level itself separates the classes; keep total power as an extra feature in that case.

**How to reason about it:**
For EEG: (1) Plot the mean power spectrum per class and find the bands that separate them. (2) Compute band power over those bands, from standard bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, low and high gamma) up to the highest band that still differs between classes. (3) Divide by total power and take the logarithm if gain differs between recordings; keep total power as a separate feature. (4) Filter only when noise inside the kept range hurts CV: a wide Butterworth band-pass, plus a notch at the line frequency. Diversity trick: train different models on different band sets for the ensemble.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt, iirnotch, welch

def bandpass(signal, lowcut, highcut, fs, order=4):
    nyq = 0.5 * fs
    b, a = butter(order, [lowcut / nyq, highcut / nyq], btype='band')
    return filtfilt(b, a, signal, axis=-1)

def notch(signal, line_freq, fs, quality=30):
    b, a = iirnotch(line_freq, quality, fs)
    return filtfilt(b, a, signal, axis=-1)

def relative_log_band_power(clip, fs, bands):
    freqs, psd = welch(clip, fs=fs, axis=-1)
    power = np.stack([psd[:, (freqs >= lo) & (freqs < hi)].mean(axis=1) for lo, hi in bands], axis=1)
    total = power.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return np.log(power / total), np.log(total)
```
