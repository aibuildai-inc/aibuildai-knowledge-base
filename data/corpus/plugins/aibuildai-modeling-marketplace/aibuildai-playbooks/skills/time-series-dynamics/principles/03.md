## Principle 3: Expose Dynamics at Multiple Scales

*Consensus: 5/5 competitions engineered lags, differences, integrals, rolling summaries, periodic references, profile derivatives, or cross-sectional aggregates.*

Give the model compact views of local change, accumulated state, and longer-scale context instead of expecting one raw sequence to preserve every relevant timescale. Choose scales from the system’s known cycles, response delay, forecast horizon, or autocorrelation structure.

**Why this matters:** Long sequences make distant information hard to retain, while raw levels confound scale with dynamics. Differences reveal velocity or slope, integrals approximate accumulated input, rolling statistics summarize local regime and uncertainty, seasonal lags expose periodic recurrence, and cross-sectional averages reveal common shocks.

**When to use:** Use when diagnostics show periodic peaks, delayed response, smooth state evolution, changing volatility, or common movement across entities. Examples include yearly/weekly web seasonality, adjacent atmospheric-level differences, epidemic growth rates, and per-timestamp market averages.

**When NOT to use:** Do not generate a large generic lag matrix without a causal or statistical rationale. Redundant rolling features can increase memory, latency, and overfitting; the Jane Street solution retained only features that improved gap-aware validation, while several web solutions found a few explicit yearly and quarterly lags more effective than complex attention.

**How to reason about it:**
Start with three bands: local changes, medium-window summaries, and domain-scale references. For each feature ask: Is it available at inference? Does its window stay within the entity? Does the validation gain persist across cutoffs? Prefer explicit distant lags when they let the core model use a shorter, better-conditioned sequence.

g = df.groupby("entity_id", group_keys=False)
