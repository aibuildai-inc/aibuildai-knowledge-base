## Principle 11: Prefer Strong Pretrained Representations and Stable Training Over Exotic Heads

*Consensus: 6/6 families relied on pretrained CNNs or transformers; several winners explicitly reported simple heads outperforming more elaborate architectures, while EMA, SWA, GroupNorm, checkpoint averaging, or frozen feature extraction improved stability.*

Start from a representation suited to the modality and resolution, then invest in receptive field, normalization, and stable optimization before designing complex heads. Use pathology-pretrained self-supervised encoders for WSI when available and adapt input stems for biological channels.

**Why this matters:** Biomedical datasets are usually small at the independent-patient or independent-experiment level. Pretraining supplies transferable texture and shape features, while stable weight averaging and batch-size-appropriate normalization reduce variance that otherwise overwhelms architectural gains.

**When to use:** Use ImageNet-pretrained CNN/transformers for fluorescence and segmentation, pathology-pretrained DINO-like features for WSI, GroupNorm when batch size is tiny, and EMA/SWA/checkpoint averaging when validation is noisy. Freeze feature extraction when I/O or inference limits dominate.

**When NOT to use:** Larger models are not automatically better when the task depends on fine relative pixel structure, labels are very noisy, or inference is constrained. Frozen features may fail when channels or stains are far from pretraining; then fine-tune selectively or use self-supervision on in-domain images.

**How to reason about it:**
Establish a small strong backbone baseline. Scale model and input resolution separately to identify which provides the gain. Compare normalization schemes at the real batch size, and evaluate representation quality with retrieval or a linear probe before changing the head.
