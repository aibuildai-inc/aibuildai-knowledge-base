## Principle 11: Prioritize Iteration Speed Over Perfectionism

*Consensus: Explicitly stated by 4/5 adversarial/game-AI winners, implicit in all winners who reported dozens to hundreds of experiments, and a recurring theme in 'what worked' sections.*

Fast, imperfect solutions beat slow, elegant ones. Favor simple implementations, small experiments, and rapid feedback over architectural purity or theoretical optimality early in the competition.

**Why this matters:** You learn by trying, not by thinking. AI Village CTF's winner explicitly chose 'inelegant brute-force' over intellectually satisfying methods to move faster. TrackML's winner noted that supercomputer access didn't help—understanding the problem did. Winners who iterated quickly (dozens of experiments per week) discovered critical insights (threshold tuning, class splits, validation strategies) that slow perfectionists missed. Premature optimization blocks learning.

**When to use:** Early in the competition when you're still understanding the problem, when testing a hypothesis that could invalidate your approach, when trying a new technique for the first time, or when debugging a failure mode.

**When NOT to use:** Late in the competition when you're refining a proven approach, when building production systems (not applicable here), or when your codebase is so messy that adding features introduces bugs faster than you can fix them (refactor then).

**How to reason about it:**
Ask: What is the smallest experiment that tests my hypothesis? Can I prototype this in 1 hour? If implementation takes >1 day, can I simplify or test a proxy first? Use small data subsets, simple models, and coarse hyperparameters for initial tests. Only invest in clean code, large-scale training, and fine-tuning after you've validated the idea on a fast prototype.

```python
# Fast iteration pattern: test on subset first\ndef fast_experiment(new_feature_fn, data, model_class):\n    # Use 10% of data for speed\n    subset = data.sample(frac=0.1, random_state=42)\n    subset = new_feature_fn(subset)  # Test new feature\n    \n    # Simple model, coarse hyperparameters\n    model = model_class(n_estimators=50, max_depth=5)  # Fast, not optimal\n    score = cross_val_score(model, subset, cv=3).mean()\n    \n    print(f'Fast score: {score:.4f}')  # If promising, scale up\n    return score
```
