## Principle 1: Reformulate the Problem at the Right Level of Abstraction

*Consensus: Seen in 4/5 analyzed fraud/risk competitions, 3/3 audio competitions with small-class challenges, and explicitly called out by multiple winners as their breakthrough moment*

The stated task is often not what you're actually predicting. Reframe the problem by identifying the true unit of prediction and the true source of distributional shift.

**Why this matters:** Misidentifying the prediction target leads to feature leakage, meaningless validation splits, and models that memorize IDs instead of learning transferable patterns. IEEE Fraud's winner realized they were predicting fraudulent CLIENTS not transactions—this single insight enabled grouped aggregation features that generalized to unseen clients. BirdCLEF winners realized adversarial validation failed because CLIENT (recording location) shifted, not just time.

**When to use:** When adversarial validation gives suspiciously perfect separation (AUC near 1.0), when categorical IDs have high feature importance, when test performance differs dramatically from training despite similar distributions of raw features, or when the evaluation emphasizes generalization to new entities (clients, locations, users).

**When NOT to use:** When the task is truly IID (independent samples with no latent grouping structure), or when the dataset explicitly provides stable entity IDs as features you're meant to use directly (rare in niche competitions).

**How to reason about it:**
Ask: What is the fundamental unit being judged? Run adversarial validation and check which features drive the split—if entity IDs dominate, you're solving the wrong problem. Check whether 'within-entity' vs 'across-entity' predictions behave differently. For time series: is the shift temporal or is it entity turnover? For audio: is it acoustic conditions or geographic/species diversity? Reformulate your target and validation strategy around the true prediction unit.
