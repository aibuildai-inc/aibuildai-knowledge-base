# The View from 46th Place

Competition: ncaam-march-mania-2021
Rank: #46
Source: https://www.kaggle.com/c/ncaam-march-mania-2021/discussion/231193

It was great to see Kaggle running a set of sports prediction competitions, and a tribute to the successive waves of Fantasy Sports for the fans and Analytics for the teams and media that have brought sports data into the mainstream.

Had this been football (the one called Soccer by  the 19th century British to distinguish it from Rugger), or even better cricket, a more obviously statistics-centric game, I would have been full of ideas for features to use to distinguish good teams from bad. And probably over-confident, since while any fan might make a decent job of ranking teams, predicting accurate probabilities is a different matter entirely. It is a truth universally acknowledged that humankind evolved for something other than precise computation of probabilities. Which, of course is where the machines and the bookmakers come in handy. But this was College basketball, a sport played in a far away (from me) country by teams of whom I knew almost nothing. A strange one too, while I kind of get a play-in game for the 16th seeding, how come a “first four” team is teleported into the 11th seeded position?

Stage 1 was useful for getting familiar with the technology and data, but pretty much useless for assessing models given the diversity of approaches to data leakage and over-fitting. However, given the short turnaround time for Stage 2 submissions, probably a necessary exercise.

My strategy for Stage 2 was to pick a good starting source of initial estimates, and then to find suitable variations on it that would give me the satisfaction of some of my own input and a little bit of social distance in the competition space. The @salma - @moore - @raddar approach, the 2019 Kaggle NCAAM winner, met the criteria of prediction quality and availability. The option of ensembling 110% Salma-Moore-Raddar with minus 10% of the default “everybody is equal” all 0.5 submission gave me an aggressive model suitable for a tournament of few upsets. For my first attempt at NCAAW I went for a 99% & 1% mixture of the same predictors as a cautious second model, but was undone by the rookie mistake of not accompanying this by a more conservative capping. Thus, I found both my submissions stuck on the fringe of the NCAAW medals behind a Suez Canal blockage of 18 apparent public kernel clones of S-M-R before all of our chances were detonated by one upset too many.

For NCAAM, however, I was doing something I’d previously done for fun at elections. That is, go to a UK odds compilation site, as indicated on my [External Data disclosure](https://www.kaggle.com/c/ncaam-march-mania-2021/discussion/222254), to get a better idea of the real probabilities. I am making some kind of “rational betting market” assumption that the people with big skin in the game have better knowledge, and probably better analytics, than me. And yes, I know that the markets can be influenced, or even Game Stopped, by less rational forces. They had odds available for all the settled first round match-ups, so then obtaining probabilities was just a matter of toggling to whichever form of odds presentation I found easiest to interpret, and doing some fairly simple computations. For example, Arkansas were listed as 1-4 to beat Colgate, which leads to an initial naïve probability estimate of 0.8. But we’re not done yet; Colgate were 17-5, naively 0.227. Thus, the naïve probabilities sum to 1.027, which is more than 100% since bookies drive around in expensive cars and ensure that there’s an over-round in the market. So, the naïve probabilities need renormalization to sum to 1.000, here this is simply division by 1.027 to yield 0.779 for Arkansas and 0.221 for Colgate.

As it turned out, my odds-based approach outperformed S-M-R in the first round and thereafter was locked in as my better NCAAM submission (despite tanking in the spread competition). And pretty much simultaneously with my NCAAW models being exploded out of the medals, the NCAAM mixture of S-M-R and odds settled into them, with a near-50-50 assessment of Baylor and Gonzaga ensuring a risk-free final day. The minor upset in the final was sufficient to upgrade my medal to silver, a pleasant surprise since I’d expected to be overtaken by a small posse of final game 1-0 coin-flippers.

Some theoretical thoughts. When I first saw the Shannon Entropy-like scoring formula, my initial thought was conditioned along the lines – “that’s very clever, it will reward the most accurate predictions of probabilities”. Now, I’m not so sure. That first issues to arise were the opposite cases of gambling 1-0 on near certainties and of capping estimates at some sensible level to avoid blow-ups. I now see that @jtrotman’s [10th place from this year](https://www.kaggle.com/c/ncaam-march-mania-2021/discussion/231068), the [Madtown 3rd place prediction from 2019]( https://www.kaggle.com/c/mens-machine-learning-competition-2019/discussion/90254) and @jleecook’s [2019 NCAAW winner]( https://github.com/jleecook/NCAAW) were all heavily discretized. Much to ponder in advance of next year.

With that, thanks to Kaggle and especially to @addisonhoward for four great competitions, and congratulations to all the winners and medalists from these contests.
