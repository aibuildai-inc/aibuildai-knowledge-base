## Principle 5: Validate by Biological Acquisition Unit and Domain

*Consensus: 5/5 competitions; experiment-level, donor-level, cell-type/modality, voxel-size, or source-dataset splits were necessary, although tiny numbers of specimens made absolute CV noisy.*

Keep all patches, slices, time points, and augmented views from one acquisition unit in the same fold. Construct folds that expose the domain shifts expected at test time, and use out-of-fold predictions to tune thresholds and post-processing on the official metric.

**Why this matters:** Random patch or slice splits leak specimen-specific texture and make near-duplicate neighborhoods appear in train and validation. A model can then score well without learning cross-experiment generalization, which is the main difficulty in microscopy data.

**When to use:** Group by tomogram, experiment, kidney/donor, plate/sample, or source dataset. Stratify groups by cell type, object count, modality, or spacing where feasible, and report per-domain errors rather than only a pooled average.

**When NOT to use:** Do not overinterpret a leave-one-donor score when there are only two or three donors or the held-out domain differs fundamentally from hidden test. In that case use CV for bug detection and large directional effects, add stress tests, inspect threshold stability, and reserve leaderboard evidence for only a few high-level choices.

**How to reason about it:**
First identify the independent sampling unit. Next list known shift variables and create the hardest leakage-free folds that remain large enough to score. Evaluate the exact competition decoder and metric. Compare rankings across folds and thresholds; prefer a slightly weaker model whose operating point is stable across domains over a fragile peak.
