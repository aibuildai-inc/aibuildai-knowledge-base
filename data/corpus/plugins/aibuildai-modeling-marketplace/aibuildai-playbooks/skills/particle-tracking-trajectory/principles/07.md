## Principle 7: Use Supervised Learning at Narrow Ambiguity Points

*Consensus: 4/6 used supervised models in a central role: logistic regression for pair/triple pruning, a deep pair classifier, ML candidate scoring, or gradient-boosted track extension. Other high-ranked solutions showed that geometry can remain the main engine.*

Apply ML to bounded decisions such as compatible pair, valid extension, outlier probability, or candidate quality rather than defaulting to an unconstrained end-to-end hit-to-track model. Geometry should define plausible candidates and invariances; learning should resolve residual ambiguity.

**Why this matters:** Track IDs are permutation-invariant and event sizes vary, making direct whole-event prediction difficult. Candidate-level learning supplies many training examples, permits hard-negative mining, and can combine geometric residuals, detector topology, cell direction, and track context.

**When to use:** Use it when deterministic cuts leave overlapping residual distributions and labeled events represent test conditions. Pair models fit local compatibility; extension models fit decisions conditioned on a partial track; candidate rankers fit global plausibility.

**When NOT to use:** Skip ML when a physical rule cleanly separates candidates, inference cost is too high, or labels do not cover detector conditions. Ranks 1, 3, and 9 obtained strong results with little or no central ML, using geometry, density, and topology instead.

**How to reason about it:**
Define the decision boundary that remains after geometric proposal, then sample negatives from that exact proposal process—not from arbitrary hit pairs. Evaluate candidate recall at a fixed accepted degree and calibrate probabilities only if downstream assignment uses them probabilistically.

        )
        y = int(hit.particle_id == dominant_truth_particle(track))
