## Principle 1: Choose Preprocessing Based on Imaging Modality

*Consensus: Most top solutions apply modality-specific preprocessing*

Different medical imaging modalities require fundamentally different preprocessing pipelines. The right preprocessing is determined by the physics of image acquisition and the diagnostic features clinicians use.

**Why this matters:** Inappropriate preprocessing destroys diagnostic information. CT scans store Hounsfield units that must be windowed to visualize different tissue types; retinal images need large inputs so that small lesions stay visible; gigapixel histopathology slides must be tiled because they cannot fit in memory.

**When to use:** Always. Inspect the imaging modality first: DICOM CT/MRI requires windowing; microscopy (histopathology, cellular) requires tile extraction; fundus photography (retinal images) needs large input sizes and can pair both eyes of a patient; skin-lesion crops may need hair removal or hair augmentation.

**When NOT to use:** Never skip this analysis. Even when 'minimal preprocessing works best', that itself is a deliberate modality-specific choice based on evidence, not a default.

**How to reason about it:**
IF imaging modality is CT/MRI DICOM: apply multiple window settings (brain [40,80], subdural [80,200], bone [600,2800] for head CT) to create multi-channel inputs. ELIF modality is gigapixel histopathology: extract tiles (192-256px) at diagnostic magnification, filter background, use tile-based models (MIL/attention). ELIF modality is fundus photography: crop to the retina, keep the input size large, and combine information from both eyes of the same patient. ELIF modality is skin-lesion photography: test hair removal or hair augmentation. Test preprocessing ablations on validation, not LB.

# CT windowing (RSNA hemorrhage 1st place)

    'brain': [40, 80],
    'subdural': [80, 200],
