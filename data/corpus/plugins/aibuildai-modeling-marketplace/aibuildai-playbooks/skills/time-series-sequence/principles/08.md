## Principle 8: Detect and Handle Data Leakage Systematically

*Consensus: Liverpool had a 'drift-free data' leakage. Career-Con had a group_id leakage. Jane Street emphasized purging. Every competition has leakage traps.*

Time series competitions have systematic leakage sources: batch effects (test samples in train batches), metadata leaks (file timestamps, sequence IDs), preprocessing leaks (using test statistics), and feature engineering leaks (future information). Build a checklist to detect each type.

**Why this matters:** Leakage creates a huge train-test distribution shift. Your model learns shortcuts that work in CV but fail on LB. The gap between local CV and LB is the primary diagnostic—large gaps (>0.01 for normalized metrics) almost always indicate leakage or distribution shift. Finding leakage is often worth more than any modeling improvement.

**When to use:** Always run leakage checks before serious modeling. Mandatory checks: (1) can you predict test set membership from features alone? (2) does CV score match LB? (3) do features have different distributions in train/test? (4) can you reconstruct time ordering from data? Extra checks for competitions with groups/batches.

**When NOT to use:** Don't become paranoid—small CV/LB gaps (<0.005) are normal. Not all distribution shift is leakage (test might genuinely be different). Focus on systematic checks, not speculation.

**How to reason about it:**
Leakage checklist: (1) Feature engineering: does ANY feature use information from time >= prediction_time? (2) Target encoding: does it use future data? (3) Normalization: did you use test set statistics? (4) Batches: are train and test samples from same batches? (5) Groups: does CV split respect group boundaries? (6) Metadata: are there hidden signals in row order, IDs, timestamps? For each 'yes', you have leakage. Fix: make your data pipeline strictly causal (only past info).
