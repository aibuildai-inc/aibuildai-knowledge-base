## Principle 8: Learn from Ambiguous Near-Misses, Not Easy Negatives

*Consensus: Hard-example generation or ambiguity-focused augmentation appeared in at least 6/12 ARC and 7/16 substantive Foursquare writeups.*

Train and test hypotheses on examples that competing explanations confuse: symmetry/color perturbations and decomposed subcases for program induction; retrieved same-name, nearby, or embedding-similar nonmatches for entity resolution. Refresh hard examples when the proposal model changes.

**Why this matters:** Random negatives teach only coarse separation and do not define the decision boundary encountered at inference. In few-shot search, nuisance-preserving augmentation can reveal whether a candidate encodes the intended relation or memorizes incidental orientation and colors.

**When to use:** Use hard-negative mining when proposal candidates are substantially more confusable than random pairs. Use equivariant augmentation when the transformation should commute with a known symmetry or symbol permutation.

**When NOT to use:** Do not apply an augmentation unless the target relation is invariant or equivariant to it; a flip, recoloring, or field deletion may alter the rule. Avoid mining only the hardest examples if many are mislabeled or genuinely ambiguous.

**How to reason about it:**
Generate examples through the same mechanism used at inference, inspect whether they preserve labels, and mix hard with representative examples. In a few-shot task, accept a hypothesis only if it remains consistent across all original demonstrations; use augmented demonstrations as additional evidence, not unquestioned truth.
