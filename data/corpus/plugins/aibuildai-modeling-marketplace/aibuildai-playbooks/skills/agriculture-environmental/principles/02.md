## Principle 2: Design Spatial-Temporal CV That Matches Test Distribution, Not Train Convenience

*Consensus: 25/30 solutions used stratified group k-fold by spatial or temporal variables; multiple solutions trained 2+ different CV schemes for ensemble diversity*

Split data by the dimension that will generalize (new locations, future dates) while stratifying by the dimension that should be balanced (target distribution, species representation). Use CV-LB correlation to validate the split, not just CV score alone.

**Why this matters:** A naive random split leaks information from the same site/date into train and validation, producing overoptimistic CV that doesn't predict LB. But an extreme split (all of State A in train, State B in val) creates distribution shift worse than test. The right split depends on whether test is 'new sites, same time' vs. 'same sites, future time' vs. 'completely new spatiotemporal block'.

**When to use:** Always for spatial/temporal data. Indicators: data has location IDs, timestamps, or grouped measurements (same farm visit, same sensor). When you have <500 training examples, stratification becomes critical to ensure each fold sees rare classes/species.

**When NOT to use:** When test is explicitly IID samples from the same population (rare in environmental tasks). When you have 100k+ samples and can afford simple random splits without distribution collapse.

**How to reason about it:**
1. Identify the test scenario: Are test samples from new locations you've never seen? Future dates? Both?
2. Group by that dimension (site, date, or site+date concatenated) so no group appears in both train and val.
3. Stratify by the target or a correlated categorical (e.g., bin the target into quantiles; use species ID; use 'State' as a proxy for climate).
4. For small datasets (<1000), try multiple strategies (GroupKFold by date, StratifiedGroupKFold by state+date, even a 'leak-aware' random split) and ensemble them — diversity helps when no single CV is reliable.
