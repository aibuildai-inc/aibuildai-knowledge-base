## Principle 9: Handle Structural Zeros Explicitly with Successive-Zero Analysis

*Consensus: 2/2 sparse demand forecasting (Walmart #1, Restaurant #1)*

Identify item-store pairs that have all-zero sales in training and predict zero for them in test. For other pairs, detect 'structural zero periods' (long stretches of zeros indicating item not stocked) by analyzing successive zeros on both sides of non-zero sales. Mark dates as zero if surrounded by more than a learned threshold of consecutive zeros.

**Why this matters:** Sparse demand data conflates 'no demand' with 'item not available.' A model trained on all data learns wrong patterns for structural zeros. Filtering all-zero entities and structural periods focuses the model on real demand variation, dramatically improving accuracy for items that do sell. Moving-average-zero periods indicate stock-outs or discontinued items—predicting non-zero there is pure error.

**When to use:** When training data has many zero values (sparse sales, occasional trips) and zeros have different causes (no demand vs. not stocked vs. holiday closures). Use if you see items with all zeros in training or long zero stretches between sales.

**When NOT to use:** Do not use if zeros are real demand signals (always-available item with low demand). Skip if data is dense (most entries non-zero). Avoid over-aggressive zero-marking that removes real low-demand days.

**How to reason about it:**
First pass: exclude any entity (item-store, route) with 100% zeros in training—predict zero for them in test. Second pass: for each entity, compute a 21-day centered moving average; mark dates as structural zero if the moving average is zero (item not stocked during this period). Third pass: for each entity, find the maximum 'min_side_zeros' (the smallest number of consecutive zeros on either side of any sale); use this as a threshold to mark dates with longer successive zeros on both sides as structural zeros. Cap thresholds at reasonable values (e.g., 1-9 days).
