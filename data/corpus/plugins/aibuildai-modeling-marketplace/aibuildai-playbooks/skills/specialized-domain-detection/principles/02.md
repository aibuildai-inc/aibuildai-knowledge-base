## Principle 2: Preserve High Resolution in Early Layers When Signal is Spatial-Frequency

*Consensus: Camera model ID solutions train on native-resolution crops instead of resized images (1st, 3rd, 4th, 5th); scientific image forgery 29th adds a stride-1 noise residual before the first pooling layer*

Remove or reduce the stride in the first convolutional layer and remove early max-pooling layers to keep the model operating at higher spatial resolution longer before downsampling.

**Why this matters:** Specialized signals—sensor noise patterns, JPEG block artifacts, subtle forgery artifacts—often reside in high-frequency spatial details between neighboring pixels. Standard CNN architectures (stride-2 first conv, early pooling) aggressively downsample to 1/4 resolution within the first two layers, irreversibly destroying these patterns. Keeping full or half resolution allows the model to learn fine-grained filters that detect pixel-level correlations the signal depends on.

**When to use:** When your signal is high-frequency or pixel-adjacent: camera model ID (sensor noise is per-pixel; the 4th place kept every crop aligned to the JPEG 8×8 grid), image forgery (copy-paste boundaries, compression artifacts), resampling or recompression detection. Verify by testing: if shuffling pixels only among close neighbors destroys performance, you have a high-frequency signal.

**When NOT to use:** When your task is object-level or semantic (deepfakes at the face-swap level, large-object detection). The deepfake 1st place used standard EfficientNets without stride modification because face-level artifacts (warping, blending) are visible at lower resolutions. Also skip if memory constraints prevent training at higher resolution; a smaller crop at full resolution is usually a better trade than a downsampled large input (the camera model ID 1st place found smaller crops gave almost the same accuracy as its full 512 crops and trained faster).

**How to reason about it:**
Run this test: take a correctly classified sample, shuffle pixels within a small neighborhood, and re-classify. If performance drops sharply, your signal is high-frequency and you need high-res early layers. Compare training curves for stride-1 vs stride-2 first layers on a validation fold; if stride-1 wins by more than the fold-to-fold noise and converges just as fast, adopt it. If a stride-1 smaller backbone beats a vanilla larger one, resolution matters more than capacity for your signal. The passenger screening 8th place saw the same direction at the input level: models trained on original-resolution scans beat those trained on downsampled scans.

```python
# Modify EfficientNet for high-res preservation
import timm

model = timm.create_model('efficientnet_b3', pretrained=True)

# Remove stride in first conv (usually stride=2 → stride=1)
model.conv_stem.stride = (1, 1)

# Remove or replace early pooling if present
# (EfficientNets use depthwise conv, but ResNets have maxpool)
if hasattr(model, 'maxpool'):
    model.maxpool = nn.Identity()  # Remove it

# Now the model stays at full or 1/2 resolution much longer,
# allowing conv filters to learn pixel-adjacent patterns
```
