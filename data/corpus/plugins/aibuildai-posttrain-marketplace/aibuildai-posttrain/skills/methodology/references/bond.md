# BOND

**Item**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14622

Instead of sampling N completions at inference time and picking the best, train the policy so a single sample already looks like the best-of-N one: fine-tune it to match the analytical Best-of-N distribution via a mix of forward and backward KL.

**BOND** (Best-of-N Distillation) is an online RLHF method that fine-tunes an LLM policy to imitate the output distribution of Best-of-N sampling without paying Best-of-N's N-times inference cost, introduced by Sessa et al. at Google DeepMind as "a novel RLHF algorithm that seeks to emulate Best-of-N but without its significant computational overhead at inference time" [1]. Its parent is Best-of-N sampling itself, an inference-time strategy that draws N generations from a reference policy and returns the one with the highest reward-model score, introduced by Stiennon et al. for summarization [2]; BOND does not change inference, it trains the policy toward the distribution that Best-of-N sampling induces. Mechanically, the paper first derives a closed-form expression for the Best-of-N distribution $\pi_{BoN}$ in terms of the reference policy and reward-quantile terms, then poses BOND as $\arg\min_\pi D(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{BoN})$ for a divergence $D$, estimating that divergence from Monte-Carlo samples [1]. The paper gives two motivations beyond cost: Best-of-N gets strong reward/KL trade-offs but scales inference linearly in N, and the paper's own connection to standard KL-regularized RLHF (Eq. 7-8) shows Best-of-N's implicit reward rewards rank rather than raw score, which the authors conjecture is more robust to reward hacking than the standard reward-plus-KL objective [1].

The paper's concrete algorithm, J-BOND, was used to fine-tune the open-weight Gemma 1.1 2B and 7B, RecurrentGemma 2B and 9B, and CodeGemma 1.1 models, and the resulting Gemma 1.1 IT 7B outperformed Mistral 7B v0.2 Instruct on safety and instruction following per the Gemma Technical Report's Table 5, as cited by the BOND paper itself [1]. In-house, J-BOND on Gemma 7B produced a better reward/KL trade-off than REINFORCE swept over four KL-regularization strengths (Fig. 7) [1]. Lineage in one line: Best-of-N sampling (Stiennon et al., 2020 [2]) -> BOND / J-BOND (Sessa et al., DeepMind, 2024 [1]), complementary to the same group's WARM and WARP policy/reward-averaging work [3][1]. The paper was confirmed as the correct match by title: the arXiv abstract page's exact title, "BOND: Aligning LLMs with Best-of-N Distillation," matches the shortlist row verbatim, and only one candidate was returned for that title [1].

**When to pick it**: choose BOND/J-BOND when you have a reward model and want a policy that reproduces Best-of-N-quality single samples without N-times inference cost, and you can afford generating from two policies (trained policy plus a moving anchor) every step [1]. Prefer plain Best-of-N sampling itself, its parent, when inference-time cost is not a concern and no training run is wanted [2]. Prefer DPO, the nearest offline alternative, when you only have static preference pairs and cannot sample fresh generations from the model each step; DPO trains directly on pairs of completions to the same prompt with no reward model or RL loop [4]. Prefer REINFORCE-style online RLHF with a leave-one-out baseline (used as BOND's own comparison baseline in the paper, run with 2 samples per prompt) as the nearest online neighbor when a reward-maximizing policy is wanted directly rather than a policy that imitates a target sampling distribution; the paper reports REINFORCE's reward/KL trade-off is sensitive to its KL-regularization strength $\beta_{RL}$ in a way J-BOND is not [1][5].

**Variant of**: distills Best-of-N sampling [2] into policy weights; not a direct variant of PPO or REINFORCE, though its backward-KL term reduces to a REINFORCE-style policy gradient (Eq. 15) [1].

**Data it needs**: a prompt set only (no preference pairs required directly) plus a reward model that scores full generations; the paper's Gemma experiments used a set of conversational prompts, a previously SFT'd reference policy, and a separately trained reward model, at batch size 128 [1]. Fully on-policy: every step generates one fresh sample from the current policy and (for J-BOND) two fresh samples from a separately tracked anchor policy; no replay buffer or offline dataset of generations is used [1].

**Extra models**: a reward model (assumed given, not trained by BOND itself) [1]; a frozen-at-each-step anchor policy, initialized as a copy of the reference policy and then updated online — either every M steps (vanilla iterative BOND) or via an exponential moving average with mixing rate $\eta$ (J-BOND, Eq. 18) [1]; no value network. The reference policy $\pi_{ref}$ also appears directly in J-BOND's extra KL regularization term (Eq. 19) [1]. See Cost for what holding the anchor costs in practice.

**Shipped by**: no open-source RL library implements BOND. trl's `docs/source/_toctree.yml` toctree has no BOND entry anywhere in it; its supported "Trainers" heading lists only DPO, GRPO, KTO, Reward, and RLOO (SFT is also there but is not an RL trainer), while PPO, Nash-MD, XPO, GKD, and several others sit under a separate "Experimental" heading the file itself marks `isExpanded: false` [6]. verl's algorithm-doc index likewise has no BOND page (its latest index lists PPO, GRPO, DAPO, GPG, SPPO, SPIN and others but no BOND) [7]. Building it would mean a new sampling loop, not a loss swap on an existing trainer: each step needs generation from two separately weighted policies (trained policy and anchor) plus reward scoring of all samples, a combined SFT-style forward-KL loss and REINFORCE-style backward-KL loss weighted by the Jeffreys mixing parameter $\beta$, an extra KL-regularization term against the anchor, and an EMA weight update on the anchor after every optimizer step [1].

## How it works

Loop, one line: sample 1 completion from the policy and 2 from the anchor; distill the anchor's Best-of-2 sample into the policy via a forward+backward-KL (Jeffreys) loss; nudge the anchor toward the policy by EMA [1].

**The Best-of-N distribution** (Theorem 1) [1]:

$$ \pi_{BoN}(y) = \underbrace{\pi_{ref}(y) \times p_{\le}(y)^{N-1}}_{(A)} \times \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\frac{p_<(y)}{p_\le(y)}\right)^{i-1}}_{(B)} $$

where $p_<(y) = \mathbb{P}_{y' \sim \pi_{ref}}[r(y') < r(y)]$ and $p_\le(y) = \mathbb{P}_{y' \sim \pi_{ref}}[r(y') \le r(y)]$ are the reward quantiles of $y$ under the reference policy; term (A) exponentially penalizes generations with a low quantile as $N$ grows, term (B) is a collision-correction factor bounded in $[1, N]$ [1]. The paper estimates $p_\le(y)$ by Monte-Carlo: sample $k$ generations from $\pi_{ref}$ for the same prompt and take the empirical fraction with reward $\le r(y)$ (Eq. 10); the XSum experiments used $k=16$ during training and $k=32$ during evaluation [1].

**The BOND objective** minimizes a divergence to that distribution, $\pi_{BOND} = \arg\min_{\pi} D(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{BoN})$ (Eq. 6), where the paper's chosen divergence is the Jeffreys divergence, a $\beta$-weighted mix of forward and backward KL (Eq. 11) [1]:

$$ J^\beta_{Jeffreys}(p \,\|\, q) := (1-\beta) \cdot KL(q \,\|\, p) + \beta \cdot KL(p \,\|\, q) $$

Minimizing the forward KL, $KL(\pi_{BoN} \,\|\, \pi)$, reduces to supervised fine-tuning on samples drawn from $\pi_{BoN}$ (Eq. 13) [1]. Minimizing the backward KL, $KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{BoN})$, reduces to a REINFORCE-style policy-gradient update (Eq. 15) with an implicit KL-regularization strength $\beta_{BOND} = \frac{1}{N-1}$ against $\pi_{ref}$; the gradient's derivation carries the reward $r_{BOND}(y) = \log p_\le(y)$ (Eq. 8), but the paper substitutes the true quantile with its Monte-Carlo estimate and, having observed the correction factor (B) does not play a significant role, states it uses $r_{BOND}(y) = \hat p_\le(y)$ in practice, without the log [1]. (A separate, noise-based rationale for dropping information from this reward appears later, for J-BOND's own $r_{J\text{-}BOND}$ substitution — see below — but is not the reason the paper gives for this Eq. 15 substitution.) The XSum ablation ($N=8$) found $\beta=0.5$ (the Jeffreys mix) minimizes both forward and backward KL from $\pi_{BoN}$ better than either pure forward ($\beta=0$) or pure backward ($\beta=1$) KL alone, and matches the mode-seeking $\beta=1$ setting's reward-quantile gains while $\beta=0$ lags behind [1].

**Iterative BOND** replaces a single large $N$ with repeated small-$N$ distillation against a moving anchor $\pi^{anchor}_t$, since Best-of-N sampling applied twice equals Best-of-$N^2$ sampling once (Eq. 16); at each round it minimizes $D(\pi \,\|\, \text{Best-of-}n(\pi^{anchor}_t))$ for small $n$ (e.g. $n=2$) and then sets $\pi^{anchor}_{t+1} = \pi_{t+1}$ (Algorithm 1) [1]. On the XSum setup, iterative BOND with $n \in \{2,4\}$ kept improving reward and reward-quantile while non-iterative BOND with fixed $N \in \{4,8,16\}$ saturated, the smaller $N$ saturating sooner [1].

**J-BOND**, the paper's practical instantiation, fixes $n=2$ and uses an EMA anchor update $\theta^{anchor}_{t+1} \leftarrow (1-\eta)\theta^{anchor}_t + \eta\theta_{t+1}$ (Eq. 18) instead of periodic anchor resets, plus an extra KL-regularization term $\gamma \cdot KL(\pi_t \,\|\, \pi^{anchor}_t)$ added to the Jeffreys loss (Eq. 19) [1]. Because only 2 anchor samples are available per step, the backward-KL reward is replaced by a coarser signal (Eq. 17) [1]:

$$ r_{J\text{-}BOND}(y) = \begin{cases} -\log(16) & \text{if } r(y) < \min\{r(y_1'), r(y_2')\} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

Worked example: policy sample $y$ scores $r(y)=0.3$, and the two anchor samples score $r(y_1')=0.5$, $r(y_2')=0.7$; since $0.3$ is below both, $r_{J\text{-}BOND}(y) = -\log(16) \approx -2.773$. If instead $r(y)=0.6$, it beats one anchor sample, so $r_{J\text{-}BOND}(y) = 0$ — the reward only fires when the policy sample loses to both anchor draws, mirroring the concavity of the ideal $\log p_\le(\cdot)$ reward [1]. The paper derives the constant $-\log(16)$ so that, in expectation over the two anchor samples, this reward matches $\log p_\le(y)$ exactly when $p_\le(y)=0.5$, i.e. for a median-quality sample [1]. J-BOND's overall gradient sums a forward-KL (SFT-on-Best-of-2) term, this backward-KL policy-gradient term, and the anchor KL-regularization term, weighted by $(1-\beta)$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ respectively (Algorithm 2) [1]; the Gemma experiments fixed $\beta=0.5$ [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: every step generates 1 policy sample plus, for J-BOND, 2 anchor samples per prompt — 3x the generations of a plain single-sample policy-gradient step; the general (non-iterative) BOND algorithm needs many more anchor samples for quantile estimation (16 during training, 32 during eval in the paper's XSum runs), so J-BOND's drop to 2 anchor samples is explicitly a cost-driven simplification [1]. No value network means no extra critic forward/backward pass, unlike PPO [1].
- Memory: two policy-sized parameter sets must be held for generation at once — the trained policy and the anchor — plus the frozen $\pi_{ref}$ that the extra KL-regularization term (Eq. 19) references; only the trained policy carries gradients and optimizer state, the anchor and $\pi_{ref}$ are inference-only copies [1].
- A naive reading might assume larger $N$ is free to increase for better Best-of-N approximation; the paper's own Section 4.3 flags the opposite: since $\pi_{BoN}(y) \propto p_\le(y)^{N-1}$, larger $N$ amplifies any error in the estimated quantile $\hat p_\le(y)$, and forward-KL estimation requires actually sampling from $\pi_{BoN}$, which becomes prohibitive as $N$ grows — motivating the small-$n$ iterative scheme rather than a large one-shot $N$ [1].

**In practice, per framework:** no framework implements BOND [6][7], so no framework-level memory/throughput numbers exist to report; a from-scratch implementation would inherit standard online-RLHF costs (reward-model scoring per sample, KV-cache memory for two generating models) that the paper itself does not quantify.

## How to use it

- Prompts: an unlabeled prompt set matching the target task distribution; the Gemma runs used a set of conversational prompts with a previously SFT'd reference policy [1].
- Reward: one scalar per generation from an externally trained reward model; the Gemma experiments used a large, previously trained reward model, and the XSum ablations used a T5 NLI reward model [1].
- Key knobs, with the paper's own values as the only available anchor (no framework ships defaults):

| knob | paper's value (XSum ablations / Gemma runs) [1] |
| --- | --- |
| Best-of-N size $N$ (non-iterative BOND) | 4, 8, 16 tested; $N=8$ used for the main Jeffreys ablation |
| iterative step size $n$ | 2 (used by J-BOND) or 4 |
| Jeffreys mixing weight $\beta$ | 0.5 for J-BOND; 0, 0.5, 1 swept in the XSum ablation |
| anchor EMA rate $\eta$ | 0.02 for the Gemma 7B EMA-vs-periodic and REINFORCE comparisons; 0.01/0.05/0.1 swept on Gemma 2B |
| extra KL-regularization $\gamma$ | 0, 0.5, 1, 2 swept on Gemma 2B (larger $\gamma$ slows drift from $\pi_{ref}$ and improved the reward/KL trade-off) |
| MC samples for quantile estimation | 16 during training, 32 during eval (non-iterative BOND) |
| learning rate / batch size | 3e-6, batch size 128, 100 warm-up steps (Adam), Gemma 2B/7B runs |

- Trade-off, $N$ vs. iteration: a large one-shot $N$ gets closer to strong Best-of-N behavior in principle but costs more MC samples and grows more sensitive to quantile-estimation error; J-BOND's small $n=2$ with a moving EMA anchor was found to reach the same reward/KL trade-off as non-iterative BOND at larger $N$ while continuing to improve rather than saturating (Fig. 4) [1].
- Trade-off, EMA rate vs. regularization: a faster-moving anchor ($\eta$ larger) increases reward faster but, per the paper's Fig. 6, also increases KL drift from $\pi_{ref}$ faster; the extra regularization term $\gamma$ trades some of that reward-increase speed for a better reward/KL trade-off [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes, all first-hand from the paper's own figures: average reward of the training policy should rise steadily; KL from $\pi_{ref}$ should rise in a stable, roughly linear fashion for J-BOND with the EMA anchor rather than spiking, and using an EMA anchor gave a markedly lower KL increase than a periodically-reset anchor for the same reward gain on Gemma 7B (Fig. 5) [1]; the average reward log-quantile (how far above the reference-policy's own reward distribution the training policy's samples sit) should increase and, with the Jeffreys $\beta=0.5$ objective, tracked the mode-seeking $\beta=1$ objective's quantile gains while the mode-covering $\beta=0$ objective lagged (Fig. 2, right panel) [1].
- Published reference runs: Fig. 7 compares J-BOND ($\eta=0.02$) against REINFORCE with a leave-one-out baseline swept over $\beta_{RL} \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1\}$ on Gemma 7B, reporting reward, KL-from-reference, and the resulting reward/KL trade-off curve as the comparison point [1]; Fig. 4 gives iterative-vs-non-iterative BOND reward and quantile curves on XSum as a second reference [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: setting $N$ (or effectively letting the anchor move too fast) too large causes reward over-optimization, the same failure mode as in standard RLHF, per the paper's own Section 4.3 discussion [1]; a purely forward-KL objective ($\beta=0$) mode-covers and under-optimizes reward quantiles relative to including backward KL, while a purely backward-KL objective ($\beta=1$) risks the policy- and entropy-collapse the paper attributes to mode-seeking divergences in general [1]. No framework ships a default configuration to diverge from, since none implements BOND [6][7].
- Named successors: none found. Searched the Semantic Scholar Graph API's citations endpoint for arXiv:2407.14622, reading the first 50 entries it returned (fetched 2026-08-09); none of the returned titles present themselves as a fix to a documented BOND bias in the way DAPO and Dr. GRPO explicitly fix GRPO's length bias, so this card reports no confirmed successor rather than inferring one [8]. The paper's own related-work section instead names concurrent, not successor, work: Amini et al.'s Variational Best-of-N alignment, which the paper says applies an "identical" formalization but with only backward KL and no iterative anchor [1].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own Section 4.3, choosing $N$ too large (a) causes reward over-optimization, (b) makes the $\pi_{BoN}$ estimate more sensitive to quantile-estimation error because $\pi_{BoN}(y) \propto p_\le(y)^{N-1}$, and (c) makes forward-KL estimation (which requires sampling from $\pi_{BoN}$ itself) prohibitively expensive [1]. No maintainer-issue failure modes exist to report since no library ships a BOND trainer to file issues against [6][7].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own framing is that BOND lets a single sample at inference time reach the reward/KL trade-off that Best-of-N sampling gets only by drawing and scoring N samples [1]; it is a distillation of the reference-and-reward-model's own Best-of-N distribution, not a source of capability beyond what Best-of-N sampling from the reference policy could already reach.
