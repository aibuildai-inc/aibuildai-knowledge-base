# ReST-MCTS*

Home: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03816 [1]

Instead of filtering self-generated chains-of-thought by final-answer correctness alone, run a process-reward-guided Monte Carlo Tree Search to collect per-step-verified reasoning traces, then jointly retrain the policy and the process reward model on that data each iteration.

**ReST-MCTS\*** (the paper's own description: "a reinforced self-training approach... based on integrating process reward guidance with tree search MCTS\* for collecting higher-quality reasoning traces as well as per-step value to train policy and reward models") is a self-training method for LLM reasoning introduced by Zhang, Zhoubian, Hu, Yue, Dong, and Tang at Tsinghua and Caltech [1]. Its parent is ReST$^{EM}$, introduced by Singh et al. as "a simple self-training method based on expectation-maximization" that generates samples, filters them by binary ground-truth feedback, and fine-tunes on the filtered set, repeating a few times [2]; ReST$^{EM}$ itself is a math/code-focused simplification of the general Reinforced Self-Training (ReST) framework of Gulcehre et al., which grows a dataset by sampling from the policy and improves the policy on it with offline RL, and which the ReST-MCTS\* paper cites directly [3]. ReST-MCTS\* keeps the outer generate-filter-retrain loop but replaces plain CoT sampling with a modified MCTS variant, MCTS\*, guided by a trained process reward model (PRM); the search tree also lets the paper infer a per-step reward without any human step-level annotation, by estimating how likely a partial solution is to reach the correct final answer [1]. The paper gives two motivating problems with prior self-training: (1) a reasoning trace that reaches the correct final answer can still contain wrong or useless intermediate steps, so outcome-only filtering keeps false positives in the training set; (2) training a reliable step-level verifier normally needs dense human per-step annotation, which does not scale [1].

No landmark production system was found citing ReST-MCTS\* as an adopted training method; a Semantic Scholar citations query for arXiv:2406.03816 (fetched 2026-08-09, first 50 results by default ordering) returned only smaller, more recent research papers building on it, none flagged as an influential citation and none a named production model [4]. In the paper's own results, two iterations of ReST-MCTS\* self-training raised LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct's average zero-shot accuracy across MATH, GPQA-Diamond, and CEval-Hard from 24.78% (0th iteration) to 29.02%, against 26.83% for ReST$^{EM}$ and 27.73% for Self-Rewarding trained on the same backbone and iteration budget (Table 2) [1]. Lineage in one line: STaR (2022, cited in the paper's own Table 1 as CoT+Reflexion with outcome reward from ground truth, no trained reward model [1]) -> ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023 [3]) -> ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2023 [2]) -> ReST-MCTS\* (Zhang et al., 2024 [1]). The paper's name search returned the original Gulcehre et al. ReST paper (arXiv:2308.08998, "Reinforced Self-Training (ReST) for Language Modeling") as a separate, similarly-cited candidate for the name "Reinforced Self-Training" [3]; this card covers ReST-MCTS\*, the paper the corpus's citation-count check settled on, and cites the original ReST and ReST$^{EM}$ papers directly wherever the card describes them.

**When to pick it**: pick when the task has a checkable final answer (math/science problems with a ground-truth answer) and the goal is a higher-quality self-training set than plain outcome filtering, at the cost of running a tree search and training a second model online at every iteration [1]. Prefer the parent, ReST$^{EM}$ [2], when simplicity matters more than trace quality: it needs only CoT sampling and ground-truth outcome filtering, no PRM, no tree search. Prefer a human-labeled process reward model, as in Lightman et al.'s "Let's Verify Step by Step" [5], when you have budget for step-level human annotation and want to avoid the noise of an inferred reward; that paper's PRM is trained on per-step human labels rather than reward inferred from search rollouts, and Table 1 of the ReST-MCTS\* paper places it as a Best-of-N method with human-annotated process reward [1]. The nearest neighbor that also infers per-step process rewards without human labels is Math-Shepherd, which estimates a step's reward from random rollout completion rather than from tree search (Table 1) [1][6].

**Variant of**: ReST$^{EM}$ [2], which is itself a simplified, math/code-focused version of the general ReST framework [3].

**Data it needs**: a set of problems with checkable final answers, at column level: a question, plus a ground-truth or oracle-verifiable correct answer used to prune and score generated traces [1]. For its own initial PRM training set $D_{V_0}$, the paper uses 11,554 science and math questions, each paired with a correct step-by-step solution, drawn from the SciInstruct dataset [1]. The main self-training experiments sample new traces for math questions drawn from the training sets of MATH, GSM8K, and TheoremQA, reused unchanged across both self-training iterations [1]. The method is on-policy in the sense that every iteration's training data comes from the search tree generated by the current policy and value model, not a fixed pre-collected set [1].

**Extra models**: two trained models, not one. The paper's own definition requires a policy model $\pi$ and a process reward (value) model $V_\theta$, trained simultaneously each iteration; there is no separate frozen reference model or KL term in the objective [1]. The value model is a full second network - the paper initializes it from Mistral-7B or ChatGLM3-6B, comparable in size to the policy backbones used (LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B: MetaMATH, SciGLM-6B) [1]. See Cost for what this doubles.

**Shipped by**: no training-library trainer implements ReST-MCTS\* under this name. The authors' own reference implementation, THUDM/ReST-MCTS on GitHub, is a research codebase of standalone scripts (`MCTS/`, `PRM/`, `self_train/`, `eval_vm.py`) with no `setup.py` or `pyproject.toml`, so it is not pip-installable as a package [7]. Neither trl's documentation table of contents nor verl's README mention ReST or ReST-MCTS\* (both fetched 2026-08-09) [8][9]. Building it on top of an existing trainer would mean writing a new sampling loop, not adding a loss: an MCTS\* rollout/expansion procedure over partial solutions, a PRM training loop separate from the policy's SFT loop, and glue code to convert the pruned search tree into (question, partial solution, value) triples for the PRM and (question, full trace) pairs for the policy - a materially bigger job than adding a loss function to an existing on-policy RL trainer.

## How it works

The loop in one line: initialize a policy $\pi$ and a value model $V_\theta$; run MCTS\* over the current policy and value model to build a search tree per question; use the tree to derive both step-level value labels and full correct traces; retrain $V_\theta$ on the value labels and $\pi$ on the traces; repeat [1].

**Value of a partial solution.** For a partial solution $p_k = [s_1, \dots, s_k]$, the paper defines a quality value $v_k$ that must stay bounded, reflect the probability the partial solution leads to a correct final answer, and increase when a step is correct and reduces the remaining distance to the answer [1]. It is built from two intermediate quantities defined in the paper: the reasoning distance $m_k$, the minimum number of further steps a policy needs to reach the correct answer from $p_k$ (estimated by rollout, not computed exactly), and a per-step PRM output $r_{s_k}$ (a sigmoid score) [1]. The weighted reward of step $s_k$ is

$$ w_{s_k} = \frac{1-v_{k-1}}{m_k+1}(1-2r_{s_k}), \quad k=1,2,\dots $$

and the value updates as

$$ v_k = \max(v_{k-1} + w_{s_k},\ 0) $$

with $v_0 = 0$ [1]. Worked example from the paper's own appendix, for a trace of $K$ steps that is entirely correct ($r_{s_k}=0$ at every step): $w_1 = \frac{1-0}{K-1+1}(1-2\times 0) = \frac{1}{K}$, so $v_1 = \max(0+\frac{1}{K}, 0) = \frac{1}{K}$, $v_2 = \frac{2}{K}$, and by induction $v_k = \frac{k}{K}$ for every $k$ - the value rises linearly to 1 as the correct trace completes [1].

**MCTS\* search (Algorithm 2).** At each of $T$ iterations, starting from the tree root, the algorithm descends by selecting the child $C$ of the current node that maximizes

$$ UCB(C) = v_C + \epsilon \sqrt{\frac{\ln n_{parent}}{n_C}} $$

where $v_C$ is $C$'s current quality value and $n_C$, $n_{parent}$ are the visit counts of $C$ and its parent, until it reaches a leaf; if the leaf's value $v_C$ already exceeds a threshold $l$, the search returns that partial solution as the output [1]. Otherwise a self-critic step asks the policy whether to keep expanding; if so, $b$ new child steps are sampled and each is scored by the value model $V_\theta$ [1]. In the paper's own benchmark setup, the ending threshold is 0.9, the rollout step limit is 2, $\alpha = 0.5$, $T = 50$, and $b = 3$ by default [1].

**Reward inference for training the PRM, no human step labels.** After a search tree is built, the paper prunes unfinished branches, computes each node's reasoning distance $m_k$ on the pruned tree, and derives its per-step reward using the "hard estimation" of Wang et al.'s Math-Shepherd (their Eq. 13), $r_{s_k} = 1 - r_{s_k}^{HE}$, i.e. a step is treated as correct if it can still reach a correct answer in the pruned tree [1][6]. These $(Q, p, v)$ triples become the next iteration's PRM training set $D_{V_i}$ [1]. Separately, the paper prunes and verifies full traces by final-answer matching (string match or LLM judge) to form the next iteration's policy training set [1].

**Loss aggregation and objective**: the paper does not give a single closed-form RL objective for the policy update; the policy model is fine-tuned (standard supervised fine-tuning, per the self-training pipeline description) on the verified correct traces collected each iteration, while the value model is fine-tuned as a step-wise regressor toward the $v_k$ targets above [1]. This differs structurally from PPO/GRPO-style methods, which optimize a clipped importance-ratio objective on freshly sampled rollouts; ReST-MCTS\* instead alternates full model retraining rounds on curated, search-verified data, in the ReST/ReST$^{EM}$ "grow-then-improve" tradition [1][2][3].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: each self-training iteration requires (a) an MCTS\* search of up to $T$ iterations and branching factor $b$ per question - the paper's default $T=50$, $b=3$ means up to 50 expansion rounds of 3 new LLM-generated steps each, i.e. up to 150 additional policy generations and 150 value-model scoring calls per question, before any training happens [1]; (b) two full fine-tuning passes per iteration, one for the policy on verified traces and one for the value model on inferred $(Q,p,v)$ triples [1]. Unlike PPO's value network, which shares most of its forward pass structure with the policy and is discarded after training, the value model here is trained as an independent full model and is needed at inference time as well, to guide every subsequent search [1] - so both the generation-time cost (repeated search) and the training-time cost (two independently trained models) are additive, not shared.

**In practice, per framework**: no framework-level cost data is available because no trl, verl, or OpenRLHF release implements ReST-MCTS\* (checked 2026-08-09; none of the three documents or READMEs mention it) [8][9][10]. The only cost data available is from the authors' own repository and paper: the value model backbone matches the policy's likely scale (Mistral-7B or ChatGLM3-6B alongside 6-8B policy backbones), so running both models concurrently during search roughly doubles the resident model memory versus policy-only self-training, before counting optimizer state for whichever model is being fine-tuned in a given iteration [1][7].

## How to use it

- Data prep: assemble a labeled set of questions with checkable final answers; the paper's PRM warm-start set $D_{V_0}$ pairs 11,554 questions with a full correct step-by-step solution each, drawn from SciInstruct [1]. The policy/value self-training set reuses the same math questions (from MATH, GSM8K, TheoremQA training splits) across iterations rather than sourcing fresh questions per iteration [1].
- Reward/label convention: correctness is checked by string matching or LLM judging against the ground-truth final answer to verify whole traces for policy training, and by the hard-estimation rule ($r_{s_k}=1-r_{s_k}^{HE}$) borrowed from Math-Shepherd to build per-step value labels for PRM training [1][6].
- Knobs table (paper's own values are the only ones available; no framework ships this method, so there is no framework-default column to compare against):

| knob | paper value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| search iterations $T$ | 50 (benchmark default) |
| branching factor $b$ | 3 |
| rollout step limit $m$ | 2 |
| $\alpha$ | 0.5 |
| leaf-return threshold $l$ | 0.9 |
| UCB exploration $\epsilon$ | not stated |
| self-training iterations | 2 (main experiments) |
| PRM warm-start set size | 11,554 questions |

- Trade-off a run designer faces: raising $T$ and $b$ finds better traces per question at the cost of more generation and scoring calls per question (see Cost); the paper does not report a sweep of $T$ or $b$ against final self-training accuracy, only against search-time accuracy on MATH and SciBench at fixed sampling budgets (Figure 2) [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper reports overall self-training accuracy per iteration (Table 2) and search accuracy at fixed token budget (Figure 2), rather than describing live training-time monitoring signals (loss curves, reward statistics) the way an RL-framework doc would; no first-hand guidance on live monitoring metrics was found in the paper or the GitHub repository (checked 2026-08-09) [1][7].
- Published reference run: Table 2 in the paper is the reference curve - on LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, average accuracy across MATH, GPQA-Diamond, and CEval-Hard rises from 24.78% (0-shot) to 27.55% (iteration 1) to 29.02% (iteration 2) under ReST-MCTS\*, versus 26.83% (iteration 2, ReST$^{EM}$) and 27.73% (iteration 2, Self-Rewarding) on the same backbone [1]. On Mistral-7B: MetaMATH, ReST-MCTS\* at iteration 2 reaches 26.50% average versus 24.16% (ReST$^{EM}$) and 25.22% (Self-Rewarding) [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the value update $v_k = \max(v_{k-1}+w_{s_k}, 0)$ is explicitly clamped at 0 by the paper to keep $v_k$ bounded, which the paper proves keeps $v_k \le 1$ [1] - an unclamped or mis-implemented update could exceed the intended [0,1] range. No degeneracy specific to group size or reward variance applies here, since the method does not use a group-relative baseline; the risk flagged by the paper instead is training-data quality (false positives passing the final-answer filter), which is exactly what the tree search's per-step reward inference is designed against [1].
- Named successors: none found citing ReST-MCTS\* as a method it explicitly fixes; the Semantic Scholar citation check (arXiv:2406.03816, fetched 2026-08-09, first 50 citing papers by default ordering) surfaced only newer, unrelated research using it as a baseline or comparison point, not a paper naming itself as its direct successor [4].
- Known failure modes, from the paper's own limitations section (Appendix H): it relies on ground-truth oracle labels to filter responses, so as-is it does not generalize to reasoning tasks without labels (e.g. coding, agent, or conversation tasks), and to complex multi-step tasks with no ground-truth answer (the paper's own example is implementing a full software system as in SWE-Agent) it would need a different reward-collection method entirely, from limited human labeling plus symbolic execution or a solver [1]; separately, the trained value model's backbone (Mistral-7B: MetaMATH) and its initial PRM training data (generated by SciGLM, which the paper says focuses on mathematical and scientific reasoning and still lacks generality) both limit generalization to non-math/science domains such as code generation [1]. Separately, in Appendix I (Broader Impact, not Limitations), the paper notes as a disadvantage that a single reward model cannot be scaled to multiple domains, and suggests training multiple reward models together across reasoning domains as a fix [1]. No maintainer replies about numerical or implementation traps were found, since the GitHub repository's issue tracker was not queried in this check.
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own comparison shows ReST-MCTS\* produces higher-quality self-training data than outcome-only filtering (ReST$^{EM}$) or LLM-judged filtering (Self-Rewarding), yielding higher accuracy after the same number of self-training iterations on the same backbones (Table 2) [1]. The gain is specifically in trace/data quality for self-training, not in the search algorithm being a new way to solve problems the base policy cannot reach at all - the same paper reports that, given the same search budget, the search accuracy of ReST-MCTS\* exceeds Self-Consistency and Best-of-N (Figure 2), which is a claim about search-time accuracy, not about capability introduced by fine-tuning [1].
