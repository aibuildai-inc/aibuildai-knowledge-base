# KTO

https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01306

DPO's implicit reward, without pairs: label each generation "desirable" or "undesirable" on its own, and push the policy's implicit reward for it above or below a KL-based reference point.

**KTO** (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) is an offline method for aligning an LLM policy to a per-example binary desirability signal, introduced by Ethayarajh et al. as a human-aware loss (HALO) that directly maximizes the utility of generations instead of maximizing the log-likelihood of preferences, as current methods do [1]. The paper frames DPO, and RLHF more broadly, as HALOs too: loss functions that implicitly model the same cognitive biases prospect theory ascribes to humans, such as loss aversion [1]. KTO's parent in that framing is DPO [2]: both start from the same closed-form optimal policy for the KL-constrained RLHF objective and the same reward reparameterization $r^*(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$ derived by Rafailov et al. [2]; DPO plugs that reparameterized reward into the Bradley-Terry preference model, while KTO plugs it into a Kahneman-Tversky model of human utility applied to single labeled outputs, replacing the paired comparison with a per-output desirable/undesirable judgment against a reference point set by the batch [1]. Two reasons the paper gives for existing beyond removing pairs: binary desirable/undesirable feedback is far more abundant and cheaper to collect than preference pairs in real deployments [1], and the paper's theoretical analysis argues that when feedback is noisy or preferences are intransitive (common in datasets gathered from many annotators), KTO's implicit down-weighting of examples with already-extreme rewards gives better worst-case robustness than DPO's likelihood objective, at the cost of a risk of underfitting when preference data is already clean [1].

The method was released together with an official training recipe, the ContextualAI/HALOs codebase, which the maintainers state has been tested at scales from 1B to 30B LLMs and was used to train an earlier version of the Archangel suite of aligned models published on the Hugging Face Hub [3]. In the paper's own head-to-head, substituting KTO for DPO on Zephyr-β's UltraFeedback alignment stage - changing nothing else - improved GSM8K 8-shot by 13.5 points and improved MMLU, HumanEval, and BigBench-Hard on average, per Table 2's generative-benchmark comparison [1]. In a separate winrate-vs-SFT-target experiment on Mistral-7B aligned on OpenAssistant data, KTO using every output per prompt scored 0.652 against DPO's 0.600 and the unaligned base model's 0.525; KTO using only one output per prompt (a 72% cut in training data) still scored 0.631, ahead of both DPO and Mistral-7B-Instruct's own 0.621 [1]. Lineage in one line: RLHF/DPO's KL-constrained reward reparameterization [2] -> KTO (2024) as an unpaired, prospect-theoretic HALO [1] -> shipped in trl [4] and the paper authors' own Archangel suite [3]; the trl implementation also exposes an unpaired variant of a related anchored-preference loss, which trl labels `apo_zero_unpaired`, as an alternate loss type [4], crediting the Anchored Preference Optimization paper [5]. The defining paper was confirmed via its own arXiv abstract page (arXiv:2402.01306, title "KTO: Model Alignment as Prospect Theoretic Optimization") [1], and the DPO parent via its own abstract page (arXiv:2305.18290) [2].

**When to pick it**: pick KTO when feedback arrives as a per-output binary label (thumbs-up/down, pass/fail) rather than as pairs, or when the desirable/undesirable classes are imbalanced - the paper calls this the natural choice for binary feedback [1]. If you already have clean, low-noise preference pairs, the paper's own theoretical analysis favors DPO [2], KTO's direct parent, since KTO risks underfitting on data that is already easy to model as consistent pairs [1]. Nearest offline alternative for unpaired data inside the same trl trainer is `apo_zero_unpaired`, which increases the likelihood of desirable completions and decreases it for undesirable ones without estimating a KL term at all, per trl's own loss-type description [4]. Nearest online neighbor is PPO [6], which the paper also analyzes as a HALO [1] but which needs on-policy sampling plus a learned reward model and (in the standard recipe) a value network, against KTO's single offline pass over pre-labeled data.

**Variant of**: DPO [2], sharing its RLHF reward reparameterization but replacing the paired Bradley-Terry preference model with a Kahneman-Tversky utility model over unpaired binary labels [1].

**Data it needs**: one prompt-completion pair per example plus a boolean desirability label - no completion pairs required [1]; trl's dataset schema is `{"prompt": ..., "completion": ..., "label": True/False}` [4]. If the source is preference pairs, the paper's own recipe treats the chosen half as desirable and the rejected half as undesirable, matching the same underlying data used for DPO comparisons [1]; the paper additionally tested a one-output-per-prompt setting to confirm the method works from genuinely unpaired data [1]. The paper's own recipe recommends keeping the ratio of $\lambda_D n_D$ to $\lambda_U n_U$ (loss-weighted desirable count to loss-weighted undesirable count) between 1 and 4/3 by fixing whichever weight sits at 1 [1]. Training scale in the paper spans 1B to 30B parameter models [1]. KTO is offline: it trains from a fixed labeled dataset, not from completions sampled fresh from the current policy each step [1].

**Extra models**: one frozen reference model, forward passes only, used both in the desirability term and to estimate a KL term from mismatched prompt-completion pairs in the batch - no value network and no reward model beyond the labels themselves [1]. The paper does not back-propagate through the KL term to keep training stable [1]. trl's `KTOTrainer` loads a `ref_model` by default; setting `precompute_ref_log_probs=True` caches its outputs up front and frees it from memory afterward [4]. See Cost.

**Shipped by**: trl (`KTOTrainer`, `KTOConfig`, both top-level package exports at commit `2396dfe` on the `main` branch, read 2026-08-09) [4][7]. verl's algorithm documentation index (checked 2026-08-09) lists no `kto.html` page; this card did not check verl's source code or issue tracker beyond that index, so it claims only that KTO is absent from the algorithm doc index, not that verl has no other reference to KTO anywhere [8].

## How it works

Each step: for every (prompt, completion, label) example, compute the model's and reference model's log-probabilities on the completion; separately estimate a KL reference point from mismatched pairs drawn from elsewhere in the same batch; combine into a per-example loss that pushes desirable completions' implicit reward above that reference point and undesirable completions' below it [1].

**The objective** (paper's Eq. 7) [1]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KTO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{ref}) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim D}\big[\, w(y)\,(1 - v_{\mathrm{KTO}}(x, y; \beta)) \,\big] $$

where the implicit reward is $r_{KTO}(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$, the reference point is $z_{ref} = \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim D}\big[\beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta(y'|x') \,\|\, \pi_{ref}(y'|x'))\big]$, and:

$$ v_{\mathrm{KTO}}(x,y;\beta) = \begin{cases} \sigma\big(r_{KTO}(x,y) - z_{ref}\big) & \text{if } y \text{ is desirable} \\ \sigma\big(z_{ref} - r_{KTO}(x,y)\big) & \text{if } y \text{ is undesirable} \end{cases} $$

$x$ is the prompt, $y$ the completion, $\pi_\theta$ the trained policy, $\pi_{ref}$ the frozen reference, $\sigma$ the logistic function standing in for the Kahneman-Tversky value function's shape, and $\beta$ has the same role as in DPO: lower $\beta$ permits more drift from $\pi_{ref}$; the paper finds $\beta = 0.1$ close to best on most of its datasets [1]. $w(y)$ is $\lambda_D$ for desirable completions and $\lambda_U$ for undesirable ones [1]. In practice $z_{ref}$ is estimated per batch of size $m$ by pairing each prompt with an unrelated completion and computing $\max\!\big(0, \tfrac{1}{m}\sum \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y'_U|x')}{\pi_{ref}(y'_U|x')}\big)$ over the batch, without back-propagating through it [1]; trl restates this same Eq. 7 form and calls $z_{ref}$ the KL divergence estimated from mismatched completions in the batch [4].

**Worked example.** Take $\beta = 0.1$, a desirable completion with $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)} = 2.0$ (so $r_{KTO} = 0.2$), and a batch-estimated $z_{ref} = 0.05$. Then $v = \sigma(0.2 - 0.05) = \sigma(0.15) \approx 0.537$, and with $\lambda_D = 1$ the per-example loss is $1 - 0.537 = 0.463$. For an undesirable completion with $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)} = -1.0$ (so $r_{KTO} = -0.1$), $v = \sigma(0.05 - (-0.1)) = \sigma(0.15) \approx 0.537$ and the loss is again $\approx 0.463$ at $\lambda_U = 1$ - the two loss terms are symmetric around the reference point by construction, and only the sign inside $v$'s argument differs by label.

**Weighting knob, Eq. 8** [1]: with $n_D, n_U$ the counts of desirable and undesirable examples, the paper sets $\lambda_D, \lambda_U$ so that $\frac{\lambda_D n_D}{\lambda_U n_U} \in [1, \tfrac{4}{3}]$, fixing whichever of the two sits at 1 and tuning the other - e.g., at a 1:1 class ratio, $\lambda_U = 1$ and $\lambda_D \in [1, 1.33]$ [1]. trl exposes these directly as `desirable_weight` and `undesirable_weight` [7].

**A documented underfitting mode is built into the math, not a bug**: Proposition 4.1 in the paper states that KTO does not learn from undesirable examples with sufficiently high reward or desirable examples with sufficiently low reward; the paper describes this informally as an example being too difficult to learn from, in which case the KTO update leaves the policy unchanged [1]. The paper calls this a double-edged sword: it can usefully ignore noisy, unrecoverable feedback, but can also underfit hard-but-necessary examples [1].

trl ships only the outcome-level, per-example form above (`loss_type="kto"`) plus the `apo_zero_unpaired` variant credited to the Anchored Preference Optimization paper [4][5]; the paper's process-level or score-based variants (thresholding a continuous score into desirable/undesirable, described in its Data subsection [1]) are not separate trl loss types - they are data-preparation choices left to the user.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: one forward pass per example through the trained policy for its own completion, plus the reference-model forward passes needed for $r_{KTO}$ and for the batch-level $z_{ref}$ estimate over mismatched pairs [1]. No generation happens inside the loop - all completions come from the fixed dataset - so unlike an on-policy method there is no rollout cost to amortize.
- Memory: no value network and no reward model are part of the method's own definition, only the trained policy and a frozen reference copy held for forward passes [1]; that is one fewer full trained model than PPO [6], whose value network the KTO paper explicitly frames its memory savings against in spirit (it inherits this framing from DPO's own comparison [2]).
- A naive reading of the KL term says the reference-point estimate needs the whole batch's mismatched pairs available at once; the paper's own fix - computing it without back-propagation - already removes the gradient-memory cost of that estimate, leaving only extra frozen-model forward passes [1].

**In practice, per framework:**

- trl `KTOTrainer` [4][7]: the reference model is loaded by default; setting `precompute_ref_log_probs=True` computes and caches its log-probabilities for the whole dataset up front so it does not need to stay resident during training, at the cost of an extra preprocessing pass, and this option is unsupported with `IterableDataset` or vision datasets [4]. All loss types except `apo_zero_unpaired` estimate the KL term from mismatched pairs and therefore require `train_sampling_strategy="sequential"` and a per-device train batch size greater than 1, so the batch's pairing structure stays stable [4] - `apo_zero_unpaired` skips the KL estimate entirely and drops that constraint [4]. `use_liger_kernel=True` is restricted to `loss_type="kto"`, disallows `precompute_ref_log_probs=True`, and is not supported with PEFT [4].

## How to use it

- Data prep: each row needs a prompt, a completion, and a boolean label; trl accepts this directly for the unpaired case, or preference pairs pre-split into separate desirable/undesirable rows [4]. If your source is score-based, the paper lists three concrete conversions to a binary label: threshold at a fixed value $\tau$, threshold at the mean/median score, or sample a Bernoulli label whose probability is a function (e.g. logistic) of the score [1].
- Class imbalance is a first-class concern, not an edge case: `desirable_weight` and `undesirable_weight` exist specifically to counter unequal counts of desirable and undesirable examples [7], following the paper's Eq. 8 ratio guidance [1].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give it; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's value):

| knob | trl default [7] | paper [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\beta$ | `beta` 0.1 | 0.1 (paper reports this as close-to-best on most datasets) |
| desirable weight $\lambda_D$ | `desirable_weight` 1.0 | tuned so $\lambda_D n_D / \lambda_U n_U \in [1, 4/3]$ |
| undesirable weight $\lambda_U$ | `undesirable_weight` 1.0 | tuned so $\lambda_D n_D / \lambda_U n_U \in [1, 4/3]$ |
| learning rate | `1e-6` (overridden from the shared `TrainingArguments` default of `5e-5`) | not stated |
| max sequence length | `max_length` 1024 | not stated |
| sampling strategy | `train_sampling_strategy` "sequential" (required for KL-estimating loss types) | not stated |
| loss type | `loss_type` "kto" (alternative: "apo_zero_unpaired" [5]) | not applicable - single loss defined |

  trl's default $\beta$ matches the paper's own reported near-optimal value exactly [1][7]; the learning rate default of `1e-6` is far below the library's general fine-tuning default of `5e-5`, and the paper does not publish a learning rate to check it against [1][7].
- Trade-off: raising per-device batch size gives a more stable KL reference-point estimate (it is averaged over more mismatched pairs) but trl requires it to be greater than 1 regardless for any KL-estimating loss type [4] - too small a batch is a documented failure source (see While it runs).
- Install, launch, and general framework mechanics (parallelism, PEFT integration details, precision flags) belong to the trl framework card; this card stops at the knobs that define the method.

## While it runs

- Signals trl logs, and what they mean [4]: `kl`, the estimated KL divergence used as the loss's reference point; `rewards/chosen` and `rewards/rejected`, the average implicit reward $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$ for desirable and undesirable completions respectively; `rewards/margins`, their gap; `logps/chosen` and `logps/rejected`, average log-probabilities on each class; and `entropy`, the average predicted-token-distribution entropy. A healthy run should show `rewards/margins` growing and desirable/undesirable reward diverging; a `kl` stuck at exactly 0.0 alongside otherwise-moving loss was the leading symptom reported in a maintainer-triaged NaN-rewards issue below [9].
- Published reference run: the paper's Table 1 winrate comparison on Mistral-7B/OpenAssistant (KTO 0.652 vs. DPO 0.600 vs. unaligned 0.525, all-$y$-per-$x$; KTO 0.631 with only one $y$ per $x$) is the citable known-good result to compare against, with 90% confidence intervals reported alongside each number [1].
- Degeneracy, maintainer-confirmed: an all-one-class batch causes a float division by zero in trl's `KTOTrainer` when the undesirable-example list is empty; maintainers agreed a warning or guard was missing and the reporter's fix was merged [10].
- Numerical trap, maintainer-confirmed: in a separate issue reporting NaN rewards and eventual NaN loss, a trl maintainer (kashif) stated the training needs a good mix of good and bad examples in each batch for correct KL estimation, and that per-batch class imbalance (the reporter tested 2k desirable vs. 10k undesirable) can produce NaN rewards and losses even when the paper's own [1, 4/3] weighting range is respected as an overall dataset ratio - the ratio guidance is dataset-level, not batch-level, and small or skewed batches can still starve the KL estimate of one class [9].
- Known failure mode from the paper's own limitations: Proposition 4.1 (see How it works) means examples the paper calls too difficult to learn from receive near-zero gradient, which the paper itself frames as a possible source of underfitting on data that would have been informative for a pairwise method [1]; the conclusion states plainly that the authors make no claim that KTO is the best HALO for all scenarios [1].
- Named successor: trl's `apo_zero_unpaired` loss type is credited by trl to the Anchored Preference Optimization paper [4][5], usable when you believe desirable completions are already better than the model's default output and you want to skip the KL estimate entirely [4].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own §4.4 guidance is that KTO is the natural choice for genuinely binary feedback, and that even for preference data it tends to win when that data is noisy or contains intransitive judgments (common in multi-annotator datasets like SHP or OpenAssistant, and even in synthetic AI feedback such as UltraFeedback), while clean, low-noise, transitive preference data favors DPO instead [1]. The comparison is about learning efficiently from the feedback given - it is not a claim that KTO adds any new model capability beyond what the same amount of clean preference data would teach via DPO.
