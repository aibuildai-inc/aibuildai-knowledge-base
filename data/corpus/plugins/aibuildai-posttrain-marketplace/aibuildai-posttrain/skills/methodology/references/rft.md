# RFT

Sample many chain-of-thought completions from an already-SFT'd model, keep one per distinct correct reasoning path per question, and run ordinary supervised fine-tuning on the enlarged dataset.

**RFT** (Rejection sampling Fine-Tuning) is a data-augmentation method for LLM math reasoning, introduced in "Scaling Relationship on Learning Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models," which proposes to apply rejection sampling to generate and collect correct reasoning paths as augmented fine-tuning datasets [1]. Its parent is supervised fine-tuning (SFT): RFT trains with the same loss as SFT, only on a dataset the SFT model itself helped build [1]. Mechanism: an SFT model $\pi$ samples $k$ candidate reasoning paths and answers per training question at temperature 0.7, wrong-answer paths are filtered out by comparing the final answer (and by Python-executing the equations), and one path is kept per distinct list of equations to deduplicate near-identical solutions [1]. The paper gives two reasons to do this instead of writing more labeled data: augmenting new data by hand is hard (writing new, diverse, correct math problems does not scale), and RFT's own ablation shows the gain tracks the number of distinct correct reasoning paths per question, not the raw sample count k, so deduplication is central to why it works [1]. Two other augmentation strategies the paper tried directly - new-query augmentation and revision augmentation - are reported to leave accuracy essentially unchanged from plain SFT, at best a small improvement [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01825 [1].

RFT was formalized as one point in DeepSeekMath's "unified paradigm" alongside SFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO, which quotes it as: "RFT further fine-tunes the SFT model on the filtered outputs sampled from the SFT model based on SFT questions. RFT filters the outputs based on the correctness of their answers" [2]. DeepSeekMath also names and formalizes "Online RFT," which differs only in sampling from the live policy instead of the frozen SFT model [2] (see When to pick it). In the originating paper's own experiments, RFT with k=100 raised LLaMA-7B from an SFT accuracy of 35.9% to 41.7% maj1@1 on GSM8K, and aggregating rejection-sampled data from four models (dataset $D'_{U13B}$) pushed LLaMA-7B further to 49.3% (Table 1, Table 3) [1]; the gain vanished for the 33B model (54.5% RFT vs. 54.6% SFT), because the larger SFT model overfits the training questions and generates far less diverse correct paths per question (88.7 correct but only 2.78 distinct, versus 5.25 distinct for the 7B model) [1]. Lineage: SFT -> STaR's generate/filter/fine-tune bootstrap loop (2022) [3] -> RFT (2023) [1] -> formalized as an offline baseline inside DeepSeekMath's unified paradigm, next to its online variant "Online RFT" and to GRPO (2024) [2].

**When to pick it**: pick RFT when you already have an SFT model, a rule-checkable correctness signal (final-answer match, unit tests, an executable checker), and want a cheap, RL-free way to squeeze more distinct correct reasoning paths out of that model without training a critic or reward model [1]. Compare against its parent, plain SFT [1]: RFT costs one round of large-scale sampling (k=100 in the paper) plus filtering before a normal SFT run, for a 2-6 point maj1@1 gain on 7B/13B LLaMA models that shrinks to nothing at 33B [1]. Compare against the nearest offline preference alternative, DPO [4]: DPO needs chosen/rejected pairs and a pairwise classification loss, and never has to generate from the model being trained during fine-tuning [4], whereas RFT needs only positive (correct) samples and a plain SFT loss, at the cost of the large one-off sampling pass RFT requires up front [1][2]. Compare against the nearest online neighbor, Online RFT [2]: Online RFT keeps RFT's exact objective and rule-based filter but samples from the live policy every step instead of the frozen SFT model once; DeepSeekMath reports Online RFT significantly outperforms RFT on GSM8K and MATH, with the gap opening up in the later stage of training as the live policy diverges further from the original SFT model [2].

**Variant of**: SFT [1]; RFT and Online RFT sit in the same "Data Source / Reward Function / Gradient Coefficient" table entry pattern as SFT in DeepSeekMath's unified paradigm, differing from SFT only in dataset and gradient mask [2].

**Data it needs**: a set of prompts/questions with a way to check correctness of a final answer (the paper used ~7,473 GSM8K training questions, each producing up to k=100 sampled reasoning paths at temperature 0.7 before filtering to distinct-equation-list paths) [1][5]. No preference pairs, no scalar reward beyond right/wrong, no process-level labels are required for the outcome-supervision variant the paper reports as its headline result; a process-reward variant is not defined in the paper [1]. Sampling is offline in the base RFT described by the paper (all k samples come from a single frozen SFT checkpoint, reused across the whole fine-tune) [1][2]; DeepSeekMath's Online RFT resamples from the current policy every step, making it on-policy [2].

**Extra models**: none required by the paper's own method - no value network, no reward model, no separate reference model; correctness is checked by string/Python-equation matching against the known answer, not by a learned judge [1]. Framework note: no library ships a dedicated RFT trainer that loads any extra model by default (see Shipped by); building it means a sampling/inference pass with the SFT checkpoint plus a rule-based filter, feeding an ordinary SFT trainer - no new model class is involved.

**Shipped by**: no training library ships a dedicated "RFT" trainer as of this reading (checked trl's exported trainers - `SFTTrainer`, `DPOTrainer`, `GRPOTrainer`, `RLOOTrainer`, `KTOTrainer`, `RewardTrainer` - and its `trl/experimental` subpackage, and verl's `recipe` directory, neither lists an RFT/rejection-sampling-fine-tuning entry) [6][7][8]. The paper's own reference implementation is the `OFA-Sys/gsm8k-ScRel` GitHub repository, which ships `group_sample_*.sh` for rejection sampling, `collect_rejection_sampling.py` for the filtering step, and `train_xb.sh` for the subsequent SFT run [5]. Building RFT on a modern stack needs no new loss: it is a rejection-sampling/filtering loop (build with any inference engine, e.g. vLLM) that writes a dataset, followed by an existing SFT trainer such as trl's `SFTTrainer`, which trl's top-level package exports [9] - a data-pipeline job, not a new training algorithm.

## How it works

The loop in one line: sample k completions per question from the SFT model, keep the correct and equation-distinct ones, fine-tune on the union with the plain SFT loss [1].

**Reasoning path selection (Algorithm 1)** [1]: for each question's k sampled paths, walk them in order; for each path, extract its list of equations (found via the `<<equation>>` markers, whitespace-stripped and joined into a key string). If a path's equation-list key has not been seen for this question, keep it and record the key. If the key has already been kept, compare the new path to the currently kept path for that key by Levenshtein distance to the other kept paths for the question, and swap in whichever path is more dissimilar to the rest of the kept set - the paper states this is meant to keep the most mutually dissimilar reasoning paths for better generalization [1]. Paths whose final answer does not match the ground truth, or whose embedded equations fail Python evaluation, are dropped before this step [1].

**DeepSeekMath's formalization of the training objective** [2], where $D$ is the augmented dataset (question $q$ drawn from the SFT question distribution, output $o$ sampled from the frozen SFT policy $\pi_{sft}$), and $I(o)$ is the correctness indicator kept from rejection sampling:

$$ J_{RFT}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim P_{sft}(Q),\ o \sim \pi_{sft}(O|q)} \left[ \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} I(o) \log \pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{<t}) \right], \qquad I(o) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{answer of } o \text{ is correct} \\ 0 & \text{answer of } o \text{ is incorrect} \end{cases} $$

This is DeepSeekMath's read of the loss RFT trains on, framed in its "gradient coefficient" formalism [2]; the originating RFT paper itself describes the same fine-tuning step in prose - "we fine-tune $D'$ on pre-trained LLM $\rho$ to $\pi_{RFT}$" - without writing this explicit per-token objective [1]. Because filtering already drops incorrect paths before training, $I(o)$ is always 1 on the retained dataset in practice; the indicator formalizes what the rejection-and-dedup step already enforces, and per-token loss is uniformly weighted across all kept tokens, unlike GRPO's group-normalized advantage [2].

**Online RFT** [2] changes only the sampling source, from the frozen SFT policy to the live policy $\pi_\theta$: $\nabla_\theta J_{OnRFT}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim P_{sft}(Q),\ o \sim \pi_\theta(O|q)}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} I(o)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{<t})\right]$ [2]. Its gradient coefficient formula is identical to RFT's [2].

**Worked example**: a question sampled $k=4$ times gives paths with equation-key strings `A, A, B, C` and answer-correctness `correct, correct, correct, wrong`. The wrong path is dropped first. Among the remaining `A, A, B`, the first `A` is kept, the second `A` is a duplicate key and is compared by Levenshtein distance to the other kept paths (here just `B`) against the first `A`'s distance to `B` - whichever of the two `A`-paths is farther from `B` is the one retained. The augmented dataset for this question ends up with exactly 2 rows (one `A`, one `B`), out of 4 sampled and 3 correct - the paper's central point that distinct-path count, not sample count, is what training sees [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: RFT adds no trained model beyond the policy being fine-tuned - there is no value network, no reward model, no reference-model KL term in the objective in Equation (8)-style form [2], so per-step training memory and compute match plain SFT once the augmented dataset exists. All of RFT's extra cost sits before training starts: generating k candidate completions per question at inference time, then running the Algorithm-1 filter (answer check, equation extraction, and O(k) pairwise Levenshtein comparisons per question in the worst case) [1]. That generation pass is a one-time offline cost for base RFT, but becomes a recurring per-step cost for Online RFT, since it resamples from the live policy every update [2].

**In practice, per framework**: the paper itself measured this trade-off in FLOPs: for the 7B model, SFT training took an estimated 1.7×10^17 FLOPs versus 1.4×10^18 FLOPs for the RFT rejection-sampling inference pass (100 samples over 7,473 training questions) - roughly 8x more compute in sampling than in the SFT step it augments, and the ratio is similar at 13B (3.3×10^17 SFT vs 2.6×10^18 RFT inference) and 33B (7.7×10^17 SFT vs 6.9×10^18 RFT inference) (Table 4) [1]. No training framework's own docs were found describing an RFT-specific cost profile, because no framework ships an RFT trainer (see Shipped by); the closest documented framework costs are trl's `SFTTrainer` for the fine-tuning half and any inference engine (e.g. vLLM, cited elsewhere in this corpus) for the sampling half, neither of which is RFT-specific.

## How to use it

- Start from an SFT checkpoint on the target task; RFT's inputs are that model, the SFT question set, and a way to check final-answer correctness (the paper used Python evaluation of extracted equations against the GSM8K ground-truth answer) [1].
- Sample k completions per question at temperature 0.7, following the same generation setting the paper attributes to Cobbe et al. for consistency with prior GSM8K work [1].
- Filter to correct answers, then deduplicate by equation-list key using Algorithm 1, keeping the most mutually-dissimilar path per key [1].
- Key knobs, paper values as the only anchor (no framework ships this method, so there is no framework-default column):

| knob | paper value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| sampling count k | swept at 1, 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, 100; headline result uses k=100 |
| sampling temperature | 0.7 |
| SFT epochs (fine-tuning stage) | 3 |
| SFT batch size | 128 |
| SFT peak learning rate | 2e-5, 3% warmup |

- Trade-off on k: the paper's own sweep shows using k=3 already beats SFT by about 2 points stably, and larger k keeps helping but with diminishing returns because distinct-path count grows sub-linearly with k - it does not double when k doubles (Table 2: e.g. 7B goes from 1.17 distinct paths/question at k=1 to 5.25 at k=100, far short of a 100x increase) [1]. A "no dedup" k=100 run performs similarly to the deduplicated k=100 run but costs much more training time, showing accuracy tracks distinct-path count rather than raw augmented-sample count [1].
- Trade-off on model choice for sampling: larger SFT models generate more correct paths per question but fewer distinct ones (33B: 88.7 correct, 2.78 distinct, versus 7B: 53.3 correct, 5.25 distinct), so aggregating rejection samples from several smaller/mid-size models ($D'_{U13B}$, combining 7B/7B-2/13B/13B-2) outperformed sampling repeatedly from one large model, and gave the 33B model a boost that single-model RFT could not [1].

## While it runs

- The paper reports no training-time monitoring metrics (no loss curves, KL, or clip-fraction guidance) because RFT's fine-tuning stage is plain SFT; the method-specific signal to watch is upstream of training: distinct reasoning paths per question and correct-paths-per-question during the sampling/filtering pass, both reported in Table 1 and Table 2 as the quantities that predict whether RFT will help [1].
- Published reference numbers to compare against: Table 1 in the paper gives SFT vs. RFT k=100 maj1@1/maj1@100 pairs per model size (e.g. LLaMA-7B: SFT 35.9/48.7 vs RFT 41.7/52.7; LLaMA-13B: SFT 43.0/55.2 vs RFT 49.1/59.9), and Table 3 gives the best aggregated result, RFT-U13B on LLaMA-7B at 49.3/61.8 [1]. The paper's own code/checkpoint repository republishes the same table alongside a further RFT-U33B row [5].
- Known degeneracy, from the paper's own finding: at 33B, RFT does not improve over SFT (54.5 vs 54.6 maj1@1) because the larger SFT model overfits the training questions and its rejection samples collapse to very few distinct reasoning paths (2.78 distinct vs 5.25-5.29 for 7B/13B models); the paper explicitly attributes this to the 33B SFT model having "difficulty generating more diverse paths on the training set questions" [1]. A named default divergence: DeepSeekMath's Online RFT keeps RFT's exact filter and objective but resamples every step instead of once; the paper states this diverges from RFT in its later training stage as the live policy departs from the initial SFT model, and reports Online RFT significantly outperforming RFT on both GSM8K and MATH [2].
- Named successor / contrast: DeepSeekMath identifies a structural gap Online RFT does not close - Online RFT does not penalize incorrect responses and uniformly reinforces all correct responses at the same intensity, whereas GRPO's group-normalized advantage differentially reinforces and penalizes responses by magnitude, and DeepSeekMath reports GRPO surpassing Online RFT because of this [2].
- Known failure modes: only the 33B diversity-collapse finding above is documented in the originating paper's own results section; the paper also reports its two other tried augmentation strategies (new-query augmentation, revision augmentation) as not clearly beating plain SFT, and does not pursue them further, implying they are known-not-to-work variants rather than RFT failure modes [1]. Searching huggingface/trl's GitHub issues for "rejection sampling fine-tuning" returns 3 results, the closest being a request to add a dedicated Expert Iteration trainer (RFT's closer relative than any currently-shipped trl trainer); a trl maintainer closed it, citing low demand and adoption that has not kept pace with other post-training algorithms, and noting that the building blocks the requester had proposed reusing (`IterativeSFTTrainer`, `best_of_n_sampler`) have since both been removed from trl [10].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own headline finding is that RFT's benefit comes specifically from the number of distinct correct reasoning paths a question is trained on, not from more copies of the same reasoning path or from more total augmented samples (the no-dedup vs. dedup comparison at equal k=100 performs similarly) [1]; RFT does not help models that already generate near-duplicate correct paths (the 33B case), so it is a fix for path diversity in weaker/smaller SFT models, not a general-purpose accuracy booster independent of a model's sampling diversity [1].
