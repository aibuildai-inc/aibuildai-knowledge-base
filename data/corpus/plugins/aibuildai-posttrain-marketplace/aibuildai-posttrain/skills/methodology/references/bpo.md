# BPO (Bootstrapped Preference Optimization)

https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08730

DPO for multimodal LLMs, run on preference pairs you never have to label by hand: keep the existing ground-truth answer as the chosen response, and generate the rejected response yourself, either by feeding the model a noise-corrupted image or by prompting its LLM component to inject a plausible error into the ground-truth answer.

**BPO** (Bootstrapped Preference Optimization) is a preference-tuning method for multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), introduced by Pi et al. to fight a specific failure mode: an MLLM's language backbone is pretrained on far more text than the multimodal alignment stage ever sees, so its output distribution stays biased toward that pretraining corpus and can override what is actually in the image [1]. The paper "conducts preference learning with datasets containing negative responses bootstrapped from the model itself" [1]. Its parent is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which fits a policy directly on preference pairs by minimizing a closed-form classification loss derived from the RLHF objective, without training a separate reward model [2]. BPO reuses DPO's loss unchanged (Sec. 4 restates DPO's derivation and loss term for term) [1] and contributes only the negative-response recipe: (1) image-weakened prompting, which adds a Gaussian mask to the image's CLIP embeddings before inference to elicit answers dominated by pretraining bias, and (2) error injection, which prompts the MLLM's own LLM component to rewrite a ground-truth answer with a plausible but wrong detail (a different object count, attribute, or color) [1]. The paper gives three reasons to exist: hand-labeling hallucination preference pairs (as prior RLHF-for-MLLM work did) is costly and does not scale; treating the MLLM as a mixture of a pretraining-text distribution and a weaker vision-alignment distribution lets negatives be generated automatically instead of collected; and reward-model-based RLHF (PPO) is unstable to tune, with multimodal reward models specifically prone to more severe reward hacking than text-only ones [1].

BPO reports gains on LLaVA1.5 checkpoints: on the paper's MM-Vet + LLaVA-Wild + Object HalBench comparison (Table 3), LLaVA1.5-7B's MM-Vet total rises from 31.7 to 36.8, its Object HalBench response-level hallucination rate falls from 45.9% to 31.9%, and its LLaVA-Wild score rises from 63.8 to 71.6 after BPO tuning; the tuned 7B model also beats the untuned 13B baseline on most of these tasks [1]. Checking Semantic Scholar's citation API for arXiv:2403.08730, which records 90 citing works for the paper [10], against the first 50 of those 90 (default result order, fetched 2026-08-09) found no named production or deployed system citing BPO; the citing set is entirely research follow-ups on multimodal hallucination mitigation and preference optimization, such as VistaDPO (video-DPO, 17 citations in that snapshot) and LPOI (listwise preference for VLMs, 6 citations) [11]. Lineage in one line: DPO (2023 [2]) -> BPO (2024 [1]), a data-construction recipe layered on the unmodified DPO loss, with no confirmed named successor in the checked citation set.

**When to pick it**: pick BPO when preference-tuning a multimodal LLM to reduce hallucination or pretraining-bias effects, you already have SFT-style image-question-groundtruth-answer triples, and you cannot afford to hand-label hallucination preference pairs — BPO's whole contribution is generating the rejected side of the pair automatically [1]. It changes nothing about the loss versus its parent DPO [2]; the parent already requires you to supply the preference pairs, typically from human labels, whereas BPO supplies them by bootstrapping from the model and a ground-truth answer [1]. Nearest offline alternative is plain SFT on the same ground-truth answers: the paper's own ablation (Table 4) shows LLaVA1.5-7B's MM-Vet total moving from 31.7 (untuned) to only 33.3 with SFT alone, versus 36.8 with BPO — SFT alone captures less than a third of BPO's total gain, showing the bootstrapped negative responses (not just more positive fine-tuning) drive most of the improvement [1]. Nearest online neighbor is PPO-based multimodal RLHF (fit a reward model, then run PPO) [3]: the paper's own text says this is harder to tune and reward models trained on multimodal inputs are subject to more severe reward hacking than text-only ones [1].

**Variant of**: DPO [2].

**Data it needs**: per sample, an image, a question, and a chosen (ground-truth) response, exactly DPO's chosen/rejected pair format but with the rejected side synthesized rather than collected [1]. The paper draws ground-truth positives from three existing instruction-tuning datasets — ShareGPT-V (57,906 samples), LLaVAR (55,445 samples), and LLaVA-Instruct (54,359 samples), about 167,710 image-question-answer triples in total (Table 2) [1]. Negatives are generated once, offline, before training starts, by running inference on the current MLLM checkpoint (noised-image prompting) or on its LLM component (error injection) [1]; training itself then runs standard offline DPO over this fixed, pre-built dataset — it is not on-policy, since no fresh sampling happens during the DPO optimization steps [1].

**Extra models**: a frozen reference policy $\pi_0$ (the DPO KL-anchor, here the LLaVA1.5 checkpoint before BPO tuning), required by the objective exactly as in DPO — no value network, no separate reward model [1][2]. The authors' own training script does not pass a `ref_model` to trl's `DPOTrainer` and trains the policy under LoRA with `target_modules` (not `target_parameters`) [6]. For that configuration, trl's `DPOTrainer` does not skip the reference model: when no `ref_model` is passed and the model is a PEFT model, it calls `model.add_adapter("ref", default_config)` and copies the `.default.` adapter weights into new `.ref.` parameters, i.e. it allocates and holds a second, frozen LoRA adapter as the reference, and only skips this (computing reference log-probs from the base model with adapters disabled) when the LoRA config uses `target_parameters` on `peft<0.20.0` (confirmed in `trl/trainer/dpo_trainer.py` at commit `85709ab`, 2026-07-31) [4][6]. Cost has the detail.

**Shipped by**: a GitHub repository-name/description search for "bootstrapped preference optimization" and "BPO" returns exactly one result, the authors' own repository, and trl's DPOTrainer docs page contains no mention of BPO — GitHub's code-search API (which would find a `BPOTrainer`-style class inside another framework's source) requires authentication not available in this session, so this is a repo-level search only, not a code-level one [5][6][13]. The authors publish a standalone repository (`pipilurj/bootstrapped-preference-optimization-BPO`, commit `ecaf280`, 2024-08-23) that defines a `CustomDPOTrainer(DPOTrainer)` subclass of trl's `DPOTrainer` inside `llava/train/bpo_llava.py`, wired to LLaVA's own training code, not merged into trl itself [6]. trl exports `DPOTrainer` as a supported, top-level trainer that BPO's loss reuses unchanged [5]. Building BPO on any other DPO-capable framework means writing the two negative-generation routines (Gaussian-mask the vision-tower embeddings before an inference pass; prompt the LLM component to inject an error into a ground-truth answer) as a one-time offline data-prep step, then handing the resulting pairs to that framework's existing DPO trainer — no new loss or sampling loop is needed.

## How it works

The loop in one line: build a chosen/rejected preference dataset once — chosen is the existing ground-truth answer, rejected is bootstrapped from the model — then run unmodified DPO training over it [1].

**The general RLHF objective** the paper restates before deriving DPO (Eq. 2) [1]:

$$ J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{I,q \sim d_0}\left[\, \mathbb{E}_{r \sim \pi(\cdot|I,q)}[\phi^*(I,q,r)] - \eta\, D_{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|I,q)\,\|\,\pi_0(\cdot|I,q)\big) \,\right] $$

$I$ is the image, $q$ the query, $r$ a response, $\phi^*$ the (unknown, Bradley-Terry) reward function, $\pi_0$ the frozen initial (reference) policy, and $\eta>0$ the KL-penalty coefficient [1].

**The training loss** (Eq. 4), identical in form to DPO's [2], is what BPO actually optimizes [1]:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{(I,q,r_w,r_l)\in\mathcal{D}} -\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\eta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(r_w|I,q)}{\pi_0(r_w|I,q)} - \eta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(r_l|I,q)}{\pi_0(r_l|I,q)}\right)\right] $$

$r_w$ is the winning (chosen, ground-truth) response, $r_l$ the losing (rejected, bootstrapped) response, $\pi_\theta$ the policy being trained, $\pi_0$ the frozen reference, and $\sigma$ the sigmoid; $\eta$ plays the role trl calls $\beta$ [1][5]. The first term inside $\sigma$ raises the reference-normalized log-likelihood of the chosen response and the second lowers that of the rejected one, so the loss widens the margin between them [1].

**Negative-response construction**, BPO's actual contribution, has two variants, both shipped in the authors' data-generation scripts [1][6]:

- *Image-weakened prompting*: apply a Gaussian mask to the CLIP image embeddings (analogous to a diffusion forward step) before the MLLM generates a response, so the image signal is progressively suppressed and the model's output drifts toward whatever is common in its text-pretraining corpus [1]. Worked example from the paper's own figure: as the noise level rises, the log-likelihood the model assigns to the correct token "bear" falls while the log-likelihoods of generic pretraining-common words such as "Person", "Man", and "Woman" rise until one of them overtakes "bear" [1].
- *Error injection*: prompt the MLLM's LLM component (Vicuna, in the paper's LLaVA setup) to rewrite a ground-truth response with a plausible-but-wrong detail. The paper's own prompt-template example: ground truth "there are 3 people in the image" is rewritten to "there are only 1 person in the image"; "the color of the apple is black" is rewritten to "the color of the apple is yellow" (Table 1) [1].

Both negative types are paired with the same ground-truth positive and trained with the one loss above; the paper does not define a separate loss variant for the two negative types [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: BPO's training-loop cost is identical to DPO's — one forward/backward pass over the policy for both $r_w$ and $r_l$, plus one forward-only pass through the frozen reference for the same two sequences, no per-step generation [1][2]. The generation cost sits entirely in a one-time, offline data-construction phase (image-weakened inference passes and/or LLM error-injection calls over the whole dataset) that happens once before training starts, not once per training step the way an on-policy method's rollout does [1].
- Memory: the objective needs the trained policy plus one frozen reference copy — no value network, no reward model — exactly DPO's footprint [1][2]. A naive reading of "bootstrapped from the model itself" could suggest the method needs a second live copy of the policy running alongside training to keep generating negatives; the data-construction pass is offline and finished before DPO training begins, so no such second copy is held during training [1].

**In practice, per framework:**

- Only the authors' own repo ships a runnable trainer [6]. It fine-tunes LLaVA1.5 checkpoints with LoRA under DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, per-device train batch size 4, gradient accumulation 1, bf16, gradient checkpointing on; the paper reports about 17 hours for the 7B model and 28 hours for the 13B model on 8× A40 GPUs (48 GB each) [1].
- Because the training script trains a LoRA adapter with `target_modules` and passes no explicit `ref_model` to `CustomDPOTrainer`/`DPOTrainer`, trl allocates a second, frozen `"ref"` LoRA adapter by copying the trained adapter's weights, rather than disabling the adapter to fall back on the base model — so this implementation does pay the frozen-reference memory cost the theory layer above describes, just as a second LoRA adapter (small relative to the frozen base weights shared by both adapters) rather than a second full model copy [4][6].
- The two negative-generation strategies are not equally expensive to run at scale: the repo maintainer states that, at the time of the work, there was no acceleration package for MLLM inference, so generating image-weakened responses was much slower than error injection, which is LLM-only and could be accelerated with vLLM — this is why the released dataset scales up error-injection negatives relative to image-weakened ones [9].

## How to use it

- Data prep: start from an existing SFT-style image-question-answer dataset (ShareGPT-V, LLaVAR, and LLaVA-Instruct in the paper) and keep its answers as the chosen responses; generate rejected responses with the image-weakened and/or error-injection procedures above, once, before training [1]. The maintainer confirms training should start from the LLaVA checkpoint after its SFT stage, not the pretraining-only checkpoint — an earlier version of the released script started from the wrong checkpoint and was corrected after a user flagged it [9].
- Reward/label convention: no scalar reward at all — each example is just a chosen/rejected pair, exactly DPO's format; the released preference dataset stores this as a chosen response labeled score 1 and a rejected response labeled score 0 [1][12].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value):

| knob | paper (Sec. 5.1) [1] | official repo script default [6] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LoRA rank | 64 | 32 (`--lora_r 32`) |
| learning rate | $2\times10^{-6}$ | $2\times10^{-6}$ |
| epochs | 2 | 2 |
| per-device batch size | 4 | 4 |
| KL coefficient $\eta$ / `beta` | not stated | 0.1 (`TrainingArguments.beta` default) |
| GPUs | 8× A40 (48 GB) | not stated in script |

  The LoRA-rank row is a real, maintainer-confirmed discrepancy, not a typo to shrug off: asked about it, the paper's author replied "the lora rank is indeed 64, as mentioned in the paper... But setting the rank to 32 does not hurt the performance much" [7] — the released script's default (32) undercuts the paper's own reported setting (64), and the author's own claim that this barely matters has not been independently re-verified on this card.
- Trade-off a run designer faces: the image-weakened and error-injection negatives are not interchangeable at equal cost — the paper's own ablation (Table 5) shows plain self-generated negatives (no image weakening) raise LLaVA-7B's MM-Vet total only from 31.7 to 32.4, while image-weakened negatives raise it further to 34.3, but image-weakened negatives are the slower of the two to generate at scale (see Cost) [1][9].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's own training-curve analysis (Sec. 5.6, Fig. 6) is the first-hand guidance here — during BPO training the log-probability of the chosen (positive) response stays roughly stable while the log-probability of the rejected (negative) response decreases steadily; contrast with SFT on the same data, where the log-probability of the ground-truth response increases continuously, which the paper flags as a path toward overfitting and catastrophic forgetting [1].
- Published reference run: Table 3's LLaVA1.5-7B-BPO and LLaVA1.5-13B-BPO rows against their untuned LLaVA1.5-7B/13B baselines are the paper's own reference numbers to compare a reproduction against (MM-Vet total 31.7→36.8 and 36.8→41.4; Object HalBench response-level hallucination 45.9%→31.9% and 45.2%→27.3%; LLaVA-Wild 63.8→71.6 and 71.2→74.4) [1]. A maintainer reply confirms these numbers are sensitive to the exact reproduction setup: asked about a lower reproduced MM-Vet score, the author said the released checkpoint should score around 36.0, and that a 33.3 result "may indicate either something is wrong with the inference code, or the package versions are incorrect" — batch size and GPU count differences alone were enough to shift the author's own reruns slightly [8].
- Degeneracies and defaults: a wrong vision-tower checkpoint path is a silent failure mode reported in the repo's issue tracker — a user reporting a low MM-Vet score self-diagnosed and fixed their own reproduction bug, tracing it to the local CLIP path being set to `clip-vit-large-patch14-336` instead of `clip-vit-large-patch14`; the maintainer did not comment further after that fix was posted [8]. The LoRA-rank gap between the paper (64) and the shipped script (32) above is a config default that diverges from the paper's own reported setting [1][6][7]. If image-weakened and error-injected negatives are both too mild relative to the ground-truth answer, the preference margin the DPO loss can learn from shrinks; the paper's own Table 5 ablation is the direct evidence that weaker negatives (no image weakening) leave more of the gain on the table [1].
- Named successors: none confirmed in the citation set checked for this card (Semantic Scholar, first 50 of 90 citing works for arXiv:2403.08730, fetched 2026-08-09); several later papers build related bootstrapped/self-generated-negative ideas for MLLM preference tuning, but none in the checked set explicitly frames itself as fixing a documented BPO bias [11].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own Limitations section (Sec. 7) — the method does not consider malicious inputs that could induce the model to produce harmful responses, and it does not include a sample-selection algorithm to filter out low-quality bootstrapped negatives, both left to future work [1]. From the repository's closed issues: generation cost asymmetry between the two negative types (image-weakened generation was the slow path at the time of release, forcing the released dataset to lean on error injection) is a maintainer-documented practical constraint, not a limitation the paper's text names [9].
- What the gain is — and is not: the method's own analysis targets a specific bias — grounding responses more in the image versus the LLM's pretraining distribution — and its ablations (Tables 4 and 5) show the gain comes specifically from the bootstrapped negatives, not merely from more positive-only fine-tuning (SFT) on the same ground-truth answers [1]. The paper's Limitations section is explicit that safety against malicious/jailbreak inputs and negative-data quality filtering are both out of scope for what BPO addresses [1].
