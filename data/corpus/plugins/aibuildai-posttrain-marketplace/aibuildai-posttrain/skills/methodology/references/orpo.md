# ORPO

Skip the reference model entirely: fine-tune on preference pairs with a normal SFT loss plus a small odds-ratio penalty term that pushes down the disfavored response, in one training stage.

**ORPO** (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization, also titled by its authors "Monolithic Preference Optimization without Reference Model") is a reference-model-free method for aligning a language model on chosen/rejected response pairs, introduced by Hong, Lee, and Thorne, who state that "a minor penalty for the disfavored generation style is sufficient for preference-aligned SFT" and that this lets the odds-ratio term be appended directly to the SFT loss, eliminating the need for a separate preference-alignment phase [1]. The paper groups ORPO, together with DPO, under a family it calls "Alignment without Reward Model" - methods that mitigate the need for reinforcement learning by skipping the separate reward-model and RL stages that RLHF/PPO use [1]; DPO is the paper's own point of reference for that family [2]. Mechanically, ORPO trains on a weighted sum of the standard causal-LM negative-log-likelihood loss and a log-odds-ratio term between the chosen and rejected responses, with a single scalar $\lambda$ controlling how much the odds-ratio term contributes [1]. The paper gives three reasons for this design: RLHF is unstable and needs extensive hyperparameter search because of PPO's own instability and reward-model sensitivity [1]; folding preference alignment into SFT removes the extra reference model and its forward passes that RLHF and DPO both require [1]; and the odds ratio is more stable than the probability ratio DPO and IPO use, because it produces less extreme discrimination against the disfavored response when the alignment step is fused into SFT rather than run afterward [1].

Adoption on the Hugging Face Hub, searched by "orpo" sorted by downloads, surfaces only community fine-tunes, the most-downloaded at 8,853 downloads, with no result matching a landmark foundation-model lab release [3]; LLaMA-Factory lists ORPO among its integrated training approaches, alongside PPO, DPO, and KTO [4]. In the paper's own controlled comparison, holding the base model fixed, Phi-2 (2.7B) fine-tuned with ORPO alone reached 71.80% / 6.35% (AlpacaEval 1.0 / 2.0), against 48.37% / 0.11% for SFT alone and 50.63% / 0.78% for SFT+DPO on the same base model (Table 1) [1]. Lineage in one line: RLHF/PPO and DPO (parent-family alternative, 2017/2023 [5][2]) -> ORPO (2024 [1]) -> shipped as a stable method in LLaMA-Factory [4] and under an unstable experimental path in trl [6], no documented bias-fixing successor found (see While it runs). The paper was confirmed as the right "ORPO" by two independent checks: it is the top-cited result (653 citations) among name-colliding "ORPO" arXiv papers, and trl's own ORPOTrainer documentation names this exact paper and these exact authors as the method's origin [7].

**When to pick it**: preference pairs are available but you want to skip both the reward-model stage of RLHF/PPO [5] and the frozen reference-model pass of DPO [2], at the cost of folding preference alignment into the SFT stage itself rather than running it after SFT. Nearest offline alternative and closest family relative is DPO [2], which needs a frozen reference model for its probability-ratio objective; ORPO needs none [1]. Nearest online neighbor is RLHF with PPO [5], which needs both a reward model and a value network and is the instability the paper cites as its motivation [1].

**Variant of**: not a strict derivative of one parent; the paper positions it against DPO [2] as the other member of its "Alignment without Reward Model" family [1], contrasting its odds-ratio objective with DPO's and IPO's probability-ratio objective (paper's Eq. 16) [1].

**Data it needs**: preference pairs - a prompt with a chosen and a rejected response per example, the same shape DPO consumes; no reward score, no reference-model logprobs are required at training time. The paper's main runs used the binarized UltraFeedback dataset and Anthropic HH-RLHF, filtering out pairs where chosen equals rejected or either is empty [1]; trl's ORPOTrainer expects the same `chosen`/`rejected` preference-dataset format and can also auto-extract prompts from those columns for an "implicit prompt" dataset, though explicit prompts are recommended [8]. Training scale: the paper's main results use one epoch of ORPO training over UltraFeedback (Llama-2 7B, Mistral 7B) or up to 10 epochs on smaller OPT models (see How to use it) [1]. On-policy or offline: offline - ORPO trains on a fixed, pre-collected preference dataset like DPO, not on samples generated by the current policy during training.

**Extra models**: none. The method's own definition requires no reward model, no value network, and no reference model - the SFT-initialized model being trained is the only model in the loop [1]. This is the paper's central efficiency claim over RLHF and DPO, both of which keep a second, frozen copy of the SFT model on top of the model being tuned [1]. trl's ORPOTrainer loads no separate reference model either; see Cost for what this saves.

**Shipped by**: LLaMA-Factory, as a stable, non-experimental training approach - its README lists ORPO alongside PPO, DPO, and KTO among its integrated methods, states it added ORPO support on 2024-03-31, and shows ORPO training with full-tuning, freeze-tuning, LoRA, QLoRA, OFT, and QOFT all checked in its supported-approaches table [4]. trl also ships it, but only under the experimental namespace: `trl.experimental.orpo.ORPOTrainer` / `ORPOConfig` [8][6]. trl's top-level `trl/__init__.py` does not export `ORPOTrainer` (read at commit `79842b8`, main branch, 2026-08-09) [9]; the trainer is importable only via `from trl.experimental.orpo import ORPOConfig, ORPOTrainer` [8]. trl's own stability contract for that namespace states that "anything under trl.experimental may change or be removed in any release (including patch versions) without prior deprecation" and advises against relying on it "for production workloads" [6]. verl's documentation home page lists its algorithm pages (PPO, GRPO, DAPO, SPPO, GPG, entropy, and others) with no ORPO page among them, so this card treats verl as not shipping it (checked by fetching verl's docs index page, 2026-08-09) [10]. A newcomer choosing a supported, non-experimental implementation should start with LLaMA-Factory [4], not trl's experimental path.

## How it works

The loop in one line: for each preference pair, run one forward pass of the model being trained on the chosen and rejected sequences, compute an SFT loss and an odds-ratio loss from those same logits, and update on their weighted sum - no second model, no separate rollout.

**Odds and odds ratio** [1], Eqs. 3-5. For an input $x$ and output sequence $y$ of length $m$ tokens:

$$ \log P_\theta(y|x) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m} \log P_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) $$

$$ \operatorname{odds}_\theta(y|x) = \frac{P_\theta(y|x)}{1 - P_\theta(y|x)} $$

$\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y|x) = k$ means the model is $k$ times more likely to generate $y$ than not to generate it [1]. The odds ratio of the chosen response $y_w$ over the rejected response $y_l$ is:

$$ \textbf{OR}_\theta(y_w, y_l) = \frac{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_w|x)}{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_l|x)} $$

**The objective** [1], Eq. 6:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{ORPO} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} \left[ \mathcal{L}_{SFT} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{OR} \right] $$

$\mathcal{L}_{SFT}$ is the ordinary causal-LM negative-log-likelihood loss on the chosen response [1]. $\mathcal{L}_{OR}$, Eq. 7, is the log-sigmoid of the log odds ratio, so minimizing it increases the odds ratio of chosen over rejected:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{OR} = -\log \sigma\left(\log \frac{\textbf{OR}_\theta(y_w, y_l)}{1}\right) = -\log \sigma\left(\log \frac{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_w|x)}{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_l|x)}\right) $$

$\lambda$ is the single scalar weight on the odds-ratio term, tuned per model in the paper's own runs (0.1-1.0; see How to use it) [1]; trl's implementation names this same scalar `beta`, and its docstring notes that the paper denotes it $\lambda$ while the code itself refers to it internally as alpha [8] - one quantity, three names across the paper, trl's config, and trl's source comments.

**Gradient** [1], Eq. 8-9 (full derivation in the paper's Appendix A): $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{OR} = \delta(d) \cdot h(d)$ for a pair $d=(x, y_l, y_w)$, where

$$ \delta(d) = \left[1 + \frac{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_w|x)}{\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_l|x)}\right]^{-1}, \qquad h(d) = \frac{\nabla_\theta \log P_\theta(y_w|x)}{1-P_\theta(y_w|x)} - \frac{\nabla_\theta \log P_\theta(y_l|x)}{1-P_\theta(y_l|x)} $$

$\delta(d)$ is a scalar penalty strength that shrinks as the model already favors $y_w$ over $y_l$; $h(d)$ contrasts the chosen and rejected gradients, each rescaled by $1/(1-P_\theta(y|x))$, so a response the model already assigns high probability to gets a proportionally larger penalty gradient when it is the rejected one [1].

**Why odds ratio, not probability ratio** [1], Sec. 7.1, Eq. 16: DPO's and IPO's quantity is the probability ratio $\textbf{PR}_\theta(y_w,y_l) = P_\theta(y_w|x)/P_\theta(y_l|x)$. The paper argues the odds ratio is less extreme in its discrimination against the disfavored response than the probability ratio when the alignment step is fused into SFT, and supports this with a sampled comparison of the two ratios' distributions (paper's Fig. 6) [1]. This is a stability argument, not a claim that the two ratios are the same quantity - they are not [1].

**Worked example.** Suppose for a given pair the model currently assigns $P_\theta(y_w|x)=0.7$ and $P_\theta(y_l|x)=0.3$. Then $\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_w|x) = 0.7/0.3 \approx 2.33$ and $\operatorname{odds}_\theta(y_l|x) = 0.3/0.7 \approx 0.43$, giving $\textbf{OR}_\theta(y_w,y_l) \approx 5.44$ and $\mathcal{L}_{OR} = -\log\sigma(\log 5.44) \approx -\log\sigma(1.69) \approx -\log(0.844) \approx 0.170$. Compare the probability ratio for the same pair: $\textbf{PR}_\theta(y_w,y_l) = 0.7/0.3 \approx 2.33$, noticeably smaller than the odds ratio $5.44$ at this same probability gap - illustrating the paper's point that the odds ratio amplifies the same probability gap less symmetrically than a naive reading suggests, since it grows fastest only as either probability approaches its extreme [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: one forward and backward pass per pair, on the model being trained only - no reference model, no reward model, no value network [1]. The paper's own accounting: RLHF and DPO each keep two copies of $\pi_{SFT}$ in memory (a frozen reference model plus the model being tuned) and compute two forward passes per model per batch, four forward passes total; ORPO updates $\pi_{SFT}$ directly, halving the forward passes per batch relative to RLHF/DPO [1]. A naive reading might expect $\mathcal{L}_{OR}$ itself to need extra passes since it is a second loss term - it does not: both $\mathcal{L}_{SFT}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{OR}$ are computed from the same single forward pass's logits over the chosen and rejected sequences [1].

**In practice, per framework**:

- trl `experimental.orpo.ORPOTrainer` [8]: no reference model is loaded at any `beta` setting, and `ORPOConfig` carries no reference-model argument at all - a structural difference from trl's `DPOTrainer`, whose source only skips loading a reference model when the model is a PEFT model or `precompute_ref_log_probs` is set, with no `beta`-based way to disable it (read at commit `85709ab`, main branch, 2026-08-09) [11]. The default data collator pads chosen/rejected pairs to the batch max length up to `max_length` (default 1024) [8]; the trainer supports PEFT wrapping via `peft_config` for further memory reduction [8]. Because chosen and rejected share one forward pass through the same model, trl's memory footprint here is bounded by one model's activations at the configured batch size, not by a separate rollout.
- verl: not implemented; no cost profile to report (checked via verl's documentation home page and its listed algorithm pages, 2026-08-09; no ORPO page found) [10].

## How to use it

- Data: prompt + chosen response + rejected response per row - the same triple DPO uses, no reward or score column. trl's `ORPOTrainer` accepts either explicit-prompt rows (a separate `prompt` column) or implicit-prompt rows where the trainer extracts the prompt from the shared prefix of `chosen`/`rejected`; explicit prompts are the trainer's own recommendation [8]. The paper filtered out any pair with `chosen == rejected` or either field empty [1].
- Sequence length: the paper truncated/padded to 1,024 tokens for HH-RLHF and 2,048 for UltraFeedback, and filtered out prompts longer than 1,024 tokens [1]; trl's `max_length` default is 1024, and its own community threads flag that prompts longer than `max_length` (or `max_prompt_length`, not `max_length`) produce NaN log-probabilities unless filtered (see While it runs) [12].
- Knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give it; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key):

| knob | trl `ORPOConfig` default [8] | paper [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lambda$ / `beta` (odds-ratio weight) | 0.1 | 0.1-1.0 depending on model: 0.25 (Phi-2), 0.2 (Llama-2 7B), 0.1 (Mistral 7B) |
| learning rate | `1e-6` | `8e-6` (OPT/Phi-2/Llama-2/Mistral runs) |
| `num_train_epochs` | 3.0 | 10 (OPT/Phi-2/Llama-2), 1 (Mistral, single-epoch UltraFeedback run) |
| `max_length` | 1024 | 1,024 tokens (HH-RLHF), 2,048 tokens (UltraFeedback) |
| `disable_dropout` | `True` | not stated |
| optimizer | `adamw_torch_fused` | AdamW / paged AdamW, linear warmup + cosine decay |

  Two readings of this table: trl's default learning rate (`1e-6`) sits an order of magnitude below the paper's own `8e-6` across every model size the paper tuned [1][8] - the paper's own default is the anchor to start from, not trl's. And $\lambda$ in the paper was tuned down as model size grew (0.25 -> 0.2 -> 0.1 for Phi-2 -> Llama-2 -> Mistral) [1], the same direction as the scaling advice given by the paper's first author on trl's issue tracker (see While it runs) [12] - trl's flat default of 0.1 happens to match only the largest model in that progression.
- Trade-off: because $\mathcal{L}_{OR}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{SFT}$ share one forward pass and one epoch budget, there is no separate "batch reuse" or "group size" knob the way there is for GRPO or PPO - the main run-design choice is $\lambda$ itself, trading how hard the odds-ratio term discourages the rejected response against how much the SFT term is allowed to dominate; the paper's own ablation (Appendix E, Mistral 7B) found $\lambda=1.0$ over-adapts to the chosen set, hurting MT-Bench performance on extraction, math, and reasoning categories relative to $\lambda=0.1$, while improving on STEM, humanities, and roleplay categories [1].

## While it runs

- Signals: trl logs `rewards/chosen` and `rewards/rejected` (mean chosen/rejected log-probabilities scaled by `beta`), `rewards/accuracies` (fraction of pairs where chosen reward exceeds rejected), `rewards/margins` (mean chosen-minus-rejected reward gap), `log_odds_chosen` (mean log odds ratio of chosen over rejected), `log_odds_ratio` (mean of $\log\sigma(\text{log\_odds\_chosen})$, i.e. $-\mathcal{L}_{OR}$), and `nll_loss` (the SFT-loss component alone) [8]. trl's own quick-start guidance: "an increasing trend in the reward margin indicates that the model is improving and generating better responses over time" [8].
- Published reference runs: trl maintainer Kashif Rasul posted a working Weights & Biases run for `examples/scripts/orpo.py` on `gpt2` with `learning_rate=1e-3`, `beta` at the config default, in response to a NaN-loss issue - offered as a known-good comparison point for trl's own example script, not the paper's own runs [12].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper's odds ratio is undefined at $P_\theta(y|x) \to 1$ (division by zero in $\operatorname{odds}_\theta$); trl contributor poutyface traced a NaN failure to exactly this edge in the trainer's loss computation, noting that `torch.exp(policy_chosen_logps)` or `torch.exp(policy_rejected_logps)` can reach exactly `1`, at which point `torch.log1p` on the resulting expression returns NaN, and reported that adding a small epsilon before `log1p` fixed it for their run [12]. A second reported cause, from GitHub user conseq2: when a prompt exceeds `max_length` (or `max_prompt_length`), the chosen/rejected log-probabilities both turn to NaN, with the suggested fix to filter out over-length prompts before training [12]. trl's current source (fetched 2026-08-09 from the `main` branch) computes the odds-ratio term with a numerically-stabilized `log1mexp` helper using a `torch.where` branch rather than a direct `torch.log1p(-torch.exp(x))` call, which is the fix these reports were asking for [13].
- Named successors: none found. Searched Google Scholar-style citation context was not available on this endpoint; searched instead by reading trl's own "Experimental" navigation and DAPO/Dr. GRPO/successor-style framing used for GRPO on this corpus - no paper describing itself as fixing a documented ORPO bias was located in the sources fetched for this card.
- Known failure modes: the paper's own limitations note that Mistral-ORPO-$\beta$ (7B) underperforms on coding and math-focused evaluations, which the authors speculate may stem from the small size of the UltraFeedback training set (about 61K instances) relative to what coding/math skills need [1]. From trl's GitHub issue tracker (huggingface/trl#1473, "TRL orpo gives everything Nan," 18 comments, queried 2026-08-09): the paper's first author, Jiwoo Hong, replied directly that larger models generally need a lower learning rate and lower $\beta$/$\lambda$ than smaller ones, citing the `kaist-ai/mistral-orpo-capybara-7k` reproduction script's own settings of learning rate `5e-6` and beta `0.05`, and recommending starting a large-model run at beta `0.1`, learning rate `5e-6` [12]; separately, GitHub user RonanKMcGovern noted in the same thread that trl expects `chosen`/`rejected` columns to already be formatted but not tokenized, to exclude the prompt, and that no BOS/EOS tokens are added automatically - a template-formatting detail, not a loss bug, that produced similar symptoms for at least one reporter in the thread [12].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's central claim is about the SFT stage itself - that a monolithic odds-ratio penalty inside SFT is "sufficient for preference-aligned SFT," letting a single training run reach preference-alignment quality that otherwise needed a separate DPO or RLHF phase [1]; the paper does not claim ORPO adds any capability beyond what SFT-plus-preference-alignment already provides, and its own limitations section documents at least one capability gap (coding/math) attributed to training-data scale, not to the method itself [1].
