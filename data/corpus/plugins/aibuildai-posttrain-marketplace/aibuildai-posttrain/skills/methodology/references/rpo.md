# RPO

DPO with an added SFT term: keep the DPO preference loss, but also push the policy to imitate the dataset's own chosen responses, and that imitation term is what keeps the reward model honest.

**RPO** (Regularized Preference Optimization), https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16436, is an offline preference-optimization method introduced by Liu, Lu, Zhang, Liu, Guo, Yang, Blanchet and Wang to provably mitigate reward overoptimization in RLHF [1]. The paper frames overoptimization as a form of distributional shift and uncertainty in learning human preferences, and derives RPO from a theoretical maximin objective that picks the best policy against an adversarially chosen reward model, one that jointly minimizes the reward model's fitting loss and a penalty on rewards the policy assigns itself relative to a baseline [1]. Its parent is DPO, cited to its own paper: DPO reparameterizes the reward model so the optimal policy is available in closed form, turning RLHF into a single classification-style loss on chosen/rejected pairs without a separate reward model or RL step [2]. RPO keeps DPO's loss term unchanged and adds a second term, weighted by $\eta\beta$, that is exactly the negative log-likelihood (SFT loss) of a "baseline policy"; the paper states the resulting objective is named "Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO)" because the added term "explicitly regularizes the policy to imitate the baseline policy" [1]. Three reasons the paper gives for existing: DPO alone is observed to show a substantial decrease in the probability assigned to the preferred (chosen) responses over training, which the authors read as a symptom of overoptimization [1]; a theoretical proof (Theorem 5.6) shows the abstract maximin/adversarial-regularization objective reduces exactly to this practical DPO+SFT sum, giving the imitation term a formal justification rather than treating it as an ad hoc regularizer [1]; and, like DPO, RPO needs no explicit reward model, so it stays computationally cheaper than reward-based RL methods such as PPO [1].

I confirmed this is the arXiv:2405.16436 paper (not one of the several other RLHF papers that also use the acronym RPO) by fetching the abstract page directly and matching its title, author list, and date against the shortlist row [1]. I found no landmark production system (e.g. a widely used open-weight model's technical report) that names RPO as its training method; the paper's own experiments only reproduce two existing 7B chat models, Zephyr-7b-beta and Zephyr-7b-gemma, retrained with RPO in place of their original DPO stage [1]. A first page of 100 of the paper's 113 citing papers (fetched unsorted from the Semantic Scholar citations API, then sorted locally by citation count) turned up follow-up empirical/theoretical studies rather than system reports; the highest-cited entry in that page, "Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=? On the Overthinking of o1-Like LLMs" (551 citations), is not an RPO adopter [3]. The clearest adoption evidence is that trl documents RPO as a directly reproducible recipe in its paper index, with the paper's own hyperparameters translated into a `DPOConfig` call [4]. In the paper's own results, RPO beat DPO in a pairwise GPT-4 preference evaluation on held-out prompts, winning 56.0% of the time against DPO (beta series) and 54.0% of the time (gemma series) [1] (Table 1), and it raised the AlpacaEval 2.0 length-controlled win rate from 21.15% (DPO, beta) to 23.28% (RPO, beta) [1] (Table 2). Lineage in one line: PPO (2017 [5]) -> DPO (2023 [2]) -> RPO (2024 [1]), reproduced as a config recipe rather than a dedicated trainer by trl [4].

**When to pick it**: offline preference optimization when you already have static chosen/rejected pairs and specifically want a cheap defense against DPO's overoptimization symptom (declining chosen-response likelihood during training) - the paper reports RPO fixes this without extra preference data or a changed training pipeline [1]. It is DPO [2] plus one extra loss term, so pick DPO instead when that overoptimization symptom is not a concern and you want the simpler, one-term loss. Prefer PPO [5] when you can score fresh rollouts with a reward model and want full online RL; RPO, like DPO, is offline and never samples from the policy being trained during training [1][2]. Nearest DPO-family alternative that also fights overoptimization by pinning to a reference distribution: none is named in the paper itself as a direct competitor beyond DPO.

**Variant of**: DPO [2].

**Data it needs**: an offline preference dataset of prompt, chosen-response, rejected-response triples - trl's DPOTrainer accepts this as `{"prompt", "chosen", "rejected"}` fields in standard or conversational format [6], the same schema DPO uses [2]. The paper's own baseline policy is the distribution of preferred (chosen) responses already in the training set, so no separate SFT dataset is needed [1]. RPO's own experiments trained ~7B-parameter chat models on two preference sets: the UltraFeedback dataset (~60K prompts, beta series) and the Argilla-DPO-Mix-7K dataset (gemma series) [1]. Offline: no fresh generation from the policy during training, unlike PPO's per-step rollouts [1][5].

**Extra models**: one frozen reference model, required by the DPO term the same way DPO itself requires one [2] - trl's DPOTrainer creates a copy of the model at the start as the reference if none is passed [6]. No value network and no separate reward model: RPO's own text says "implementing RPO does not require to maintain a reward model" [1]. No extra model is needed for the added imitation term, since its target is the dataset's own chosen response, not a generated sample scored by another model [1]. Cost details below.

**Shipped by**: trl, via `DPOConfig(loss_type=[...], loss_weights=[...])` on the exported `DPOTrainer`/`DPOConfig` pair (both top-level package exports) [7][6] - not a dedicated `RPOTrainer` class, but trl's own paper index gives RPO a named reproduction recipe on `main`; the page states no separate support/maintenance status for this recipe [4]. verl's documentation has no DPO or RPO page at all (its `/algo/dpo.html` returns a 404, and no "dpo" or standalone "rpo" trainer name appears in its docs index) [8][9], so verl does not ship this method; building it there would mean writing a DPO-style pairwise loss (plus one SFT NLL term) on top of verl's actor-training loop rather than its RL rollout loop, since RPO needs no rollout sampling.

## How it works

Each step, on a fixed batch of (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples: compute DPO's pairwise loss between chosen and rejected under the trained policy vs. the frozen reference, and add a weighted NLL loss that scores only the chosen response under the trained policy; backpropagate the sum [1].

**The objective** [1], Eq. 4.8:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPO}}(\theta) := \eta\beta \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d_0,\, a^0\sim \pi^{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot|x)}\big[-\log(\pi_\theta(a^0|x))\big]}_{\text{Imitation (SFT) loss}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left(\beta\cdot\log\frac{\pi_\theta(\cdot|\cdot)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|\cdot)}\right)}_{\text{Preference opt. loss}} $$

$\pi_\theta$ is the policy being trained, $\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}$ the frozen reference policy, $d_0$ the prompt distribution, and $\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$ the "baseline policy" whose samples $a^0$ the imitation term pushes the model to reproduce; $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}$ is DPO's own cross-entropy loss functional applied to the implicit reward $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})$, i.e. the second term is exactly the DPO objective on chosen/rejected pairs [1]. $\beta$ scales the implicit-reward margin as in DPO [2]; $\eta$ scales the whole imitation term, and the paper writes it multiplied by $\beta$ as a single coefficient $\eta\beta$ in front of the SFT loss [1]. In the paper's own experiments the baseline policy $\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$ is set to the distribution of preferred (chosen) responses already present in the training data, so the imitation term becomes the NLL of the chosen response - "the SFT loss in the RPO objective explicitly regularizes the LLM to imitate the preferred responses. We choose this baseline policy in our experiments" [1]. Under that choice, the gradient decomposes cleanly, Eq. 4.9 [1]:

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPO}}(\theta) = \eta\beta \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d_0,\,a^0\sim\pi^{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot|x)}\big[-\nabla_\theta \log(\pi_\theta(a^0|x))\big]}_{\text{increase the alignment with the reference policy}} \;+\; \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)}_{\text{decrease the DPO loss}} $$

A worked micro-example with $\eta=0.005$, $\beta=0.01$ (the paper's own beta-series values [1]): for one training pair, suppose the DPO term's gradient contribution has some magnitude $g_{\mathrm{DPO}}$, and the chosen-response NLL gradient has magnitude $g_{\mathrm{SFT}}$; the total gradient is $g_{\mathrm{DPO}} + (0.005)(0.01)\, g_{\mathrm{SFT}} = g_{\mathrm{DPO}} + 5\times10^{-5}\, g_{\mathrm{SFT}}$ - at these paper-chosen defaults the imitation term is a small correction on top of the DPO gradient, not a comparable-magnitude second objective. The paper proves this practical objective is the exact reformulation of an abstract adversarial (maximin) objective under an equivalence result (Theorem 5.6, connecting the maximin form Eq. 3.3 to a minimax form via the reward-policy correspondence) [1]; it does not define any other practical variant of the loss itself, only the choice of $\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$, which the paper says "can also be flexibly adapted in practice, e.g., the preferred response in the dataset, or the responses of the initial model," while stating it uses the preferred-response choice throughout its own experiments [1]. trl ships exactly the chosen-response NLL choice: its `"sft"` loss type computes cross-entropy only on the chosen half of the batch [10], matching the paper's own experimental choice, not an alternative.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: one forward/backward pass per training pair over the trained policy for the DPO term (chosen and rejected sequences), plus one reference-model forward pass (no gradients) - identical to DPO's own cost [2]. The added imitation term reuses the same chosen-response forward pass already computed for the DPO term, so RPO's own formula adds no new forward pass beyond DPO's; it adds one loss computation (a cross-entropy over the chosen tokens) on activations DPO already produces.
- Memory: same as DPO - one trained policy plus one frozen reference model held in memory, no value network and no reward model [1][2]. RPO's math introduces no third model.
- A naive reading of the two-term sum might suggest RPO needs an extra generation step to draw $a^0\sim\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$; the paper's own experimental choice of $\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$ (the dataset's chosen response) removes that, since $a^0$ is then a fixed dataset field, not a sample requiring inference [1].

**In practice, per framework:**

- trl: implemented by combining `loss_type=["sigmoid", "sft"]` with `loss_weights=[1.0, alpha]` in `DPOConfig`, not by a dedicated class [4][7]. The reference model is created automatically as a copy of the initial policy unless one is passed explicitly, or `precompute_ref_log_probs=True` is set to save memory by precomputing reference log-probabilities once instead of holding the reference model resident during training [6]. trl previously exposed this composition through a single `rpo_alpha` scalar on `DPOTrainer`; that parameter is deprecated with a warning stating it "is equivalent to including `'sft'` in `loss_type`" and is fully removed as of trl's latest tagged release (`v1.9.2`, confirmed by a zero-hit grep for `rpo_alpha` in that tag's `dpo_config.py` and `dpo_trainer.py`, fetched 2026-08-09) [11].
- verl ships no DPO or RPO trainer at all, so there is no framework-specific cost entry for it; a verl-based implementation is unbuilt (see Shipped by) [8][9].

## How to use it

- Data: prompt/chosen/rejected triples, same schema as DPO [2][6]; no separate SFT split is required if you follow the paper's own baseline-policy choice, since the imitation target is just the chosen field already present in the preference data [1].
- Reward/label convention: no learned reward score anywhere in the loss; "reward" only enters as the implicit DPO reward $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})$ inside $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}$, exactly as in DPO [1][2].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value):

| knob | trl default (`DPOConfig`) [7] | trl RPO recipe (paper index) [4] | paper, beta series [1] | paper, gemma series [1] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `loss_type` | `["sigmoid"]` (DPO only, RPO term off) | `["sigmoid", "sft"]` | DPO term + chosen-NLL term | DPO term + chosen-NLL term |
| SFT/imitation weight† | not applicable (term absent) | `loss_weights[1]` = `0.005` | $\eta = 0.005$ (selected by validation loss, combined with $\beta$ below) [1] | $\eta = 0.005$ |
| $\beta$ | `0.1` | `0.01` | `0.01` | `0.05` |
| learning rate | not stated | `5e-7` | `5.0e-7` | `5.0e-7` |
| training epochs | not stated | `1` | `1` | `2` |
| batch size | not stated | not stated | `128` (grad. accumulation 2, per-device 8) | `128` (grad. accumulation 16, per-device 1) |

  † The paper's and trl's SFT/imitation weights are not the same quantity: the paper's loss adds the chosen-response NLL scaled by the product $\eta\beta$ (Eq. 4.8) [1], so at $\eta=0.005$ the beta-series term is actually multiplied by $\eta\beta = 0.005 \times 0.01 = 0.00005$ inside the loss. trl's `"sft"` loss term instead multiplies the raw per-sequence NLL by `loss_weights[1]` alone, with no separate $\beta$ factor applied (`loss += per_sequence_loss.mean() * loss_weight`, trl `dpo_trainer.py`, read at the `main` branch HEAD fetched 2026-08-09) [10]. Setting `loss_weights[1] = 0.005` therefore reproduces $\eta$ but applies roughly 100x the paper's own effective beta-series coefficient ($0.005 / 0.00005 = 100$); matching the paper's Eq. 4.8 exactly requires setting `loss_weights[1] = 0.00005` (beta series) or `loss_weights[1] = 0.00025` (gemma series, $\eta\beta = 0.005 \times 0.05$).

  Two readings of that table: trl's plain default (`beta=0.1`, RPO term off) is not a usable RPO starting point - the paper's own $\beta$ values are an order of magnitude smaller (0.01-0.05), and the SFT term must be added explicitly via `loss_type`/`loss_weights` since it is absent by default [7][1]; and the paper's own $\beta$ differs between its two model series (0.01 vs. 0.05) while $\eta$ stays fixed at 0.005, so $\beta$ - not $\eta$ - is the knob the paper retunes per base model [1].
- Trade-off: raising the SFT weight pulls the model harder toward the literal chosen responses at the expense of the DPO margin signal; the paper selected its weight by validation loss on a held-out split rather than by a fixed rule [1], so treat 0.005 as a starting point tuned for its own two model/dataset pairs, not a universal default.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's central diagnostic is the log-probability the model assigns to chosen responses over training - it reports DPO alone shows "a significant and continual decrease in log probability on chosen responses... during training" which it treats as the signature of overoptimization, while RPO's log-probability on chosen responses is "notably higher than that given by the DPO-trained model" over the same training run (Figures 1 and 6 in the paper) [1]. In trl, the same quantity is logged directly as `logps/chosen` (and `logps/rejected`), alongside `rewards/chosen`, `rewards/margins`, and `rewards/accuracies` (the implicit DPO reward and its margin/accuracy across chosen vs. rejected) [12] - watching `logps/chosen` stay flat or rise, rather than falling as under plain DPO, is the RPO-specific check the paper's own analysis motivates.
- Published reference runs: the paper's own Table 1 pairwise GPT-4 win rates give a directly reusable known-good comparison point - RPO beat DPO 56.0% to 44.0% (beta series) and 54.0% to 46.0% (gemma series) on 150 held-out test-split prompts [1]; Table 2 gives absolute benchmark numbers (RPO-beta: MT-Bench 7.381, AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate 23.28%; RPO-gemma: MT-Bench 7.916, LC win rate 15.51%) against DPO-trained and officially released Zephyr baselines [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: with the SFT term absent (trl's plain default, `loss_type=["sigmoid"]` only), the run is plain DPO and gets none of RPO's chosen-likelihood protection - the term must be added explicitly [7]. The paper picks its baseline policy $\pi^{\mathrm{base}}$ as the dataset's own chosen responses in every experiment, so if a different baseline (e.g. self-generated responses) is substituted, the imitation term's effect is unverified by the paper's own reported results [1].
- Named successors: none found in the 15 highest-cited entries of one 100-of-113-paper page from the Semantic Scholar citations API for arXiv:2405.16436 (fetched 2026-08-09, unsorted by the API itself, sorted locally by citation count after the fact); the remaining ~13 citing papers were not fetched, so this check does not cover the full citing set. None of the 15 examined names itself as a direct successor that fixes a documented RPO bias, in the way DAPO or Dr. GRPO explicitly correct GRPO [3].
- Known failure modes: the paper's own conclusion section states future work as extending the theoretical algorithm to iterative RLHF with fresh data collection, not a documented weakness of the current method [1]; no dedicated limitations section was found in the fetched full text. trl's closed GitHub issues for `rpo_alpha` (11 results, repo `huggingface/trl`, fetched 2026-08-09) include a maintainer-acknowledged numerical trap: issue #1962 and its fix PR #1961 report that with `precompute_ref_log_probs=True`, `DPOTrainer`'s guard `self.args.rpo_alpha is not None` skipped loading the precomputed `reference_chosen_logps`/`reference_rejected_logps` from the batch whenever `rpo_alpha` was left at its default `None`, leaving the training loss stuck at ~0.6931-0.6932 (i.e. the reference term silently drops out and the model does not train); the fix (merged 2024-08-25) changed the guard to no longer depend on `rpo_alpha` [11].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own claim is narrow and empirical - RPO "consistently achieves better alignment performance than DPO in in-data distribution" on its own held-out test split, and "can also achieve consistently better performance" on MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0, which it frames as evidence of better generalization from mitigating overoptimization, not as a change to what the base model can do [1]. The paper does not report any capability benchmark (e.g. reasoning or math accuracy) improving under RPO.
