# SeqKD

Run beam search with a trained teacher model over the training set to build a new target dataset, then train a smaller student with ordinary supervised cross-entropy on the teacher's own outputs instead of the human references.

**SeqKD** (Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation) is a model-compression method for sequence-to-sequence models, introduced for neural machine translation (NMT) by Kim and Rush [1]. The paper describes the resulting procedure as one where "the student network is trained on a newly generated dataset that is the result of running beam search with the teacher network" [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07947 [1]. Its parent is the general knowledge-distillation family formalized by Hinton et al., which trains a smaller model to reproduce a larger model's output distribution instead of the raw training labels [2]; the SeqKD paper itself also defines a word-level variant (Word-KD) that applies this idea directly to per-token distributions, and treats SeqKD as the sequence-level counterpart [1]. Mechanistically, SeqKD approximates the (intractable) teacher sequence distribution by its single highest-scoring beam-search output for each source sentence, then minimizes ordinary negative log-likelihood of the student on that generated target [1]. The paper gives three reasons for going beyond word-level KD: wrong per-word predictions compound at test time in NMT, so matching only local word distributions under-transfers sequence-level behavior; exact sequence-level KD requires summing over an exponential space of output sequences, which the mode approximation sidesteps; and the resulting student distribution turns out to be far more peaked around its own greedy output, which the paper links to no longer needing beam search at inference [1].

SeqKD was adopted as the training-data construction step for non-autoregressive NMT: Gu et al. apply sequence-level knowledge distillation from Kim and Rush to build the parallel data their non-autoregressive Transformer trains on, because raw parallel data is too multimodal for a non-autoregressive decoder [3]. Shleifer and Rush's DistilBART paper implements the same technique as their "Pseudo-labels" method, explicitly crediting "Kim and Rush (2016)" and naming it "Sequence-level Knowledge Distillation" in contrast to word/logit-level KD [4]. In the originating paper, distilling a 4x1000-LSTM English-to-German teacher (17.7/19.5 BLEU, greedy/beam-5) into a 2x500-LSTM student with SeqKD alone reached 18.9/19.0 BLEU, a +4.2/+1.4 BLEU gain over a student trained without distillation, and, in the paper's separate pruning experiment, taking their best English-German student (2x500, trained with Seq-KD combined with Seq-Inter) and pruning 80% of its weights gave a model with 13x fewer parameters than the teacher at a cost of only 0.4 BLEU (Table 3) [1]. Lineage: word-level KD (Hinton et al., 2015 [2]) -> SeqKD (Kim and Rush, 2016 [1]) -> adopted for non-autoregressive NMT (Gu et al., 2018 [3]) and DistilBART (Shleifer and Rush, 2020 [4]) -> generalized/named successor GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024 [5]), which benchmarks directly against "SeqKD" by name and reports on-policy GKD's relative gains over baseline KD methods (2.1x/1.7x/1.9x, see When to pick it) [5]. The paper was confirmed by an exact-title arXiv search on "Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation" (arXiv:1606.07947), and independently cross-checked against GKD's own reference-list entry for "Yoon Kim and Alexander M Rush. Sequence-level knowledge distillation" [5].

**When to pick it**: pick SeqKD when a trained teacher is available and the goal is a lightweight, one-pass compression recipe: run the teacher's beam search once over the training source data, then fine-tune a smaller student with plain cross-entropy on the generated targets, with no reference model and no per-step teacher calls at student-training time [1]. Its parent, word-level KD (Hinton et al. [2]), matches token-level distributions directly and needs no beam-search dataset-generation pass, but the SeqKD paper's own comparison shows it transferring less improvement on this NMT setup (Word-KD +0.7 vs. Seq-KD +4.2 BLEU greedy, 2x500 English-German student) [1]. The nearest offline alternative is the same paper's Seq-Inter, which instead picks the beam-search candidate closest by BLEU to the gold reference, interpolating teacher-generated data with the true target instead of discarding it [1]. The nearest online neighbor is GKD [5]: it generates from the student itself (not the teacher) during training and gets per-token feedback from the teacher on those self-generated sequences, and, averaged across T5 student sizes, GKD's own experiments report relative gains over the initial student of 2.1x on summarization, 1.7x on machine translation, and 1.9x on arithmetic reasoning, versus the gains from baseline KD methods including SeqKD [5].

**Variant of**: knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) [2], specialized from word-level to sequence-level targets by the SeqKD paper itself [1].

**Data it needs**: parallel (source, target) sequence pairs to run the teacher's beam search over; the student's SeqKD loss trains only against the teacher-generated targets, with no gold labels required at student-training time (mixing in gold data is the separate Seq-Inter variant) [1]. Paper's own scale: WMT 2014 English-German with about 4M training sentence pairs and a 50k-word vocabulary, and IWSLT 2015 Thai-English with 90k training sentences and a 25k-word vocabulary [1]. As defined by the paper, SeqKD is offline: the teacher-generated dataset is built once via beam search before student training begins, and student training is standard supervised learning over that fixed dataset, not fresh sampling from a live policy each step [1]. This offline separation is not preserved by trl's implementation; see Extra models and Cost.

**Extra models**: as defined by the paper, the teacher is needed only to build the distilled dataset (beam-search forward passes) and can be discarded before student training starts; no value network, reference model, or reward model is used, and the training loss is plain cross-entropy [1]. trl's `GKDTrainer` with `seq_kd=True` does not preserve that separation: it keeps the teacher model loaded for the full training run and calls it every step the seq_kd branch triggers, and its loss compares student and teacher logits with a Jensen-Shannon divergence rather than plain cross-entropy on hard targets [6][7]. Details in Cost.

**Shipped by**: trl, but only under the experimental namespace `trl.experimental.gkd.GKDTrainer` with `GKDConfig(seq_kd=True, lmbda=0.0)` [6]; `GKDConfig` and `GKDTrainer` are exported from `trl/experimental/gkd/__init__.py` (`main` branch, commit `c3e240a`), not from the top-level `trl` package (`trl/__init__.py` does not list either name, `main` branch, commit `ef0a079`) [8][9]. trl's docs state that "the trl.experimental namespace provides a minimal, clearly separated space for fast iteration on new ideas" and that "anything under trl.experimental may change or be removed in any release (including patch versions) without prior deprecation," adding "do not rely on these APIs for production workloads" [10]. trl's separate, non-experimental `DistillationTrainer` implements only the on-policy GKD variant (student self-generates) and has no `seq_kd`-equivalent teacher-generation mode [11]. verl's on-policy distillation documentation (its `algo/opd.html` page and its async on-policy distillation recipe) describes only students sampling their own rollouts and matching a teacher's next-token log-probabilities on those self-generated sequences, with no mention of a fixed beam-search-generated dataset or of Kim and Rush's method by name [14][15]; no framework was found shipping the paper's own offline pipeline (build a static beam-search dataset once, then run ordinary `SFTTrainer`-style training on it). Building that offline pipeline requires no new loss, only a teacher beam-search decoding script plus a standard supervised fine-tuning run.

## How it works

The loop in one line: for each source sentence, take the teacher's beam-search top hypothesis as the new target, then minimize the student's cross-entropy on that generated target [1].

**Sequence-level objective.** The paper starts from the exact (intractable) sequence-level distillation loss, which sums the teacher's sequence distribution $q(t\mid s)$ over the entire space $\mathcal{T}$ of possible target sequences [1]:

$$ L_{SEQ\text{-}KD} = -\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} q(t \mid s)\, \log p(t \mid s) $$

Because this sum is exponential in the target vocabulary and length, the paper approximates $q(t\mid s)$ by its mode, found via beam search, giving the loss actually used [1]:

$$ q(t \mid s) \approx \mathbb{1}\{t = \hat y\}, \qquad L_{SEQ\text{-}KD} \approx -\log p(t = \hat y \mid s) $$

where $\hat y$ is the highest-scoring output of running beam search with the teacher model over source $s$, and $p$ is the student's distribution [1]. This is the paper's `LWORD-NLL`-style cross-entropy applied to a new dataset: `Step (3) is identical to the word-level NLL process except now on the newly-generated data set` [1]. The paper used beam size $K=5$ for building the SeqKD training data, reporting no improvement from a larger beam [1].

**Worked example.** The paper reports directly measurable numbers for this approximation's quality: with beam size 1, the teacher's mode $\hat y$ accounts for only 1.3% of the teacher's total probability mass on average for English-to-German, and 2.3% for Thai-to-English (their Table 1, column `p(t=ŷ)`, teacher row) [1]. After training a 2x500 English-German student on SeqKD data, the student's own mode captures on average 16.9% of its total probability mass (versus 0.9% for the equivalent student trained without distillation), which the paper reads as the student concentrating its distribution around a single easy-to-find mode rather than spreading mass across many plausible translations [1].

**Related variants defined in the same paper.** Word-level KD (`Word-KD`) instead matches the student's per-token distribution to the teacher's per-token distribution via cross-entropy at every position, optionally mixed with the standard NLL loss by a factor $\alpha$ (the paper tested $\alpha \in \{0.5, 0.9\}$ and found $0.5$ better) [1]. Sequence-level interpolation (`Seq-Inter`) replaces $\hat y$ with $\tilde y \approx \arg\max_{t \in T_K} \mathrm{sim}(t, y)$, the beam-search candidate (beam size $K=35$) closest by smoothed sentence-level BLEU to the true target $y$, and can be combined with SeqKD or Word-KD [1]. Of these, trl's experimental `GKDTrainer` ships only a `seq_kd` flag that follows the SeqKD idea of training on teacher-generated sequences; it does not implement Word-KD or Seq-Inter [6].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time, as designed: SeqKD's dataset construction requires one full beam-search decoding pass over the entire training set with the teacher (beam size $K=5$ in the paper), performed once before student training starts; student training itself is then ordinary supervised cross-entropy, requiring no teacher forward pass at all [1]. Beam-search run-time grows linearly with beam size, which is why the paper reports large speedups from the student's ability to use greedy (beam-1) decoding after SeqKD training [1].
- Memory, as designed: only the student model needs to be held during the training loop (weights, gradients, optimizer state); the teacher is needed only during the separate, earlier data-generation phase and can be freed afterward [1].
- Naive reading to flag: because "distillation" methods elsewhere in this corpus (e.g., KL-regularized RL) keep a teacher/reference model resident throughout training, a reader might assume SeqKD does too. The paper's own design decouples generation from training so the teacher is off the critical path once the dataset is built; the practice layer below shows this decoupling is not what trl's implementation does.

**In practice, per framework:**

- trl `GKDTrainer` (`seq_kd=True`, experimental; source read at `main` branch commit `671cc0b`) [6][7]: the teacher model is loaded for the entire run, not just a one-off preprocessing pass, and `training_step` calls the teacher's `generate` on every batch where the seq_kd branch is taken (with probability $1-\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is `lmbda`) [7]. Teacher generation uses `model.generate` with `do_sample=True, top_k=0`, temperature set by `GKDConfig.temperature` (default `0.9`) — sampling, not the paper's beam search [6][7]. The training loss is `generalized_jsd_loss` between student and teacher logits (a forward/reverse-KL interpolation controlled by `beta`, default `0.5`), not the paper's plain cross-entropy on a fixed hard target [6][7]. A maintainer-filed and merged fix (PR #5726, merged 2026-05-07) documents that before this fix, `GKDTrainer.training_step` always ran the costly teacher generation first and then silently overwrote it whenever the on-policy (student-generation) branch was also taken, wasting up to 100% of the teacher's generation compute at `lmbda=1.0` and about 50% at the default `lmbda=0.5`; the current code checks the on-policy branch first and only falls through to teacher generation (`seq_kd`) when it is skipped [12]. Net effect versus the paper's design: both models are resident and both are called on every relevant step, so per-step cost includes a teacher generation pass, a teacher logits pass, and a student forward/backward pass, rather than a single one-time teacher pass followed by teacher-free student training [6][7].
- No other framework was found shipping a `seq_kd`-equivalent trainer. verl's distillation pages describe only on-policy distillation — the student samples its own rollouts and the teacher scores them, with no fixed beam-search-generated dataset [14][15] — so no verl cost entry is given here; that architecture is a different offline/on-policy split from SeqKD's, not a SeqKD implementation to cost out.

## How to use it

- Data prep, per the paper: gather parallel (source, target) pairs, run the teacher's beam search (K=5) over every source sentence in the training set, and save the resulting hypothesis as the new target; this replaces (or, for Seq-Inter, is blended with) the original target for that example [1]. Vocabulary was capped at 50k (English-German) and 25k (Thai-English) word types in the paper's experiments, with the rest replaced by an UNK token [1].
- Data prep, per trl `seq_kd`: pass a dataset of `messages`/prompts; the teacher generates a completion for each prompt via `generate_on_policy_outputs`, which masks the prompt positions with `-100` in the labels and truncates at the first EOS token, matching a standard prompt-masked SFT labeling convention [7].
- Reward/label convention: none — SeqKD needs no reward model or scalar reward; the paper's label is simply the teacher's beam-search output token sequence, trained with standard token-level cross-entropy [1]. trl's `seq_kd` path instead uses the JSD-divergence label convention shared with the rest of `GKDTrainer` (see How it works) [6].
- Key knobs, each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value):

| knob | paper [1] | trl `GKDConfig` (seq_kd path) [6] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| teacher decoding for target generation | beam search, beam size $K=5$ | sampling: `do_sample=True`, `top_k=0`, `temperature` 0.9 |
| training loss on generated targets | cross-entropy / NLL | generalized JSD, `beta` 0.5 (interpolates forward/reverse KL) |
| when generation happens | once, offline, before student training | every training step where the seq_kd branch fires (probability $1-\lambda$) |
| student/teacher generation mix | not applicable (pure SeqKD uses teacher targets only) | `lmbda` (default 0.5) trades off student on-policy generation vs. teacher `seq_kd` generation per batch |
| max generated length | not stated | `max_new_tokens` 128 |
| interpolation with gold data | separate Seq-Inter variant, via mixture weight $\alpha$ | not applicable to `seq_kd` path |

  The paper's own values sit far from trl's: the defining choice of decoding (beam search vs. sampling) and of loss (hard-target cross-entropy vs. soft JSD) both differ, so a trl `seq_kd=True` run is not a drop-in reproduction of the paper's numbers in Table 1 [1][6].
- Trade-off a run designer faces: under trl, raising `lmbda` toward 1.0 shifts more batches to student self-generation (on-policy GKD) and away from the `seq_kd` teacher-generation branch entirely, so `seq_kd=True` only has an effect when `lmbda<1.0`, and the two branches are mutually exclusive per batch rather than combined [7].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes, from the paper's own analysis: the fraction of total teacher/student probability mass captured by the greedy/mode output, $p(t=\hat y \mid s)$, rises sharply after SeqKD training (0.9% to 16.9% for the 2x500 English-German student) and the paper interprets this rise as evidence the student's distribution has become peaked enough that beam search is no longer needed at inference [1]. No paper-defined or framework-defined logging name for this quantity was found; a practitioner has to compute it manually.
- A documented degeneracy: perplexity is not a reliable proxy for output quality under SeqKD. The paper reports that "the perplexity of the baseline 2x500 English-German model is 8.2 while the perplexity of the corresponding Seq-KD model is 22.7, despite the fact that Seq-KD model does significantly better for both greedy (+4.2 BLEU) and beam search (+1.4 BLEU) decoding" [1] — a run whose perplexity worsens after SeqKD training can still be a correct, improved run.
- Published reference run: the paper's own Table 1, English-German WMT 2014 newstest2014, 2x500 student: baseline (no distillation) 14.7 BLEU greedy / 17.6 BLEU beam-5; Seq-KD 18.9 / 19.0; teacher (4x1000, no distillation) 17.7 / 19.5 [1]. Table 3 gives the pruning reference curve for the 2x500 Seq-KD+Seq-Inter student: 0% pruned, 84M params, 19.3 BLEU; 80% pruned, 17M params (13x fewer than the 221M-parameter teacher), 19.1 BLEU; 90% pruned, 8M params (26x fewer), 18.5 BLEU [1].
- Known trl-specific numerical trap, from a merged maintainer fix: before PR #5726 (merged 2026-05-07), `GKDTrainer.training_step` ran the teacher's generation for the `seq_kd` branch unconditionally and then discarded it whenever the on-policy student-generation branch was also selected for that step, wasting up to 100% of the `seq_kd` teacher compute at `lmbda=1.0` and roughly 50% at the trl default `lmbda=0.5`; current trl code checks the on-policy branch first so `seq_kd` generation only runs when it will actually be used [12]. Anyone reproducing older trl results with `seq_kd=True` should check which side of this fix their installed version is on.
- Named successor: GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024) fixes the train/inference distribution mismatch that offline methods like SeqKD have — the student never sees its own generation errors during SeqKD training, only the teacher's beam-search outputs — by training the student on its self-generated sequences with teacher feedback instead [5].
- Known failure modes: no section titled "Limitations" was found in the paper (checked via full-text search of the fetched PDF text for "limitation"); the closest first-hand caveat is the perplexity/BLEU divergence noted above, which the paper frames as a finding rather than a limitation [1]. No maintainer-reported failure mode specific to `seq_kd` (beyond the wasted-compute issue above) was found in a GitHub search of `huggingface/trl` issues and PRs for "seq_kd" (15 results returned, reviewed by title) [13].
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's stated motivation and result are about model size and inference speed at roughly matched translation quality (student BLEU close to or exceeding an undistilled student, at 10x faster greedy decoding and up to 13x fewer parameters with pruning) [1]; the paper does not claim SeqKD makes the student capable of translations the teacher itself could not produce, since the student is trained to reproduce the teacher's own beam-search mode.
