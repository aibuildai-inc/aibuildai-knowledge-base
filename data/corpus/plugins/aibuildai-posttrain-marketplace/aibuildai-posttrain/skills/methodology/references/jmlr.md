# JMLR

Fine-tune a retriever and an LLM together on the same QA loss: the retriever's ColBERT scores are pushed up or down by how much each candidate document actually lowers the LLM's loss on the answer, instead of freezing the retriever the way RAG does.

**JMLR** (Joint Medical LLM and Retrieval Training) is a supervised fine-tuning method for building a retrieval-augmented medical QA model, introduced in "JMLR: Joint Medical LLM and Retrieval Training for Enhancing Reasoning and Professional Question Answering Capability" [1]. The paper's own framing: unlike previous RAG methods where the retrieval model was trained separately from the LLM, JMLR jointly trains the LLM and information retrieval during the fine-tuning phase [1]. Its parent is RAG, a fine-tuning recipe that combines a pre-trained parametric seq2seq model with a non-parametric memory - a dense Wikipedia vector index accessed through a pre-trained neural retriever - for language generation [2]. JMLR keeps RAG's retrieve-then-generate shape but couples the two halves of training: it initializes the retriever from ColBERT [1], a BERT-based retriever that independently encodes the query and document with BERT and then applies a late-interaction step to model their fine-grained similarity, rather than scoring the pair with one combined pass through the network [3] (JMLR's own restatement of this scoring step, discussed under How it works, gives the exact sum-of-maximum-similarity formula [1]), and adds a rank loss $L_{rank}$ that rewards a document for lowering the LLM's answer loss more than a competing document does [1]. The paper gives two motivations beyond accuracy: freezing the retriever means it has no signal for whether a retrieved document is actually useful to the LLM, only whether it is topically relevant [1]; and synchronous joint training reduces the compute needed to reach a given knowledge level compared to the conventional pretrain-then-finetune pipeline used by domain LLMs such as Meditron [1].

Confirmation: this is a single-candidate title match against the exact paper title, with no other paper of that name found [source: arXiv id 2402.17887 abstract page, matches the shortlist row]. The paper reports no landmark downstream system adopting JMLR; of the 62 citing papers recorded by Semantic Scholar, the highest-cited citers are large surveys of LLMs in healthcare and medicine (343, 241, and 209 citations respectively) that list JMLR as one example of joint retriever-LLM training, not a system that adopted it [4]. In the paper's own results, JMLR-13B reached 70.5% average accuracy across four medical QA benchmarks versus 68.9% for the pretrain-finetune baseline Meditron-70B and 67.7% for a frozen-retriever RAG-13B baseline, while training in 148 GPU-hours against Meditron-70B's 42,630 GPU-hours (Table 2, Fig. 1) [1]. Lineage in one line: RAG (2020 [2]) + ColBERT (2020 [3]) -> JMLR (2024 [1]); the paper names no successor method, and none was found in the citation search above [4].

**When to pick it**: a domain QA task with a fixed document corpus, when you can afford to fine-tune both a retriever and an LLM together and want the retriever's document choices measured by whether they lower the LLM's loss, not by topical similarity alone [1]. Prefer plain RAG [2] when retriever and generator are trained (or used) independently, which is cheaper to set up and is what the paper's own RAG-7B/RAG-13B baselines do [1]. The paper's related work groups RA-DIT [5], REALM, and REPLUG together as systems usually trained independently from the LLM, contrasting all three with JMLR's synchronous joint training [1]; the paper does not benchmark against any of the three directly. The paper's own ablation reproduces an asynchronous variant, JMLR-7B-Separate, which alternately freezes the retriever and the LLM instead of updating both every step [1]; JMLR is only worth the added coupling over that asynchronous alternative if the gap in Table 5 (below) matters for the task.

**Variant of**: RAG [2], with the retriever initialized from and structured as ColBERT [3].

**Data it needs**: QA pairs, each with a gold answer choice and a rationale text, plus a background document corpus the retriever searches over [1]. Training and evaluation used four medical QA datasets - MedQA (USMLE-style questions), the Amboss question bank, MedMCQA, and the medical subset of MMLU - against a corpus built from medical textbooks, Wikidoc, and clinical guidelines (exact per-source document/article counts are given in the paper's Table 1, not reproduced here as this card did not verify every row) [1]. The method is on-policy with respect to the retriever only in the sense that document choices are resampled every training iteration by weighted random sampling over the top 30 retriever-ranked documents per question, with higher-scored documents more likely to be selected, so the LLM sees a different subset of documents each pass [1]; the underlying document corpus and QA pairs themselves are a fixed offline dataset, not freshly generated.

**Extra models**: a retriever (ColBERT-initialized [1]) is trained jointly with the LLM - this is the method's defining extra model, not optional. No separate reward model or judge is used for training; GPT-4 and human doctors are used only for offline evaluation of rationale quality, not as part of the training loop [1]. No frozen reference-policy model is used, unlike KL-regularized RL methods - JMLR's training is supervised fine-tuning on QA pairs, not policy-gradient RL. Compute detail in Cost.

**Shipped by**: no packaged training library implements JMLR - it is not available as an importable trainer in trl or verl. The paper's authors publish a standalone reference implementation at `github.com/believewhat/JMLR-Joint-Medical-LLM-and-Retrieval-Training` [6], consisting of separate scripts (`IRTrainer.py`, `supervised-fine-tune2.py`, `pre_ir.py`, `inference_ir.py`, `llama_ir2.py`, `llama_attn_replace.py`) rather than a pip-installable package with a top-level export; as of the repository's last push (2025-01-31) it has 8 stars, 1 fork, and no open issues [6]. Building this in a framework like trl or verl would mean writing a new joint training loop that alternates or fuses gradient updates across a retriever and an LLM through the shared rank loss below - not a loss function you can drop into an existing single-model SFT or RL trainer.

## How it works

Each training step: retrieve the top-k documents for a question, feed the sampled subset to the LLM to generate an answer, compute the LLM's answer loss, and use the *change* in that loss across candidate documents to update the retriever's ranking, while also updating the LLM on the answer loss itself [1].

**Formulation.** The paper poses joint training as a single optimization over the LLM parameters $\theta$ and the retriever parameters $\phi$ [1]:

$$ (\theta^*, \phi^*) = \arg\min_{\theta,\phi} \; \mathbb{E}_{(Q,A)\sim T} \left[ L\big(f_A(Q, f_R(Q;\phi), \theta),\, A\big) \right] $$

where $f_A$ is the answer function (the LLM, taking a question and retrieved documents), $f_R(Q;\phi) = \arg\top_{D\subseteq\mathcal{D}, |D|=k}\, rel(Q,D;\phi)$ selects the top-$k$ scoring documents by the retriever's relevance function $rel$, and $L$ is a loss over both answer correctness and document relevance [1]. Gradients are taken jointly, $\nabla_{\theta,\phi} \propto \nabla_{\theta,\phi} L(f_A(Q, f_R(Q;\phi),\theta), A)$ [1].

**ColBERT scoring.** The retriever embeds a query and a document independently through a shared BERT encoder followed by a CNN, then scores a pair by late interaction, per JMLR's own restatement of the ColBERT scoring function [1]:

$$ E_q := \mathrm{CNN}(\mathrm{BERT}([Q]\,q_0 q_1 \dots q_l)), \qquad E_d := \mathrm{Filter}(\mathrm{CNN}(\mathrm{BERT}([D]\,d_0 d_1 \dots d_n))) $$

$$ S_{q,d} := \sum_{i \in [|E_q|]} \max_{j \in [|E_d|]} E_{q_i} \cdot E_{d_j}^{T} $$

**The rank loss.** $L$ is split into the LLM's own answer loss plus a rank loss $L_{rank}$ that trains the retriever [1]:

$$ L = L_{rank} + L\big(f_A(Q, f_R(Q;\phi)), \theta, A\big), \qquad L_{rank} := -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \mathbb{I}(L_i \neq 0) \cdot F_i $$

$$ F_i = \mathbb{I}(L_i)\log(C_i) + (1-\mathbb{I}(L_i))\log(1-C_i), \qquad C_i = \sigma(\hat S_{q_i,d_k}) - \sigma(\hat S_{q_i,d_j}) $$

$$ L_i = \hat L_i(y, y'(x,d_j)) - \hat L_j(y, y'(x,d_k)) $$

Here $M$ is the number of queries in the batch, $\hat S$ is the retriever's normalized document score, and $\hat L_i$/$\hat L_j$ are the LLM's answer losses when document $d_j$ or $d_k$ is supplied [1]. In words: for a pair of candidate documents $d_j, d_k$ for the same question, $L_i$ is positive when $d_j$ lowered the LLM's loss more than $d_k$ did; $C_i$ is the retriever's own predicted score gap between the two documents, squashed to $(0,1)$; $F_i$ is a binary cross-entropy term that pushes the retriever's score gap to agree in sign with the LLM's loss-reduction gap; and the $\mathbb{I}(L_i \neq 0)$ indicator drops pairs where the two documents helped the LLM equally, since there is no ranking signal to learn from them [1]. Worked example: if document $d_j$ yields LLM loss 1.2 and $d_k$ yields loss 1.8 on the same question ($L_i = 1.8 - 1.2 = 0.6 > 0$, so $\mathbb{I}(L_i)=1$), and the retriever currently scores $d_j$ lower than $d_k$ ($C_i = \sigma(\hat S_{d_j}) - \sigma(\hat S_{d_k}) < 0.5$), the term $\log(C_i)$ is pushed up by the gradient step, moving $\hat S_{d_j}$ above $\hat S_{d_k}$ to match the fact that $d_j$ was actually more useful to the LLM.

**Long context.** Because feeding several retrieved documents can exceed Llama's native 4k-token context, the LLM side uses Shifted Sparse Attention (S2-Attn) from LongLoRA to extend context length without the full cost of dense attention over the longer sequence [1][7].

**Variant actually run.** The paper trains with outcome-style pairwise comparisons over the top-30 retrieved documents per question (resampled by weighted random sampling each iteration, favoring higher-scored documents), with the top 7 documents by score fed to the LLM during a given training pass [1]. No other loss variant is defined in the paper, and there is no packaged trainer to check for a shipped default (see Shipped by).

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: every training step needs an LLM forward-and-backward pass over the sampled documents (for the answer loss) plus a retriever forward pass over the candidate documents (for the rank loss), so training holds two trained models' weights, gradients, and optimizer state simultaneously - unlike RAG-style setups that only train one side. Because $L_{rank}$ requires comparing the LLM's loss across different candidate documents for the same question, computing it in the most direct reading of the formula implies rerunning the LLM's loss for multiple document choices per question, not just once; the paper does not specify how many document-pairs per question are evaluated this way, so this is a naive reading the paper's own text does not resolve.

**In practice, per the reference implementation** [6][1]: the paper reports wall-clock training on four A100 80GB GPUs for both JMLR and its baselines (Section 4.4, "Training Details") [1], and separately reports total GPU-hours (a normalized, not wall-clock, figure) per model: JMLR-13B 148 GPU-hours, Meditron-70B 42,630 GPU-hours, JMLR-7B "approximately 100" GPU-hours, RAG-7B 72 GPU-hours, and RAG-13B 128 GPU-hours [1]. These GPU-hours figures are not the same quantity as wall-clock time on four GPUs at once; the paper does not state the conversion it used. Separately, the intro states that training the 7B model "with a single Nvidia A100 GPU" takes 100 hours, versus 588 hours pretraining plus 36 hours fine-tuning for the Meditron pipeline [1] - this single-GPU wall-clock figure and the "100 GPU-hours" figure from the results section are consistent in magnitude but the paper does not explicitly reconcile the two framings (single-GPU wall-clock in the intro vs. four-GPU experiments in Section 4.4); this card does not resolve that ambiguity beyond reporting both. No independent framework (trl, verl) implementation exists to give a second cost reading.

## How to use it

**Data preparation.** The paper builds its training set from four QA sources - MedQA, Amboss, MedMCQA, and MMLU-Medical - each example a question plus the correct choice and a rationale, and augments MedQA with GPT-4-generated reasoning traces incorporated into the guideline corpus, released as the MedQA-Reason dataset [1][8]. The retrieval corpus draws from medical textbooks, Wikidoc, and public clinical guidelines (per-source counts in the paper's Table 1, not reproduced here) [1].

**Label convention.** Supervision is the correct answer choice plus its rationale text; there is no separate reward model, and correctness is judged by exact match to the gold choice for the accuracy metric, with rationale quality judged separately by a UMLS-concept-overlap F1 score and by GPT-4 given a 1-5 Likert rubric along three dimensions (question comprehension, recall of relevant facts, and medical reasoning) [1].

**Key knobs**, from the paper's own training run (no framework default exists to contrast against; both the LLM and the ColBERT retriever share these except where noted):

| knob | paper's value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| optimizer | AdamW, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, eps $=1\times10^{-5}$ |
| LR schedule | cosine, 10% warmup, decays to 10% of peak |
| LLM learning rate | $1\times10^{-5}$ (matches Llama 2-chat's own recipe) |
| ColBERT learning rate | $3\times10^{-5}$ |
| weight decay | 0.1 |
| batch size | 2 |
| epochs | 5 |
| documents extracted per question (pre-training) | top 30, by retriever score |
| documents shown to LLM per training step | top 7, weighted-random-sampled from the 30 |
| GPUs used | four A100 80GB |

**Trade-offs a run designer faces.** The paper's own document-count ablation (Appendix, Fig. 3) varied the number of documents retrieved and found accuracy on USMLE-style questions lowest at 1 document, peaking at 7, and declining again at 10 due to retrieving an excess of irrelevant material [1] - this card read only that ablation's stated shape (worst at 1, best at 7, worse again at 10), not the individual numeric accuracy values plotted in the figure. Resampling from a top-30 pool each iteration (rather than always showing the same top-7) is the paper's stated mechanism for forcing the LLM to cope with both useful and less-useful retrieved documents, which it frames as a robustness measure [1].

## While it runs

**Signals and their healthy shapes.** The paper does not describe a first-hand training-time monitoring practice (no loss curves, no described dashboard); its own evidence for "is this working" comes from held-out evaluation, not from a live training signal. The clearest in-run signal implied by the method's own math is the sign-agreement rate between the rank loss's $L_i$ (LLM loss-reduction gap between two candidate documents) and $C_i$ (retriever's predicted score gap) - if this stops improving, the retriever has stopped learning anything from the LLM.

**Published reference runs.** The paper's own comparison table is the reference: JMLR-13B reaches 70.5% average accuracy (MedQA 62.5%, Amboss 81.2%, MMLU-Medical 72.8%, MedMCQA 65.5%) versus Meditron-70B's 68.9% and RAG-13B's 67.7% (Table 2) [1]. The deciding ablation is Table 5 (7B scale, MedQA/Amboss): a naive fine-tuned Llama-7B with no retriever scores 44.0%/46.5%; adding a frozen ColBERT retriever used only at inference, not during fine-tuning (FT-Llama-7B-ColBERT), scores lower at 40.6%/45.6% in Table 5 itself [1]; the asynchronous joint variant JMLR-7B-Separate (alternately freezing retriever and LLM) reaches 55.3%/69.0%; and the fully joint JMLR-7B reaches 56.2%/71.2% [1] - the gap between the no-retriever baseline and JMLR-7B (12.2 and 24.7 points) is the paper's evidence the method works at all, and the smaller gap between JMLR-7B-Separate and JMLR-7B (0.9 and 2.2 points) is its evidence that *synchronous* joint training, specifically, adds value over asynchronous joint training of the same two components. Note a discrepancy in the source itself: the paper's prose bullet discussing this table states "FT-Llama-7B+ColBERT (44.0% on MedQA, 46.5% on Amboss) showed significant improvements over FT-Llama-7B" [1], which matches the FT-Llama-7B row's own values (44.0%/46.5%), not the FT-Llama-7B-ColBERT row's values (40.6%/45.6%) two paragraphs later in the same section [1]; this card follows Table 5's numbers, since a second prose bullet in the same section independently restates FT-Llama-7B-ColBERT as 40.6%/45.6% when comparing it to JMLR-7B-Separate [1].

**Degeneracies and defaults.** The rank loss's indicator $\mathbb{I}(L_i \neq 0)$ drops any document pair where the LLM's loss was unaffected by which one was retrieved - the paper states this is exactly why the indicator is there, since such pairs carry no ranking signal [1]; a corpus or task where most documents are equally (ir)relevant to the LLM would starve the rank loss of usable pairs. No config-level defaults exist to diverge from, since no framework ships this method.

**Named successors.** None found in the paper's own text and none found among the 62 papers that cite it on Semantic Scholar as of this check (queried the Semantic Scholar citations endpoint for arXiv:2402.17887, all 62 returned entries inspected by title; the highest-cited citers are broad healthcare-LLM surveys, not a follow-on method) [4].

**Known failure modes.** The paper's own Limitations and Ethical Considerations section states two: the method is validated only on medical QA and reasoning, and its adaptation to other domains "remains unexplored" [1]; and human evaluation relied on only three doctors as annotators, which the authors say limits the statistical significance of the preference results and which they leave to future work alongside fairness, cross-domain generalizability, and bias concerns [1]. No maintainer-reported failure modes were found: the reference repository's issue tracker shows 0 open issues as of the API check on 2025-01-31 (repo last-push date), so this card found no maintainer discussion of numerical or implementation traps to report [6].

**What the gain is - and is not.** The paper's own analysis attributes JMLR's advantage over frozen-retriever RAG specifically to the retriever learning which documents are useful to the LLM rather than merely topically relevant, evidenced by the retriever trained with JMLR reaching 57.5% average accuracy when its retrieved documents are handed to a different, unrelated model (GPT-3.5) versus 56.7% for the RAG-trained retriever on the same test [1]. The paper also reports JMLR-13B improves rationale quality and factuality (UMLS-F1 and GPT-4-rubric scores) over Claude3-Opus and GPT-3.5, and that JMLR-13B's rationales won an expert-preference comparison against GPT-3.5's at a 0.60 rate on a 20-question human-evaluated sample [1]. The gain is scoped by the authors to medical QA with access to a curated document corpus; nothing in the paper's own analysis claims the method adds reasoning capability the base Llama 2 model could not reach with the right documents in context - its case studies frame the improvement as better document selection and use, not new capability.
