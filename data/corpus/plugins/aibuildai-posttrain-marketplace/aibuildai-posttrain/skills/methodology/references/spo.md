# SPO

https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04056

Delete the reward model and the second dueling policy: sample a trajectory, score its win rate against a queue of the policy's own past rollouts using a preference function, and feed that win rate as the reward to any on-policy RL algorithm.

**SPO** (Self-Play Preference Optimization) is an online-RL method for reinforcement learning from human feedback, introduced by Swamy, Dann, Kidambi, Wu, and Agarwal as an algorithm that "does not require training a reward model nor unstable adversarial training" [1]. It builds on the framing of RLHF as a two-player zero-sum game whose Nash equilibrium is a Minimax Winner (MW), a preference-aggregation solution concept from social choice theory (Kreweras, Simpson, Kramer, Fishburn) [1]. Prior work computes an MW by "dueling" two policies against each other in an adversarial-training loop; SPO's parent technique is this dueling approach, credited to Yue, Broder, Kleinberg, and Joachims' analysis of the K-armed dueling-bandits problem, and named in SPO's own Table 1 alongside the online Dueling-Bandit-Gradient-Descent baseline DBGD (Yue & Joachims, 2009) [1][2]. SPO proves that, because preference functions are anti-symmetric, one agent playing against a queue of its own past trajectories suffices to converge to a symmetric MW, removing the second policy [1]. The paper gives three reasons for building SPO instead of reusing reward-based RLHF or dueling: reward models force a Copeland-Winner tie-break that can leave half a population dissatisfied under intransitive preferences, and are not truly reward-model-free; dueling inherits the instability of adversarial training and doubles the models held in memory; and single-agent self-play only needs one no-regret optimizer, which the paper proves converges to an approximate MW at the underlying optimizer's regret rate [1].

The paper's own identity was confirmed by an exact-title match on Semantic Scholar (paperId `324786abbbc22ca1fba487709536ee682fe0af60`, citationCount 163 as of 2026-08-09, against the shortlist's 164 — a difference consistent with normal drift on a live citation count), distinguishing it from four other arXiv papers that also use the acronym "SPO" (Segment Policy Optimization, Self-Supervised Prompt Optimization, Simple Policy Optimization, Sequential Preference Optimization) [3]. This covers only 30 of the paper's 163 total citing papers and was not the highest-cited subset, so it does not rule out a landmark adopter or successor among the other 133. The paper's own deciding result is Figure 5(a): across three discrete intransitive-preference configurations (three sub-populations with cyclic aggregate preferences), SPO's learned policy has total-variation distance to the exact MW of 0.01, 0.02, and 0.00, versus 0.51, 0.07, and 0.63 for an iterative reward-model (RM) baseline run under the same online, continuously-updated setup [1]. Lineage in one line: reward-based RLHF / DPO (both provably fail to recover the MW under intransitive preferences, Theorem 2.4) and dueling MW-computation (Yue et al., 2012) [1][2] -> SPO (2024) [1] -> no named successor found among the first 30 of 163 total citing papers checked [4].

**When to pick it**: online RL when you have query access to a preference function over pairs of trajectories (a human rater or an AI comparator) and want to avoid both training a reward model and the instability of two-policy adversarial dueling, especially when preferences may be intransitive, non-Markovian, or aggregated across raters with disagreement [1]. Prefer dueling (Yue et al., 2012) [2] only if you specifically need its established online-regret guarantees and can tolerate holding and training two policies. Prefer DPO [5] when you have a fixed offline dataset of preference pairs and no ongoing query access to a preference oracle — DPO is offline and assumes an implicit reward model, which the SPO paper proves (Theorem 2.4) can provably miss the Minimax Winner under intransitive preferences [1]. The nearest online neighbor tested in the paper is iterative reward modeling (RM): an RM that is continuously retrained on freshly sampled on-policy trajectories during the same online run as SPO; the paper's own comparison (Figure 5a) shows this iterative RM baseline still misses the MW where SPO does not [1].

**Variant of**: the two-policy dueling technique for computing Minimax Winners (Yue et al., 2012, cited via [1]) [2] — SPO's contribution is proving a single self-play agent against its own past iterates suffices, removing the second policy [1].

**Data it needs**: query access to a preference function $P: \Xi \times \Xi \to [-1,1]$ over pairs of trajectories, anti-symmetric ($P(\xi_1,\xi_2) = -P(\xi_2,\xi_1)$) and zero on ties ($P(\xi,\xi)=0$) — either a preference model trained offline (RLAIF-style) or a human-in-the-loop rater [1]. All experiments in the paper use this online, context-free oracle setting; the paper explicitly leaves the contextual, offline-dataset variant (Figure 4, right) unexplored [1]. On-policy: a trajectory is sampled fresh from the current policy at every round and scored against a queue of $B$ of the policy's own past trajectories (Algorithm 2) [1]. Paper's own scale is continuous-control robotics, not language models: DMControl walker/cheetah/quadruped tasks trained to roughly $10^7$ environment steps (Figure 6), plus a discrete 3-armed bandit run for 50M steps to test exact MW convergence (Appendix F.1) [1].

**Extra models**: no reward model and no second dueling policy by SPO's own definition [1]. The method still needs whatever the chosen base RL optimizer requires: the paper's continuous-control experiments run SAC, whose actor-critic architecture (same critic network size as used for the RM baseline) is a property of SAC, not something SPO itself adds [1]. No KL term against a frozen reference model is part of SPO's objective. Details in Cost.

**Shipped by**: no library implements it. `trl`'s trainer directory (as of the 2026-08-09 fetch) exports `dpo`, `grpo`, `kto`, `reward`, `rloo`, and `sft` trainers and no self-play or SPO trainer [6]. A GitHub repository search finds no official release from the paper's authors, only two unofficial, unstarred reimplementations (`kingsleykimm/SPO-paper`, `Hoponga/SPO`), neither maintained by a framework team [7]. Building it would take a reward-relabeling wrapper around an existing on-policy RL trainer (PPO, TRPO, or SAC): replace the trainer's per-trajectory reward with the win rate against a queue of $B$ past trajectories and divide it equally across timesteps (Algorithm 2, Lines 6–8) [1] — a loss/reward-computation change on top of an existing trainer, not a new sampling loop or new network.

## How it works

The loop: sample a trajectory from the current policy; score its win rate against a queue $Q$ of $B$ past trajectories using the preference function; split that scalar win rate equally across the trajectory's timesteps as a reward; run one step of any on-policy RL optimizer on the relabeled reward; push the new trajectory into the queue [1].

**Preference oracle and MW.** The oracle is $P: \Xi \times \Xi \to [-1,1]$ (Eq. 1), extended to a preference over policies as $P(\pi_1,\pi_2) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\xi_1\sim\pi_1,\xi_2\sim\pi_2}[P(\xi_1,\xi_2)]$ (Eq. 2) [1]. The Copeland Winner formalizes the natural idea of picking the option that beats the largest number of other options [1]: $CW(P) \triangleq \arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi} \sum_{\pi'\in\Pi} P(\pi,\pi')$ (Eq. 3); it is not always unique, and reward-based RLHF and DPO are shown to implicitly compute it [1]. The Minimax Winner is defined, immediately after Eq. 3 and before Eq. 4 in the paper's own numbering (this definition itself carries no equation number in the source text), as the Nash equilibrium of the two-player zero-sum game with payoffs $P$:

$$ MW(P) \triangleq \Big( \arg\max_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)} \min_{q\in\Delta(\Pi)} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_1\sim p,\pi_2\sim q}[P(\pi_1,\pi_2)],\ \arg\min_{q\in\Delta(\Pi)} \max_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_1\sim p,\pi_2\sim q}[P(\pi_1,\pi_2)] \Big) $$

Because $\Pi$ is convex, any distribution over policies collapses to a single policy via the weighted average $\tilde{p}(\xi) = \sum_{\pi\in\Pi} p(\pi)p(\xi|\pi)$ (Eq. 4), so a practitioner never needs to maintain an explicit distribution over policies [1]. The MW always exists (Sion's minimax theorem) even when the CW does not, needs no assumption of an underlying reward function, and strictly generalizes the CW: when a reward function does explain the preferences, the MW coincides with the reward-optimal policy [1].

**Self-play loss and reward.** The SPO loss at round $t$ is the negative preference of a candidate strategy $p$ against the current iterate $p_t$: $\ell^{SPO}_t(p) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim p,\pi'\sim p_t}[-P(\pi,\pi')]$ (Eq. 6) [1]. Running any no-regret online optimizer on this sequence of losses, using only a single copy of the policy against its own past iterates, makes the average iterate a $2\text{Reg}(T)/T$-approximate MW (Theorem 2.3), where $\text{Reg}(T)$ is the optimizer's own no-regret bound (Eq. 5) [1]. Equivalently, this update is an RL step against a dynamic, policy-dependent reward model $r_t^{SPO}(\xi) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\pi'\sim p_t}[\mathbb{E}_{\xi'\sim\pi'}[P(\xi,\xi')]]$ (Eq. 7): the reward of a trajectory is its expected win rate against trajectories drawn from the learner's own current distribution [1]. Theorem 2.4 proves that reward-based RLHF and DPO's optimal policies can fail to be the MW: with a three-option $\Pi$ whose unique MW is $[x,x,1-2x]$ for $x\in(\tfrac13,\tfrac12)$, any reward function can only express one option beating all others, two beating a third, or all tied — none of which represents that MW [1].

**Practical algorithm (Algorithm 2).** Instead of sampling multiple trajectories per update as the theoretical version (Algorithm 1) requires, the practical version keeps a fixed-size queue $Q$ of $B$ past trajectories and reward-relabels new samples against it [1]: for a freshly sampled trajectory $\xi_t \sim \pi_t$, compute $r_t(\xi_t) = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q=1}^{B} P(\xi_t,\xi_q)$, set every per-timestep reward $r_t^h = r_t(\xi_t)/H$ for the trajectory's $H$ timesteps, run one update of the chosen RL algorithm (PPO, TRPO, or SAC) on this relabeled reward, and slide the queue forward by dropping the oldest trajectory and appending $\xi_t$ [1]. Splitting the trajectory-level reward equally across timesteps preserves the optimal-policy set under the resulting per-timestep reward (Lemma 2.7) [1].

**Worked micro-example.** Take a queue of $B=4$ past trajectories and a new trajectory $\xi_t$ with a horizon of $H=10$ timesteps. Suppose the preference function scores $\xi_t$ as beating 3 of the 4 queue trajectories and losing to 1: $P$ values $\{+1,+1,+1,-1\}$. Then $r_t(\xi_t) = (1+1+1-1)/4 = 0.5$, and every one of the trajectory's 10 timesteps is assigned $r_t^h = 0.5/10 = 0.05$ (Algorithm 2, Lines 6–8) [1]. If instead the queue happens to contain 4 trajectories identical in behavior to $\xi_t$, every $P$ value is 0 (anti-symmetry forces $P(\xi,\xi)=0$), so $r_t(\xi_t)=0$ and the step carries no learning signal that round.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: at each round, scoring one sampled trajectory against a queue of $B$ past trajectories costs $B$ evaluations of the preference function per trajectory (Algorithm 2, Line 7) [1], in place of a reward model's single forward pass per trajectory. Whether this is more or less expensive depends entirely on how costly one preference-function query is (a cheap ground-truth comparator vs. an expensive human or LLM judge) — the theory only fixes the query count at $B$, not the wall-clock cost of a query.
- Memory: no reward-model parameters and no second policy are trained, by construction [1]. The queue of $B$ past trajectories is stored, not trained — it holds no gradients or optimizer state, so its cost is $B$ trajectories' worth of storage, not $B$ times a model's parameter memory. Whatever value network the chosen base optimizer (PPO/TRPO/SAC) itself requires is unaffected by SPO; SPO only changes how the reward fed to that optimizer is computed.
- A naive reading of Algorithm 1 (which samples full distributions over policies) suggests a distribution over models must be held; Eq. 4's collapse and the practical Algorithm 2 show this is not needed — only a single policy and a queue of past trajectories are held at once [1].

**In practice:**

- No shipping framework implements SPO (see Shipped by), so there is no framework-reported profiling to cite. The only recorded compute is the paper's own experimental configuration (Appendix F): the discrete bandit experiment ran a queue of $B=1000$ trajectories with 50M PPO steps and a learning rate of $10^{-4}$; the continuous-control non-Markovian and intransitive-preference experiments used $B=10$ with a 1M-trajectory replay buffer; the noisy and maximum-reward-preference continuous-control experiments used a larger queue of $B=100$ "due to the higher noise in the preferences," paired with a shorter 100k replay buffer "to avoid preference information becoming too stale" [1]. The RM baseline used for comparison in the same experiments updates its reward model every 256 policy updates with batch size 64 and learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ [1].
- Do not carry these numbers to a hypothetical framework build: they are the specific $B$ and buffer sizes this paper tuned for its own three experiment families, not defaults any library ships.

## How to use it

- Preference function design: it must be anti-symmetric ($P(\xi_1,\xi_2) = -P(\xi_2,\xi_1)$) and zero on self-comparison ($P(\xi,\xi)=0$) [1]; a scalar comparison function $P(\xi_1 \succ \xi_2)$ can be converted via $P(\xi_1,\xi_2) = 2P(\xi_1\succ\xi_2)-1$ [1]. This can be a preference model trained offline on human/AI comparisons or a live human-in-the-loop rater [1].
- Reward convention: SPO never trains a reward model; the "reward" handed to the base RL optimizer at each step is the queue win rate itself, recomputed fresh every round (Algorithm 2, Line 7) rather than cached from when a trajectory entered the queue [1].
- Key knobs, from the paper's own three experiment families (Appendix F) — there are no framework defaults to compare against, because no library ships this method:

| knob | discrete bandit (Fig. 5a) [1] | continuous control, non-Markovian (Fig. 6c) [1] | continuous control, Ant-v3 intransitive (Fig. 5b, 13) [1] | continuous control, noisy / max-reward (Fig. 6a, 6b, 9) [1] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| queue size $B$ | 1000 | 10 (Appendix F.2's general setting for "non-Markov and intransitive preferences") | 100 (Appendix F.3 explicitly overrides F.2's general B=10 for this specific Ant-v3 experiment) | 100 |
| replay buffer size | not stated | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 (F.3 does not state an override, so F.2's general value applies) | 100,000 |
| base RL algorithm | PPO | SAC | SAC | SAC |
| learning rate | $10^{-4}$ | policy $3\times10^{-5}$ (Table 2) | policy $3\times10^{-5}$ (Table 2) | policy $3\times10^{-5}$ (Table 2) |
| RM baseline update frequency | every 16 policy updates | every 256 policy updates | every 256 policy updates | every 256 policy updates |

- Queue-size trade-off: the paper's general setting for "non-Markov and intransitive preferences" is a small $B=10$ (Appendix F.2), but for the Ant-v3 intransitive-preference experiment specifically, Appendix F.3 overrides this to $B=100$, with no stated reason in that section; separately, for the noisy/max-reward tasks the paper raises $B$ to 100 because higher preference noise needs a larger sample to average over, and correspondingly shortens the replay buffer to keep queue trajectories from going stale [1]. A run designer facing a noisier or higher-variance preference source should expect to raise $B$ and shrink the buffer window together, following this paper's own reasoning rather than a fixed rule.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: on the discrete intransitive-preference task, where the exact MW is computable, the paper's primary signal is total-variation distance from the learned policy to the exact MW (Figure 5a) — this is only computable because the MW is known in closed form for that toy problem, not a general-purpose live metric [1]. On the continuous-control Ant-v3 intransitive task, the paper instead watches whether the agent's end-of-episode position traces circles of the expected radius across training (Figure 5b, more seeds in Figure 13) as a qualitative proxy for MW convergence [1]. In the reward-consistent ("Maximum Reward Preference") setting, the signal is simply return $J(\pi)$ over environment steps compared against RM and an oracle RL-on-ground-truth-reward baseline (Figure 6a) [1].
- Published reference runs: Figure 5 (discrete and Ant-v3 dTV/trajectory results), Figure 6 (walker/cheetah/quadruped return curves), Figure 9 (larger set of noisy-preference environments), and Figure 10 (larger set of non-Markovian environments) are the paper's own reference curves for this method [1]. Figure 6's own caption assigns panel (a) to matching ground-truth-reward preferences, panel (b) to the noisy-preference robustness ablation, and panel (c) to the non-Markovian threshold-crossing task [1]. This conflicts with the paper's own body text, which instead attributes the non-Markovian threshold-crossing result to "Figs. 6 (b) and 9" [1] — an internal inconsistency in the source between the figure caption and the body prose; this card follows the figure's own caption (panel c = non-Markovian, panel b = noise ablation) as the more specific label, but a reader checking the figure should expect this mismatch rather than assume one of the card's two possible readings without checking [1].
- Degeneracies: if every trajectory in the queue scores $P=0$ against the newly sampled trajectory (anti-symmetry forces this when the queue is behaviorally identical to the new sample), the round's win-rate reward is 0 and carries no learning signal that step — worked out above in the micro-example; the paper does not separately name or study this case. The paper does report, for its RM baseline (not SPO itself), that freezing the reward model partway through training — moving closer to an offline RLHF recipe — causes a consistent decline in RM performance (Figure 15) [1].
- Named successors: none found in the first page checked. Search performed: Semantic Scholar's citing-papers list for this paper's paperId, the API's default (unsorted) first page of 30 results out of 163 total citing papers, checked 2026-08-09; the highest citationCount in that page is 6 and none of the 30 titles frames itself as a bias-fixing or drop-in successor to SPO [4]. The remaining 133 citing papers were not checked, so this is not a search of the full citing population.
- Known failure modes, from the paper's own results and Discussion section: on the noisy-preference experiment (ground-truth preferences flipped by i.i.d. Bernoulli noise), SPO "performs comparably to RM on some environments while performing noticeably worse on some," because it does not learn an extra averaging model the way RM does [1]. In its Section 4 Discussion, the paper lists open problems rather than solved ones: better understanding of last-iterate convergence under bandit feedback, and handling uncertainty in learned preference functions, are named as open research directions, not settled behavior [1]. The same section names applying SPO to fine-tuning generative models with AI or human feedback as future empirical work, not something the paper has validated [1], and flags a computational limitation of preference-model-based methods versus reward-model-based ones in the contextual (e.g. LLM prompt-conditioned) setting: scoring one generation needs at least two preference-function queries, versus one forward pass for a reward model [1].
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's own results show SPO nearly exactly recovers the Minimax Winner where an iterative RM baseline converges to an arbitrary corner under intransitive preferences (Figure 5a, the deciding number above), is more sample-efficient than iterative RM when preferences are consistent with a single ground-truth reward, and more often exhibits the qualitatively non-Markovian behavior a threshold-constrained task requires than RM does (Figure 6, panel labeled non-Markovian in the caption — see the caption/body-text discrepancy noted in While it runs) [1]. It is not shown to be more robust than RM to i.i.d. noisy/inconsistent preferences — the paper's own noisy-preference result shows the opposite on some environments [1] — and every one of these results is on continuous-control robotics benchmarks (DMControl, MuJoCo Ant-v3); the paper explicitly has not tested language-model or generative-model fine-tuning [1].
