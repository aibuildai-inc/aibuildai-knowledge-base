# SCOTT

Distill a giant LM's chain-of-thought into a small student that actually answers according to its own rationale: elicit answer-grounded rationales from the teacher with contrastive decoding, then fine-tune the student with an added counterfactual-reasoning loss that forces it to change its answer when the rationale would lead to a different one.

**SCOTT** (Self-Consistent Chain-of-Thought Distillation) is a knowledge-distillation method for training a small "self-rationalizing" student LM (one that generates a rationale and then answers conditioned on it) to reason faithfully, introduced by Wang et al. as a two-sided fix to vanilla chain-of-thought (CoT) distillation. The paper defines it as "a knowledge distillation (KD) method for eliciting faithful CoT reasoning, where a small student model learns from a large teacher model to generate CoT rationales that are consistent to its own predictions" [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01879 [1]. Its parent is standard rationale-distillation KD, where a large LM is prompted in-context to generate rationales for a downstream dataset which are then used to fine-tune a small student LM, as done by Li et al. and Shridhar et al. [1][2][3]. SCOTT keeps that self-rationalization pipeline but changes two steps: teacher-side, it decodes rationale tokens by contrasting the token probabilities under the gold answer against the same context with the answer perturbed to empty or wrong, so tokens are preferred only when they become more plausible once the answer is considered [1]; student-side, it adds a counterfactual training loss that trains the student to predict a different answer when fed a rationale that was generated to support a wrong answer, removing the question-to-answer shortcut that lets the student ignore its own rationale [1]. The paper gives two reasons beyond fixing existing faithfulness issues: prompted teacher rationales are prone to hallucination and often fail to justify the given answer (in a 100-rationale pilot on StrategyQA, 42% added no new information and 37% did not justify the answer) [1], and self-rationalizing students exploit spurious question-answer correlations as a reasoning shortcut, decoupling rationale generation from the actual prediction [1].

The method is an ACL 2023 outstanding-paper result [4]; its own gains are internal, comparing decoding/training variants on the same student architecture. Of the first 100 papers (of an unstated larger total, per the API's pagination) returned by Semantic Scholar's citations endpoint for this arXiv ID [5], one is a deployed production system: Amazon's COSMO e-commerce commonsense system, presented at SIGMOD-Companion 2024 [6]. Reading COSMO's own text shows it cites SCOTT once, in a passing related-work aside noting that scaling up presents "challenges in terms of inference efficiency when distilling knowledge from LLMs," alongside one other citation - not as an adopted pipeline component or a description of using SCOTT's method [6]. This card did not open the full text of the other 99 titles in the batch, so it cannot say whether any of them also cite SCOTT as a passing mention; COSMO is the only production system among them that this card verified by full-text reading. Across four knowledge-intensive QA datasets (CSQA, StrategyQA, CREAK, QASC), a human evaluation of 100 StrategyQA teacher rationales shows contrastive decoding with a wrong perturbed answer raises the fraction judged to support the gold answer from 0.48 (greedy decoding) to 0.63, and the fraction rated as adding new information from 0.65 to 0.82, at a small grammaticality cost (0.99 to 0.97) [1, Table 1]. On the student side, Figure 6 reports that on CSQA the CoT-prompting baseline (prompting the 20B teacher directly) reaches only 38.4% accuracy, while every KD-trained T5-3B student variant, including SCOTT, clusters between about 74.9% and 75.7% accuracy [1], and the paper reports that its full method (contrastive decoding plus counterfactual training) achieves higher LAS faithfulness than any baseline while accuracy stays comparable across KD variants [1]. Lineage in one line: prompted CoT (Wei et al., 2022 [7]) and rationale-distillation KD (Li et al., Shridhar et al., 2022 [2][3]) -> SCOTT (2023 [1]) -> QCRD and CoPeD (2024-2025 [8][9]), each extending one of SCOTT's own mechanisms (see While it runs).

**When to pick it**: pick SCOTT when you need a small model that both answers and emits a rationale, and you specifically care that the rationale is faithful to the model's own decision (measured by LAS, not just task accuracy) [1]. Prefer plain rationale-distillation KD - fine-tune directly on greedy-decoded or human-annotated teacher rationales, the paper's own "Learn from Greedy Decoding" and "Learn from Human" baselines - when faithfulness is not a requirement and you want to avoid the extra teacher forward pass and extra counterfactual training data [1]. Prefer direct CoT prompting of the large teacher (no distillation) only if you can afford teacher-scale inference at serving time and do not need a faithfulness guarantee; the paper's own CSQA result shows this baseline is far less accurate than any distilled student (38.4% vs ~75%) [1].

**Variant of**: rationale-distillation knowledge distillation in the self-rationalization paradigm (student generates a rationale, then predicts conditioned on it), as opposed to post-rationalization (predict then explain) or multi-task rationale generation, a framing SCOTT's own paper uses to distinguish itself from Li et al. and Shridhar et al. [1][2][3].

**Data it needs**: for the teacher step, a handful of hand-written few-shot exemplars, each a question, gold answer, and a human-written rationale, used as an in-context prompt [1]; and a training set of question-answer pairs (no rationales required) for the teacher to rationalize over - the paper used CSQA, StrategyQA, CREAK, and QASC, with official dev sets repurposed as test sets since test labels are not public [1]. For the student step, the teacher's silver rationales (factual pass) plus a second set of rationales generated by prompting the teacher with a wrong answer swapped in (counterfactual pass) [1]. The paper does not report per-dataset split sizes; it states only that the official training set of each dataset is randomly split into a new training set and development set [1]. The method is offline in the sense that student training data is generated once from a frozen teacher up front, not resampled from the student during training.

**Extra models**: a frozen teacher LM for rationale generation - the paper uses GPT-NeoX-20B, chosen because the checkpoint is public and exposes token-wise probabilities needed for contrastive decoding [1]; no reward model and no value network. No additional model is needed at student training or inference time beyond the student itself; the contrastive-decoding step needs only the frozen teacher, run twice per rationale (once under the gold answer, once under the perturbed answer) to compute the plausibility-growth term [1].

**Shipped by**: no post-training library implements SCOTT as a ready trainer; the authors' own PyTorch research code (`consistent-CoT-distillation`) is the only implementation, with separate scripts for contrastive-decoding data generation and counterfactual-training fine-tuning, unaccompanied by a stated support status [10]. Building it in a modern trainer would mean two custom pieces: a batched contrastive-decoding sampler that runs the teacher twice per token position and combines the two log-probabilities before greedy selection (not a trainer-loss change), and a supervised fine-tuning loss on top of an existing seq2seq trainer that adds the counterfactual example's answer-only cross-entropy term - a data-generation job plus a small SFT loss change, not a new RL-style sampling loop.

## How it works

The loop in one line: prompt the frozen teacher for a factual rationale (gold answer given) and a counterfactual rationale (wrong answer given) via contrastive decoding, then fine-tune the student on both with two summed losses.

**Contrastive decoding (teacher).** Standard greedy decoding of the rationale picks the locally most likely token given the prompt, question, and gold answer [1, Eq. 1]:

$$ t_i^{*} = \arg\max_{t_i} \log P(t_i \mid p, q, a^{*}, t_{<i}) $$

SCOTT instead measures how much more plausible each candidate token becomes specifically because the gold answer $a^{*}$ is in context, by comparing against the same context with $a^{*}$ replaced by a perturbed answer $a'$ (either an empty string or a randomly sampled incorrect answer) [1, Eq. 3]:

$$ G(t_i \mid a^{*}) = \log \frac{P(t_i \mid p, q, a^{*}, t_{<i})}{P(t_i \mid p, q, a', t_{<i})} $$

The final decoding objective adds this plausibility-growth term to the ordinary log-probability, trading off fluency against answer-grounding [1, Eq. 4]:

$$ t_i^{*} = \arg\max_{t_i} \Big[\log P(t_i \mid p, q, a^{*}, t_{<i}) + G(t_i \mid a^{*})\Big] $$

**Student training.** Given a question $q$, gold answer $a^{*}$, and teacher rationale $r$, the student is fine-tuned with standard sequence-to-sequence cross-entropy over the rationale-then-answer output, tagged with a `[Factual]` prefix [1, Eq. 2]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{factual} = -\sum_i \log P(t_i \mid q, t_{<i}) $$

For the counterfactual pass, the teacher is re-prompted with a randomly perturbed wrong answer $a'$ in place of $a^{*}$ (Eq. 4's contrastive decoding again, now grounded in $a'$), producing a counterfactual rationale $r'$ that argues for $a'$; the student is then taught, tagged `[Counterfactual]`, to output $a'$ when $r'$ is fed to its decoder as teacher-forced context, with the loss applied only to the answer tokens [1, Eq. 5]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{counterfactual} = -\sum_{i \in a'} \log P(t_i \mid q, r', t_{<i}) $$

The total training loss is the sum $\mathcal{L}_{factual} + \mathcal{L}_{counterfactual}$ [1].

**Worked example** (paraphrasing the paper's own StrategyQA illustration [1, Fig. 3]): for "Would someone with back pain enjoy picking strawberries?", greedy decoding under the gold answer "no" produces a rationale about backs not being fruit - fluent but not answer-grounded. Contrastive decoding with the perturbed answer set to an empty string instead surfaces "manual labor can cause back pain," a token sequence that is far more likely once the true answer "no" is in context than it is with no answer at all; contrasting against a perturbed wrong answer ("yes") pushes the rationale further toward tokens that are especially probable for "no" and improbable for "yes," such as "the spine is needed to support the body."

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: preparing training data needs, per rationale token position, two teacher forward passes instead of one (Eq. 3's numerator and denominator context), so contrastive decoding costs roughly one extra full teacher forward pass compared to greedy decoding for the same rationale [1, Limitations]. The counterfactual pass repeats this for a second (perturbed-answer) rationale per training example, which by the method's own two-pass structure means roughly double the teacher-generated data compared to a factual-only baseline; the paper's own Limitations section states only that the KD process introduces additional training data, without quantifying it as a doubling [1, Limitations]. Student-side training itself is ordinary seq2seq fine-tuning with no extra forward passes per step - all the extra teacher cost is a one-time, offline data-preparation cost, not a per-training-step cost.
- Memory: the teacher is used only for offline rationale generation and is never trained, so it need not be held alongside the student's optimizer state; the student is a single trained model (a fine-tuned T5, sizes 220M/770M/3B were tested [1, Fig. 7]) with no separate value network, reward model, or persistent reference model at training time.
- A naive reading might assume contrastive decoding requires two full models resident simultaneously at training time; the formulas show it is the same teacher model queried twice with different answer contexts, at data-generation time only, not two distinct models.

**In practice, per framework:**

- No framework ships SCOTT, so there is no framework-level implementation detail to report here beyond the authors' own repository, which is organized as two shell-script stages (`run_contrastive_decoding.sh` for teacher rationale generation, then `run_counterfactual_training.sh` for student fine-tuning) with no stated multi-GPU or throughput guidance in the top-level README [10].

## How to use it

- Data preparation: a labeled QA dataset split into train/dev/test, plus a small set of hand-written few-shot exemplars (question, gold answer, human rationale) used to prompt the teacher [1]. A separate "counterfactual" training file is built by sampling a wrong answer per training question (flip the label for yes/no or true/false tasks; pick a random incorrect option for multiple-choice) [1].
- Reward/label conventions: there is no scalar reward; the only "labels" are the gold answer used for factual rationale elicitation and the sampled wrong answer used for counterfactual rationale elicitation [1]. Student output is free text: rationale tokens followed by an answer, matched against the gold or perturbed answer by exact string/label match for accuracy scoring [1].
- Knobs table, this card's only two anchors (paper values; no framework ships this method, so there is no framework-default column to compare against):

| knob | paper value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| teacher model | GPT-NeoX-20B |
| student model | T5-3B (main results); T5-base/T5-large also tested in the size ablation |
| perturbation type for contrastive decoding | empty string or randomly sampled wrong answer (two variants reported; wrong-answer variant scores higher on the paper's own faithfulness metric) |
| training loss | sum of $\mathcal{L}_{factual}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{counterfactual}$, no reported weighting coefficient between the two |
| runs per config | 5 runs with fixed random seeds, averaged |

- Trade-off a run designer faces: the wrong-answer perturbation strategy for contrastive decoding costs an extra sampling step (choosing the wrong answer) over the empty-string variant but yields rationales the paper's own human evaluation rates as more answer-supporting (0.63 vs 0.58 "Supports Answer") [1, Table 1]. The accuracy effect of that choice is mixed across the four datasets in Figure 6: CD-Wrong scores higher than CD-Empty on CSQA and QASC, but lower on StrategyQA (both training variants) and on CREAK's Factual+Counterfactual pairing (78.5 vs 79.3) [1, Fig. 6]. There is no group-size or batch-reuse knob analogous to an RL method, since training is standard offline SFT on a fixed, pre-generated dataset.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's own metric is LAS (leakage-adjusted simulatability) [11], computed as the accuracy gain a rationale gives a separately trained simulator model over not seeing the rationale at all - $\mathrm{Acc}(qr \to a) - \mathrm{Acc}(q \to a)$ - reported both for the teacher's rationale-answer consistency (Figure 5) and for the student's rationale-prediction faithfulness (Figure 6, upper panels) [1]. A higher LAS means the rationale is doing more of the causal work in the prediction; the paper reports LAS rising from the CoT-prompting baseline to every KD variant, and rising further with contrastive decoding and again with the counterfactual loss on top, though the paper does not publish the raw per-run logs behind these figures [1].
- Published reference runs: none beyond the paper's own reported result tables and figures (no separate release of training curves or logs was found in the repository's README) [1][10].
- Degeneracies and defaults: this card found none stated in the paper's own text about training-time failure states (e.g., degenerate rationale collapse); the paper's ablation does note that larger student models achieve higher task accuracy but lower faithfulness (LAS), i.e., bigger students are more prone to ignoring their own rationale despite SCOTT's countermeasures still improving faithfulness at every size tested (T5-base, T5-large, T5-3B) [1, Sec. 4.6].
- Named successors: a Semantic Scholar citations-endpoint fetch for arXiv:2305.01879 (first 100 results) surfaced two papers that extend SCOTT's own mechanisms and cite it. QCRD extends the contrastive-decoding idea by adding a self-adversarial "negative knowledge" source (low-quality rationales sampled from the student's own earlier checkpoints) alongside the positive rationales, distilled with a contrastive loss and a learned discriminator that weights rationale quality [8]. CoPeD (Chain-of-Thought Correctness Perception Distillation) targets the same noisy-rationale problem SCOTT's pilot study identified, adding a correctness-aware task setting that trains the student to revise its answer when its own rationale is wrong, plus a loss that up-weights training instances where the rationale most strongly supports the correct answer [9]. The paper's own Related Works section separately frames SCOTT itself as improving on Li et al. and Shridhar et al., which it critiques for not considering rationale faithfulness [1][2][3].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own Limitations section, SCOTT requires strictly more computation than vanilla KD - one extra teacher forward pass for contrastive decoding, plus the extra counterfactual training data - and the method targets faithfulness rather than task performance, which the authors describe as complementary to, not a replacement for, prior work aimed purely at improving accuracy [1, Limitations]. This card did not search a maintainer issue tracker for closed-issue failure reports; the GitHub repository shows 45 stargazers as of the fetch date and this card did not query its issues for numerical or reproducibility traps [10].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own analysis finds that human-annotated rationales, used as an oracle, give the trained student less of a performance boost than machine-generated (contrastive-decoded) rationales do [1, Sec. 4.7]. A separate analysis attributes this to the human rationales being less consistent with the answers, so the student learns to generate rationales and predict answers more independently, exploiting the spurious question-answer correlation instead [1, Sec. 4.5]. SCOTT's gain is specifically in rationale-answer faithfulness (LAS) and in the ability to steer the student's prediction by editing its rationale, not in raw task accuracy: the paper reports all KD variants land at comparable accuracy on each dataset, with SCOTT neither the best nor the worst [1, Fig. 6].
