# GPG

REINFORCE on group-normalized rewards with the value network, reference model, clip term, and KL penalty all removed, plus a bias-correction factor that reweights the gradient when a batch contains all-right or all-wrong groups.

**GPG** (Group Policy Gradient) is an on-policy RL method for fine-tuning an LLM policy on math and multimodal reasoning tasks, introduced in "GPG: A Simple and Strong Reinforcement Learning Baseline for Model Reasoning" (Chu, Huang, Zhang, Wei, Wang, Alibaba AMAP) [1]. The paper drops the critic and the reference model, removes the KL-divergence term, and corrects a bias it identifies in how the advantage and gradient are estimated, presenting the result as a simpler training recipe than Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [1], its parent method [2]. Like GRPO, GPG samples a group of G completions per prompt and normalizes their rewards by the group mean to form a per-completion advantage, but it plugs that advantage directly into the vanilla policy-gradient loss instead of GRPO's clipped surrogate objective, and drops GRPO's KL term against a reference model entirely [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02546 [1]. The paper gives three reasons for the design: removing the critic and reference models cuts compute and memory versus GRPO and PPO [1]; GRPO's std-normalization of the group reward is itself a function of the state and therefore introduces a reward bias into the advantage, so GPG defaults to a plain mean-centered advantage instead [1]; and a batch that contains examples with all-right or all-wrong groups contributes zero gradient for those examples under naive backpropagation, biasing the mean gradient estimate, which GPG corrects with a reweighting factor the paper calls Accurate Gradient Estimation (AGE) [1].

The paper's own code targets the verl framework, and as of the fetched revision verl documents an implementation of GPG under its Algorithm Baselines section [3][1]. The paper reports no third-party landmark adopters beyond its own released checkpoints, GPG-RS1 (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B trained on the open-s1 recipe) and GPG-7B (Qwen2.5-Math-7B trained on a DAPO-derived dataset) [1]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B with the MATH-lighteval dataset, the paper's own ablation is the deciding comparison: GRPO scores 43.7% average across five math benchmarks, a naive GPG with a plain mean-centered advantage and no gradient correction scores 43.9% (barely ahead of GRPO), and the same GPG with the AGE correction factor applied scores 47.8%; adding the threshold-and-resample mechanism on top raises it to 48.3% (Table 1) [1]. The paper's title-search hit had 155 citations at the time this card was written, far above two other arXiv preprints that also use the "GPG" name — "Group Policy Gradient" (2510.03679, 1 citation) and "GPG: Generalized Policy Gradient Theorem for Transformer-based Policies" (2512.10365, 0 citations) — and the citation-count gap is the evidence this card relies on to confirm 2504.02546 as the intended paper for this row. Lineage in one line: REINFORCE / vanilla policy gradient -> PPO (2017) [4] -> GRPO (DeepSeekMath, 2024) [2] -> GPG (2025) [1], shipped by verl [3].

**When to pick it**: on-policy RL for LLM reasoning when every sampled completion gets a single scalar outcome reward and you want to avoid GRPO's extra reward-std normalization, clip term, and KL-regularized reference model — the paper's own Table 1 shows GPG's final configuration beating or matching GRPO on all five reported math benchmarks under matched hyperparameters, including a tie on AMC23 (62.5 vs 62.5) alongside gains on AIME24 (30.0 vs 16.7), MATH-500 (76.2 vs 73.4), Minerva (34.2 vs 30.2), and OlympiadBench (39.0 vs 35.7) [1]. Prefer GRPO [2] itself, its parent, when you want the community-standard, more heavily battle-tested clipped-surrogate-plus-KL recipe. Prefer DPO [5] when you only have static preference pairs and no ability to sample fresh completions from the current policy: DPO is offline, and trains on pairs of completions to the same prompt without a separate reward model [6]. Nearest online neighbor is RLOO [7], which is also a REINFORCE-family method without PPO-style clipping and uses a leave-one-out baseline computed across the other samples in the batch while keeping the model close to a reference policy via a KL term [8] — a component GPG's own definition removes entirely [1].

**Variant of**: GRPO [2], with the clipped surrogate objective, KL-vs-reference term, and std-based reward normalization removed and a gradient debiasing factor added [1].

**Data it needs**: prompts (question, ground-truth answer) with a rule-based accuracy reward (1.0 correct / 0.0 incorrect) as the main experiments' reward signal, plus an optional formatting reward and an IoU reward for a grounding task [1]. The paper's 7B math ablation used a global batch of 144 prompts with G=8 sampled completions per prompt [1]. On-policy: completions for every batch are freshly sampled from the current policy; there is no offline replay buffer in the method's definition [1].

**Extra models**: none required by the method's own definition — Table 2 in the paper marks GPG as having no value model, no reference model, no surrogate loss, and no policy constraint, the only one of PPO/GRPO/TRPO/GPG with all four off [1]. verl's documented "Advanced Extensions" for GPG show an optional KL-loss add-on (`use_kl_loss: True`, `kl_loss_coef: 0.01`) that reintroduces a frozen reference model [3], but verl's own global actor default has `use_kl_loss: false` (a live `main`-branch read of `verl/trainer/config/actor/actor.yaml`, not pinned to a commit hash and not independently dated by verl's docs) [9], so no reference model loads unless that flag is set. The paper's own ablation on adding a distribution constraint back reports that it negatively impacts performance, without giving a specific number in the sections read for this card [1].

**Shipped by**: verl, via `algorithm.adv_estimator: gpg` plus `actor_rollout_ref.actor.policy_loss.loss_mode: "gpg"` [3], listed under verl's "Algorithm Baselines" heading without an experimental or maintenance-mode label [3]. trl does not implement GPG: its top-level package `__init__.py` (fetched revision) exports no GPG-named trainer [10]. Building it in trl would mean writing a custom policy-loss function on top of an existing sampling loop (e.g. adapting `GRPOTrainer`'s rollout/scoring path) rather than a new sampler, since the mechanism only changes the advantage normalization and the loss term, not how completions are generated.

## How it works

The loop in one line: sample G completions per prompt from the current policy, score each completion, mean-center and debias the group's rewards into per-completion advantages, and take a gradient step on the plain (unclipped) REINFORCE-style loss.

**The objective** [1], Eq. 5:

$$ \mathcal{J}_{GPG}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{o_i\}_{i=1}^{G}} \left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}|o_i|} \sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \left( -\log \pi_\theta(o_{i,t} \mid q, o_{i,<t})\, \hat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right] $$

Unlike PPO's and GRPO's objectives, there is no probability ratio $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ and no clip; the log-probability of each sampled token is weighted directly by its advantage [1]. Normalization here is by total tokens across the whole group ($\sum_i |o_i|$), not per-completion length as in GRPO [1][2].

**Advantage** [1], Eq. 6:

$$ \hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(\{R_i\}_{i=1}^{G})}{F_{norm}} \quad \text{for every token } t \text{ of } o_i $$

The paper treats $F_{norm}=\operatorname{std}\{R(o)\}$ (GRPO's choice) as introducing reward bias because it is itself a function of the sampled state, and defaults to $F_{norm}=1$ instead — a plain mean-centered advantage with no division by the group's reward spread [1].

**AGE (Accurate Gradient Estimation)** [1], Eq. 7: within a training batch of size $B$, if the first $M$ examples belong to groups that are all-right or all-wrong, their advantage is zero and they contribute no gradient; naive backpropagation still divides the summed gradient by the full $B$, understating it. The paper's correction multiplies the naive gradient by $\alpha = B/(B-M)$:

$$ \hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac{\sum_{i=M+1}^{B}\mathbf{g_i}}{B-M} = \alpha\,\mathbf{g}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{B}{B-M} $$

Because gathering which samples are zero-gradient across GPUs to compute $\alpha$ exactly would add communication overhead, the paper proves (Appendix A) an equivalent reformulation that avoids the extra gather: scaling the whole batch loss by $\alpha$ gives the same result, $\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{GPG}(\theta) = \alpha\,\mathcal{J}_{GPG}(\theta)$ (Eq. 8) [1]. Concretely, in Algorithm 1 the per-example loss becomes $-\log\pi_\theta(o)\cdot \hat{A} \cdot \alpha$ [1].

**Worked example**: G = 4 completions to one prompt, rewards $\{1, 0, 0, 0\}$ (paper's default $F_{norm}=1$), so $\operatorname{mean} = 0.25$ and the four raw advantages are $\{0.75, -0.25, -0.25, -0.25\}$ — none are exactly zero here because the group is mixed, so $M=0$ for this group and $\alpha=1$ contributes normally. AGE only reweights when a whole group (or enough of the batch) is degenerate: with rewards $\{1,1,1,1\}$ or $\{0,0,0,0\}$, every advantage in that group is 0, so if such groups make up $M$ of the $B$ batch examples, the surviving mixed-group examples get their loss scaled by $\alpha = B/(B-M)$ instead of being silently down-weighted by the dead examples [1].

**Threshold and resample** [1]: because $\alpha$ can grow large and noisy when the valid-sample fraction is small, the paper defines $\beta_{th} = 1/\alpha_{th}$ and, when the fraction of non-degenerate samples in a batch falls below $\beta_{th}$, accumulates valid samples into the next batch until the threshold is met, rather than fully resampling to $\alpha=1$ (which the paper says is not training efficient because it is bounded by the slowest worker) [1]. The 7B experiments use $\beta_{th}=0.6$ [1].

**Reward normalization variant tested** [1]: the paper also reports $F_{norm}=\operatorname{std}\{R(o)\}$ with $\alpha=1$ (i.e. GRPO's normalization without AGE), scoring 45.3% average — 1.4 points above the plain $F_{norm}=1,\alpha=1$ setting but still below the AGE-corrected $F_{norm}=1$ setting (47.8%) [1]. verl's shipped `compute_gpg_outcome_advantage` applies the same mean-centered, no-std-division advantage by default ($F_{norm}=1.0$) and multiplies by an $\alpha$ scalar [11], but its $\alpha$ is not the paper's Eq. 7 formula: the code sets $\alpha = B/\text{count\_nonzero}(scores).clamp(min=1)$, where the denominator counts individual samples anywhere in the batch with a nonzero total reward, while the paper's $M$ in $\alpha=B/(B-M)$ counts examples belonging to groups that are entirely degenerate (all-right or all-wrong) [1][11]. These diverge whenever a batch has mixed groups with some zero-reward samples: for example, an 8-sample batch with one fully-degenerate all-zero group of 4 and one mixed group of 4 with rewards $\{1,1,0,0\}$ has the paper's $M=4$ (the degenerate group only), giving $\alpha=8/4=2$, while the code's nonzero-count is 2 (just the two reward-1 samples), giving $\alpha=8/2=4$ — a 2x difference on this example [1][11]. A maintainer of the paper's own reference repository, replying to an issue asking where $\alpha$ is implemented, confirmed only that it can equivalently be folded into the advantage or the loss since it is a constant scalar within a batch, and that the released code multiplies it into the loss — the reply does not address this formula discrepancy [12].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: each prompt needs G completions, so generation scales with G times average completion length, same as GRPO. Training passes run over all G completions for the policy only — no critic forward/backward, since the objective (Eq. 5) contains no value term [1]. With the optional KL add-on off (the method's own default), there is no reference-model forward pass either [1].
- Memory: GPG is the only method in the paper's own Table 2 comparison (against PPO, GRPO, TRPO) with no value model and no reference model [1], so its trained-plus-frozen footprint is the policy model alone under default settings.
- A naive reading of Eq. 7's AGE correction implies cross-GPU communication to count zero-gradient examples exactly; the paper's own Appendix A proof (Eq. 8) shows this is avoidable by rescaling the batch loss instead, so the practice layer does not need an extra gather step [1].

**In practice, per framework:**

- verl [3][11]: `algorithm.adv_estimator: gpg` plus `actor_rollout_ref.actor.policy_loss.loss_mode: "gpg"` selects the advantage estimator and loss function; the global actor config's `use_kl_loss` defaults to `false` (live `main`-branch read of `actor.yaml`, not pinned to a commit hash) [9], so no reference model loads unless a user follows verl's documented "Advanced Extensions" and sets `use_kl_loss: True` with `kl_loss_coef: 0.01` [3]. Peak training memory is bounded by verl's usual `ppo_micro_batch_size_per_gpu` micro-batching, not by the group size G, the same mechanism verl uses for GRPO [9].
- Do not carry PPO's or GRPO's per-framework cost figures to GPG: GPG's own definition removes the value model that dominates PPO's extra memory and the reference model that GRPO keeps by default in some framework configs.

## How to use it

- Prompts: math or multimodal reasoning questions with a checkable ground truth. The paper's 7B ablation used the MATH-lighteval dataset; its 1.5B experiments used the open-s1 and open-rs recipes; its multimodal experiments used SAT, GEOQA, four classification datasets (Flower102, Pets37, FGVCAircraft, Car196), and 239 LISA grounding images [1].
- Reward: for most tasks, a 1.0/0.0 accuracy reward, sometimes combined with a 1.0/0.0 formatting reward for the `<think></think><answer></answer>` tag structure; the grounding task instead uses an IoU-based reward [1]. The 7B math ablation used the accuracy reward alone [1].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key for this row):

| knob | paper's 7B setting [1] | verl default, live `main` snapshot [9][11] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| group size G | 8 | `rollout.n` 1 (must be raised for GPG, same requirement as GRPO) |
| $F_{norm}$ | 1 (mean-only; std variant also tested at 45.3% vs 47.8% for mean-only+AGE) | `f_norm` 1.0 |
| AGE correction $\alpha$ † | $B/(B-M)$, $M$ = count of samples in fully degenerate groups | `B / count_nonzero(scores).clamp(min=1)`, denominator = count of individual nonzero-reward samples |
| threshold $\beta_{th}$ | 0.6 | not checked |
| KL loss | off (method's own default) | `use_kl_loss` false by default; optional `kl_loss_coef` 0.01 shown only in verl's "Advanced Extensions" example [3] |
| learning rate | `1e-6` (AdamW, weight decay 0.1) | not checked |
| training scale | 1100 steps, 48 NPUs, batch of 144 prompts | not applicable (framework default) |

  † The two $\alpha$ cells are not the same quantity: they can diverge numerically on the same batch (worked example above), so this row is a comparison of formulas, not of a shared computed value. Separately, verl's KL add-on is off by default, matching the paper's own "no KL constraint" design point, so a user who wants to reproduce the paper's headline numbers should leave `use_kl_loss` at its default rather than following the "Advanced Extensions" example verbatim [1][3][9].
- Group size trade-off: the paper's own group-size ablation (without AGE) on Qwen2.5-Math-7B shows progressively better average scores as G grows from 2 to 16, and the paper picks G=8 as a tradeoff between training cost and performance [1] — the same tradeoff GRPO faces, since larger G means a steadier group mean at the cost of more generation per prompt.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: this card found no first-hand GPG-specific logging or monitoring guidance in the fetched paper text or in verl's GPG documentation page beyond the config keys already listed [1][3]; verl's GPG page documents configuration only, not runtime metrics [3]. A user monitoring a GPG run has to fall back on the generic policy-gradient signals verl and other frameworks expose for the loss and reward curves, none of which this card verified as GPG-specific.
- Published reference runs: the paper's own Table 1 curve (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MATH-lighteval, 1100 steps, 48 NPUs) is the reference point read for this card: GRPO 43.7% average, naive GPG 43.9%, GPG+AGE 47.8%, GPG+AGE+threshold 48.3% [1]; raw training logs are not included in the fetched paper text or the fetched GitHub README.
- Degeneracies and defaults: a batch where every sampled group is all-right or all-wrong drives $M \to B$ in the AGE formula, making $\alpha = B/(B-M)$ diverge — this is exactly the case the threshold-and-resample mechanism exists to bound, by requiring the valid-sample fraction to stay above $\beta_{th}$ before a batch is used [1]. A group of size 1 gives no group mean to subtract against, the same degenerate case GRPO faces; verl's `rollout.n` defaults to 1 and must be raised for GPG's advantage estimator to be meaningful [9][11].
- Named successors: this card found none citing GPG specifically as their starting point. The paper does not name a successor relationship in the other direction either: it calls Dr. GRPO "a very recent and concurrent work" that studies reward and loss normalization and observes GRPO tends to generate more tokens, not a method that follows or fixes GPG [1][13]. DAPO appears in [1] only as one more row in Table 9's loss-formula comparison, showing its asymmetric clip-higher/clip-lower surrogate, and as a bare reference-list entry with no stated motivation [1][14]; the paper does not say what DAPO targets or fixes.
- Known failure modes: the paper's own ablation reports that adding a distribution (KL) constraint back on top of GPG negatively impacts performance, without a table this card could resolve to a specific number in the sections read [1]. This card queried the GitHub API for `AMAP-ML/GPG`'s closed issues and read all 12 returned; none reports a training failure or divergence. Issue #8 ("Where is the alpha parameter implemented in the code?") asks a question directly relevant to this card's own AGE-formula discussion above; the maintainer's reply states only that $\alpha$ can equivalently be folded into the advantage or the loss since it is a constant scalar within a batch, and that the released code multiplies it into the loss, without addressing whether the released code's $\alpha$ matches Eq. 7 exactly [12]. Issue #18 ("How many GPUs were used during actual training, and what was the batch size?") states that for multi-GPU training, accurate gradient calculation requires gathering non-zero-gradient samples across all GPUs and averaging uniformly, rather than computing the AGE count separately per GPU [15]; its comments only answer a follow-up about a specific Open-R1 GPU count and do not add further detail [15]. This is a numerical trap directly on the mechanism this card documents in Extra models and Cost: verl's `compute_gpg_outcome_advantage` computes `count_nonzero(scores)` on the local batch with no `all_gather` call, so a multi-GPU verl run's $\alpha$ is a per-rank quantity, the same divergence issue #18 warns about.
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's own claim is that GPG "consistently outperforms GRPO across various unimodal and multimodal tasks" under matched hyperparameters copied from GRPO's own settings [1]; its ablation (Table 1) attributes most of that gain specifically to the AGE gradient correction (43.9% to 47.8%) rather than to dropping the KL term or the clip term alone, since the naive mean-only advantage without AGE barely beats GRPO. The paper does not claim GPG adds reasoning capability beyond what its RL setup elicits; it presents GPG only as a more efficient and less biased way to fit the same RL objective GRPO fits [1].
