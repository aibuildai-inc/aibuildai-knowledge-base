# CFT

Instead of fine-tuning a model to imitate a correct answer, fine-tune it to generate a written critique of a noisy (possibly wrong) answer, on ([question, noisy response], critique) pairs.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17703 [1]

**CFT** (Critique Fine-Tuning) is a supervised fine-tuning method for improving LLM reasoning, introduced in the paper "Critique Fine-Tuning: Learning to Critique is More Effective than Learning to Imitate," which frames it as a method more effective than SFT for reasoning tasks: instead of simply imitating correct responses, CFT trains models to critique noisy responses [1]. Its parent is Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), the standard practice of training an LLM to imitate annotated responses to instructions, as used in instruction-tuning pipelines such as InstructGPT [2]. Mechanically, CFT concatenates a question $x$ and a noisy response $y$ as input and trains the model to generate a critique $c$ of that response, with GPT-4o used as the teacher that produces the critique targets from an existing dataset's original (uncurated) answers [1]. The paper gives two motivations beyond the imitation-vs-critique framing: standard SFT forces imitation of annotated responses regardless of their quality, and gains from scaling SFT data diminish once the base model is already strong; critique-based training is inspired by human learning processes that emphasize critical thinking, deeper analysis, and nuanced understanding, traits the paper says are often overlooked by standard SFT [1]. The right paper was confirmed as the top-cited result under an exact title search on Semantic Scholar, carrying 62 citations as of the check date and clear of the nearest name-colliding "critique fine-tuning" paper [3].

Landmark adoption: the paper's own release, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-CFT, needs only 1 hour of training on 8 x H100 over 50K examples, yet matches or outperforms Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct (trained on 2.5M samples) on most benchmarks, and matches SimpleRL, a DeepSeek-R1 replication trained with roughly 140x more compute [1]. In the paper's own head-to-head (Table 2), CFT on WebInstruct beats each model's best SFT variant by 3.5 points on DeepSeek-Math-7B (24.0% -> 27.5%, best SFT variant being GPT-4o-SFT), by 10.4 points on Qwen2.5-7B (38.2% -> 48.6%, best SFT variant being verified-SFT), and by 6.7 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (50.4% -> 57.1%, best SFT variant being GPT-4o-SFT) [1]. The same table carries the number that shows quality control is not optional: fine-tuning on WebInstruct's raw, uncurated responses (WebInstruct-SFT) drives every one of the three base models below its own un-tuned baseline - Qwen2.5-7B falls 37.4% -> 20.2% (-17.2 points), DeepSeek-Math-7B falls 20.3% -> 16.1% (-4.2 points), and Qwen2.5-Math-7B falls 37.8% -> 35.1% (-2.7 points) - which the paper summarizes as SFT degrading performance without stringent quality control [1]. A later paper, Critique-GRPO, folds the same critique-as-training-signal idea into an online RL loop, combining natural-language critiques with numerical rewards to fix plateaus that pure numerical-reward RL hits [4]. Lineage in one line: SFT (InstructGPT-era instruction tuning, 2022 [2]) -> CFT (2025 [1]) -> Critique-GRPO (2025 [4]).

**When to pick it**: pick CFT when you have (or can synthesize) a set of question/noisy-response pairs and a strong critique-capable teacher model (the paper used GPT-4o), and you want reasoning gains from a single SFT-style pass rather than an RL loop [1]. Prefer plain SFT [2] when your response data is already high quality and imitation is the goal, not error-finding. Prefer an RL method such as GRPO [5] when you need fresh on-policy samples scored against a verifiable reward each step and can afford an RL training loop; the paper's own comparison shows CFT reaching a comparable average score to SimpleRL's RL-based training (50.2% vs. 50.9%) at roughly 1/140th of the GPU-hours (8 GPUs x 1 hour vs. 32 GPUs x 1.5 days), using no additional rollout sampling (Table 4) [1]. CFT is offline and single-pass like SFT, not an online method: it does not sample from the policy being trained during training.

**Variant of**: SFT [2].

**Data it needs**: (question, noisy response, critique) triples. The paper's main dataset, WebInstruct-CFT, is a 50K-example subset of WebInstruct (an instruction dataset scraped from online educational and quiz sites, spanning math 65%, physics 8%, chemistry 4%, business 10%, humanities 4%, and other topics) where GPT-4o-1120 was prompted to judge and critique the original WebInstruct answers; about 56% of the responses in this subset are judged "correct" by the critique model and the rest "wrong" [1]. The same recipe was applied to 50K-example samples of MetaMathQA and NuminaMath to test generalization [1]. Training scale in the paper: 50K examples, 1 epoch, global batch size 512, on 8xH100 for about 1 hour [1]. CFT is offline: the critique targets are generated once by the teacher model before training, and training is a single supervised pass over that fixed dataset, not on-policy sampling from the model being trained.

**Extra models**: a teacher/critique-generation model is required only at data-construction time, before training starts - the paper uses GPT-4o (and separately ablates GPT-4o-mini as a weaker teacher) to write the critiques [1]. No value network, no reward model, and no frozen reference model are used during the fine-tuning step itself, since the training loss is a plain next-token log-likelihood on the critique text [1].

**Shipped by**: no library ships a distinct "CFT trainer." The method's own released code trains it as a standard supervised-fine-tuning run - the paper's GitHub repository instructs users to install LLaMA-Factory and run its training scripts over the critique-formatted WebInstruct-CFT data [6], and LLaMA-Factory's own supported-approaches table lists "Supervised Fine-Tuning" as one stage with no separate critique-tuning stage [7]. Building it on any other SFT trainer - e.g., trl's `SFTTrainer` [8] - takes only a data step (assembling the ([question; noisy response], critique) examples with a teacher model) on top of an existing SFT loss; no new loss or sampling loop is needed.

## How it works

Generate a teacher critique for each (question, noisy response) pair once, offline; then run one epoch of standard next-token supervised fine-tuning where the model's input is the concatenated question and noisy response and its target is the critique text [1].

**The objective** [1]:

$$ \arg\max_{\theta} \log P(c \mid [x; y]; \theta) $$

where $x$ is the question, $y$ is the noisy (possibly incorrect) response being critiqued, $c$ is the critique text supplied by the teacher model, and $\theta$ are the model's parameters [1]. This is the same maximum-likelihood form as SFT's objective; the only change from plain SFT is what the model is trained to produce - a critique of a given response, instead of a from-scratch correct response - and correspondingly what the input contains [1].

The paper does not define a separate advantage or reward term: there is no RL machinery, no baseline, and no clipping in CFT's objective [1]. A worked instance of the data (from the paper's own appendix example): given the question "How does mitosis contribute to the maintenance and continuity of life in eukaryotic organisms?" and a student solution enumerating mitosis's roles, GPT-4o is prompted with "please explain briefly whether his answer is correct or not. Finally, conclude your judgement with 'Conclusion: right/wrong [END]'" [1] and returns a prose critique ending in that verdict; that (question, solution, critique) triple is one training example, with the model's loss computed only on generating the critique text [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: CFT's training-time cost is exactly one SFT pass over the critique dataset - one forward/backward per example, no extra generation inside the training loop, since the critiques are pre-generated by the teacher before training starts [1]. All of the "extra" compute (calling GPT-4o to critique each noisy response) happens once during dataset construction, off the training critical path.
- Memory: one trained model, the policy itself - no value network, no reward model, no frozen reference model are held during training, because the loss is a single log-likelihood term with no KL or advantage term [1].
- A naive reading might assume critique text roughly doubles sequence length versus a plain SFT target (since it discusses the noisy response as well as producing a verdict), inflating the tokens-per-example cost. The paper's own token-length-controlled ablation shows this is not the source of CFT's gains: length-matched ("short") critique examples still beat SFT (55.2% vs. 50.4% average) (Table 10) [1], so the extra tokens are not what is doing the work, though they are still an extra input/target-length cost relative to imitation-only SFT of the same response.

**In practice, per framework:**

- The paper's own released code runs CFT through LLaMA-Factory's standard SFT training scripts, with no special CFT flag; the framework surface (config file layout, launch script, hardware settings) is LLaMA-Factory's ordinary SFT-stage surface [6][7]. LLaMA-Factory's own documented distinction is full-tuning vs. freeze-tuning vs. LoRA/QLoRA/OFT/QOFT for the SFT stage [7] - CFT inherits whichever of those a run chooses; the paper's main 7B runs used full fine-tuning to reproduce the reported table (8xH100, 1 hour, 50K examples, 1 epoch) [1].
- Because CFT reduces to an SFT loss, any other framework's SFT cost analysis (e.g., trl's `SFTTrainer` memory/throughput profile [8]) applies unchanged once the critique-formatted data is assembled; this card does not carry trl's own documented defaults, since the paper's runs did not use it.

## How to use it

- Build the input format as `[question; noisy response]` -> critique text, i.e., concatenate the question and the (possibly wrong) response as the model's input and set the teacher-generated critique as the training target [1].
- Reward/label convention: there is no scalar reward. The "label" is free-text critique from the teacher model, optionally ending in a fixed verdict string; the paper's own appendix example prompts the teacher to close with `Conclusion: right/wrong [END]` [1], but the training loss covers the critique text as a whole, not just the verdict token.
- Noisy-response source: the paper found negligible performance difference between critiquing the dataset's original (WebInstruct) responses versus responses freshly sampled from the base model itself and then critiqued (57.1% vs. 55.1% average) [1] - either source works, so an unverified response pool does not need to be replaced with fresh policy samples.
- Teacher-model choice is a real knob, not incidental: switching the critique teacher from GPT-4o-1120 to the weaker GPT-4o-mini still beat the SFT-verified baseline (52.0% vs. 40.4% average) but scored lower than the GPT-4o-1120 teacher (57.1% average) (Table 7) [1] - a stronger teacher gives a consistent, not essential, further gain.

| knob | paper's value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| training examples | 50,000 |
| epochs | 1 |
| learning rate | 5e-6 |
| LR schedule | cosine decay, warm-up ratio 0.1 |
| global batch size | 512 |
| checkpoint selection | best on MATH-500 validation accuracy |
| hardware / time (7B) | 8xH100, about 1 hour |

  No shipping framework publishes its own default hyperparameters specifically for "CFT" (it is not a named trainer mode); the table above is the paper's own setting, since there is no second source to contrast it against.
- Trade-off the paper itself surfaces: mixing CFT data with additional high-quality SFT data (either mixed in one stage or applied as a second stage) slightly hurt performance relative to pure CFT training (54.8% and 51.6% vs. 57.1% average) (Table 9) [1] - combining critique training with imitation training is not free, and the paper recommends training on critique data alone rather than blending it with standard SFT data.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper does not report training-time loss curves, gradient norms, or other live monitoring signals for CFT; because it is a single-epoch SFT run, the paper's own guidance is to select the checkpoint with the highest validation score on MATH-500, checked periodically during the epoch, rather than watching a live signal [1].
- Published reference runs: the paper's own Table 2 gives the reference numbers to match - e.g. Qwen2.5-Math-7B-CFT on WebInstruct reaches a 57.1% six-benchmark average versus a 37.8% base-model average and a 50.4% best-SFT-variant average [1]; no separate raw training log was located for this method beyond the paper's reported final-checkpoint scores.
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper's own limitation section reports that a manual check of 50 GPT-4o-1120 critiques on WebInstruct found roughly 20% contain inaccuracies - misjudging correct steps, missing errors, or giving imprecise explanations - which caps how much signal the critique data itself carries [1]. The paper separately reports that a self-critique variant, where the model critiques and iteratively refines its own outputs at inference time, consistently underperformed direct inference, because of inconsistent critique standards (overlooking genuine errors or mislabeling correct solutions as wrong) and instability from the higher sampling temperature needed for diverse refinement [1] - this is a limitation of using the trained critic for iterative self-refinement, not of the training method itself.
- Named successors: Critique-GRPO extends the critique-as-signal idea into an online RL loop that combines natural-language critique feedback with numerical rewards, motivated by the finding that RL policies which have plateaued on scalar reward alone can still improve when given a natural-language critique of a failed solution [4].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own limitations section - critique-data noise (about 20% inaccuracy rate in a 50-sample manual check) and the self-critique/iterative-refinement failure described above [1]. A GitHub issues search of the paper's own repository (`TIGER-AI-Lab/CritiqueFineTuning`, all 12 issues open+closed as of 2026-08-09) surfaced one substantive maintainer-confirmed numerical trap: issue #6 argues that the MATH-500 set used for checkpoint selection is a strict subset of the 5,000-example full MATH test set the paper reports results on, so validation and test overlap; the paper's author replies that this overlap is by construction, since the reported MATH benchmark is the full 5,000-example test set rather than the MATH-500 validation subset, and that removing MATH from the result tables leaves the gains largely intact, since the largest improvements are on Minerva-Math, OlympiadBench, and AMC23 [9]. A second issue (#2) has a maintainer reply attributing reproduction gaps to checkpoint-selection timing (over-training past the MATH-500-selected best checkpoint lowers the score) and to hardware/library version (the maintainers recommend an A6000 GPU, CUDA 12.4, and vllm==0.6.6 to reproduce the paper's numbers) [9].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own ablations tie CFT's improvement specifically to learning to identify and critique errors, not to using higher-quality solution data (WebInstruct-CFT outperforms training on the higher-quality-by-construction MetaMathQA and NuminaMath under CFT, even though those datasets outperform WebInstruct under plain SFT) [1], and not to longer training sequences (a length-controlled critique subset still beats SFT) [1]. The paper also reports gains transfer beyond math: on IFEval and MT-Bench, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-CFT scores 6.49 on MT-Bench versus 5.49 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct (Table 8) [1], evidence CFT is not narrowly overfitting to the math-critique task. The paper does not claim CFT surpasses RL training outright, only that it matches RL-trained SimpleRL's average score (50.2% vs. 50.9%) at far lower compute (Table 4) [1].
