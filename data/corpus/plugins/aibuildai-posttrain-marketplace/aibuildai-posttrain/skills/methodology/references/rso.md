# RSO

Train an SFT policy offline on preference pairs whose responses are drawn, by rejection sampling, from an estimate of the reward-optimal policy instead of from the SFT policy or from arbitrary human-collected data.

**RSO** (Rejection Sampling Optimization, full name Statistical Rejection Sampling Optimization) is an offline preference-optimization method introduced by Liu et al. to source preference data from the estimated optimal policy using rejection sampling, enabling a more accurate estimation of the optimal policy than DPO or SLiC provide [1]. Its parent methods are DPO [2] and SLiC [3]: DPO directly optimizes a language model on human preference pairs via a Bradley-Terry logistic loss without a separate reward model, and SLiC fits a contrastive hinge-calibration loss on sequence pairs sampled from the SFT policy [1]. RSO keeps both loss families (renaming DPO's loss "sigmoid-norm" and proposing a normalized variant of SLiC's hinge loss, "hinge-norm") and changes only how the preference pairs are constructed: a pairwise reward-ranking model is trained on human preference data, then used together with a statistical rejection-sampling procedure to draw response pairs that approximate samples from the reward-optimal policy $\pi_r$, which are then labeled and used for training [1]. Two reasons the paper gives for this: DPO's own preference data is not sampled from the target optimal policy it is trying to fit, and SLiC's calibration data is limited to what the SFT policy produces, so neither is a maximum-likelihood-consistent way to estimate $\pi^*$ [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06657 [1].

A name collision affects this entry: the shortlist's candidate arXiv ID 2312.11456 ("Iterative Preference Learning from Human Feedback: Bridging Theory and Practice for RLHF under KL-constraint," Xiong et al.) only cites "Rejection Sampling Optimization (RSO)" as a baseline it compares against and extends [4]; it does not define the acronym. The paper that defines RSO is Liu et al., "Statistical Rejection Sampling Improves Preference Optimization," arXiv 2309.06657, whose abstract introduces "a novel offline approach called Statistical Rejection Sampling Optimization (RSO)" [1]. This was confirmed two ways: 2309.06657's own abstract names and defines RSO [1], and independently, the trl documentation's DPOTrainer loss-type table attributes its `"hinge"` loss to "the RSO authors" and links the term directly to arXiv 2309.06657, not to 2312.11456 [5]. This card is therefore written against 2309.06657, the paper the shortlist lists as an alternate.

RSO's most direct adoption is as a foil that motivated a named successor: Xiong et al. build "multi-step RSO," an iterative rejection-sampling-based offline DPO algorithm explicitly motivated by RSO's finding that rejection sampling improves DPO, and report it beating both DPO and single-step RSO on their Anthropic-HH-style dialogue benchmark: offline gold reward 2.59 (multi-step RSO) versus 2.25 (RSO) versus 2.15 (DPO), with gold win rate 0.63 versus 0.54 versus 0.50 [4, Table 2]. The originating paper's own comparison, with a T5-large policy on Reddit TL;DR, shows RSO's `rso-sample-rank` construction beating both `direct` (DPO's own data choice) and `sft-sample-rank` (SLiC's own data choice) under the same loss: sigmoid-norm loss scores AutoSxS win rate 71.86% with `rso-sample-rank` versus 69.02% with `sft-sample-rank` and 67.72% with `direct`, and hinge-norm loss scores 70.84% versus 68.46% versus 66.63% [1, Table 1]. Semantic Scholar's citations endpoint for arXiv 2309.06657 returns citing papers ordered by recency, not citation count, and paginates 100 at a time; among the 100 most recently returned citing-paper titles (offset 0, fetched 2026-08-09), none is a landmark production LLM system report (e.g. Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen, GPT-family) naming RSO as its post-training method [6] - this covers only that 100-paper slice, not the full citing set. RSO's main traceable influence found by this card is the multi-step RSO extension above and the `loss_type="hinge"` option shipped in trl's DPOTrainer. Lineage in one line: DPO / SLiC (2023 [2][3]) -> RSO (2023 [1]) -> multi-step RSO (Xiong et al., 2023/2024 [4]).

**When to pick it**: pick RSO over plain DPO [2] when you can afford to run an SFT policy plus a trained reward-ranking model to generate fresh preference pairs before each offline training run, and you want those pairs to approximate the reward-optimal policy rather than reusing whatever policy produced your raw preference data [1]. It is still offline: unlike online RL methods such as PPO [7], which sample from the live policy and update on those samples inside the training loop, RSO's rejection sampling happens once, upfront, to build a static dataset that is then trained on with a DPO/SLiC-style loss [1]. Compared to the plain best-of-N/top-k-over-N reranking used by AnthropicHH and Llama2 (keep the single highest-reward sample and discard the rest), RSO's rejection sampling is a generalization: the paper shows top-k-over-N is the $\beta\to0$ limit of its acceptance rule, and argues that limit trusts the reward model too much without regularization toward the SFT policy [1].

**Variant of**: DPO [2] and SLiC [3]; RSO reuses their loss functions and changes only the preference-pair construction step [1].

**Data it needs**: a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) policy to sample from, a trained pairwise reward-ranking model to score and rank candidates, and prompts to sample completions for (from the SFT training distribution) [1]. Offline: for each prompt the paper samples 64 candidate responses from the SFT policy with temperature 0.7 and top-k 40, sub-samples 8 via rejection sampling, and constructs preference pairs from that subset - none of this happens inside the policy-training loop, and the resulting labeled pairs form a static dataset [1]. The paper's main experiments use T5-large (770M) SFT policies and an 11B T5-XXL pairwise reward-ranking model on Reddit TL;DR (117K/6K/6K SFT examples, 93K human preference examples) and AnthropicHH-helpful (161K/9K examples) [1].

**Extra models**: a pairwise reward-ranking model is required to score and rank the rejection-sampling candidates - RSO's definition depends on it, unlike DPO which has no reward model [1]. A frozen reference policy is also required at training time: RSO's sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm losses both normalize by $\pi_{sft}(y|x)$ in the denominator, the same reference-model dependence DPO has [1]. No value network. No library trains RSO end to end (see Shipped by and Cost).

**Shipped by**: no framework I checked implements RSO's rejection-sampling data-construction step. trl's `DPOTrainer` exposes `loss_type="hinge"`, which its own docs attribute to "the RSO authors" using a hinge loss on normalized likelihood from the SLiC paper, with `beta` playing the role of the reciprocal margin [5]; reading `trl/trainer/dpo_trainer.py` at commit `85709ab` (main, fetched 2026-08-09) confirms the loss is `relu(1 - beta * delta_score)`, matching RSO's hinge-norm formula (Eq. 10) [1][5]. That option only ships the loss function, not the upstream reward-model-guided rejection sampling that builds the preference pairs - a user still has to write that pipeline themselves (see Cost). verl has no DPO or RSO trainer: its `verl/trainer/config` directory (commit `87c68d2`, main, fetched 2026-08-09) contains actor/critic/PPO/rollout configs but no DPO- or RSO-named entry [8], and its documentation site returns a 404 for `/algo/dpo.html` [9]. Building RSO from scratch means two separable jobs: an offline data pipeline (sample N candidates per prompt from the SFT policy, score with a reward model, run Algorithm 1's acceptance/rejection loop) plus a DPO/SLiC-style offline trainer to consume the resulting pairs - not a new online sampling loop [1].

## How it works

Each round, offline: fit a reward-ranking model on human preference data; use it and the SFT policy to rejection-sample preference pairs approximating $\pi_r$; label the sampled pairs; fit the policy on them with a DPO- or SLiC-style loss [1].

**Reward model.** RSO trains a pairwise text-to-text ranking model $\rho_\psi(x, y_1, y_2)$ that outputs the probability response $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$; a pointwise reward is then derived by comparing any $y$ against a fixed baseline response $y_b$ with score 0 [1, Eq. 7]:

$$ r_\psi(x, y) = \operatorname{logit}\big(\rho_\psi(x, y, y_b)\big) $$

**Optimal policy target.** Starting from a reward $r$ and a KL-constrained-reward-maximization objective shared with RLHF, the closed-form optimal policy is [1, Eq. 4]:

$$ \pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{sft}(y|x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big) $$

**Statistical rejection sampling (Algorithm 1).** To draw samples from $\pi_{r_\psi}$ using $\pi_{sft}$ as the proposal distribution, given a pool of candidate responses with rewards $r_\psi(x,y)$, the paper's own Python implementation repeatedly computes the current maximum reward `max_reward` among not-yet-accepted candidates, draws $u \sim U[0,1]$ for each remaining candidate, and accepts it if $u < \exp\big((r_\psi(x,y) - \text{max\_reward})/\beta\big)$, otherwise rejects and retries with the updated max [1, Algorithm 1]. Worked example: four candidates with rewards $\{2, 1, 0, -1\}$ and $\beta = 0.5$; with `max_reward` $=2$, the acceptance probabilities are $\exp(0/0.5)=1$ (100%), $\exp(-1/0.5)\approx0.135$ (13.5%), $\exp(-2/0.5)\approx0.018$ (1.8%), and $\exp(-3/0.5)\approx0.0025$ (0.25%) - the highest-reward candidate is always accepted and acceptance probability falls off exponentially with the reward gap to the current best, scaled by $1/\beta$ [1, Algorithm 1 & Theorem 1]. As $\beta\to\infty$ every sampled candidate is accepted (recovering `sft-sample-rank`); as $\beta\to0$ only the single highest-reward candidate is accepted, which the paper identifies as the top-k-over-N / best-of-N rejection sampling used by AnthropicHH and Llama2 [1].

**Loss functions.** RSO reframes DPO's loss as "sigmoid-norm" and decouples its inverse-temperature $\gamma$ from the KL coefficient $\beta$ used in sampling [1, Eq. 8]:

$$ L_{\text{sigmoid-norm}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} \log\sigma\!\Big(\gamma\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{sft}(y_w|x)} - \gamma\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{sft}(y_l|x)}\Big) $$

and defines a normalized hinge loss, "hinge-norm," as the SVM counterpart of sigmoid-norm [1, Eq. 10]:

$$ L_{\text{hinge-norm}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} \max\!\Big(0,\ 1 - \gamma\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{sft}(y_w|x)} + \gamma\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{sft}(y_l|x)}\Big) $$

The method's own ablation runs both losses over three preference-pair distributions - `direct` (DPO's raw human data), `sft-sample-rank` (SLiC's SFT-sampled pairs), and `rso-sample-rank` (the paper's contribution) - and finds `rso-sample-rank` best under either loss [1, Table 1]. Both trl and (via the `"hinge"` loss_type) the trl ecosystem ship the loss functions [5]; no framework ships the `rso-sample-rank` data-construction step (see Shipped by).

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: the rejection-sampling stage is a separate, upfront offline job, not part of the training loop. It needs $N$ SFT-policy generations and $N$ reward-model scoring passes per prompt to draw a subsample (the paper uses $N=64$ candidates subsampled to 8) [1]; the training stage then runs a standard DPO/SLiC-style forward-backward pass over the resulting pairs, which needs only the policy and a frozen reference model forward pass, with no value network [1].
- Memory: during data construction, the reward-ranking model and the SFT policy must both be resident (the paper's ranking model, an 11B T5-XXL, is far larger than its 770M T5-large policy) [1]; during training, memory is the usual policy-plus-frozen-reference footprint shared with DPO, with no added critic [1]. A naive reading might assume the reward model must stay loaded throughout training; the method's math only requires it during the offline data-generation phase, not during the training forward/backward pass itself [1].

**In practice, per framework:**

- trl: only the loss (`loss_type="hinge"` in `DPOTrainer`) is shipped, so the practical cost of the data-construction phase (running the SFT policy and a reward model to build the rso-sample-rank dataset) is not covered by trl's own cost model for `DPOTrainer` and must be estimated from the standalone reward-model and generation costs a user's own pipeline incurs [5]. Once the offline dataset exists, `DPOTrainer`'s cost is governed by the same reference-model-loading and batch-size settings as any other `loss_type`; per-key detail belongs on trl's own trainer card, not here.
- verl: not applicable - no DPO or RSO trainer exists in the codebase at commit `87c68d2` (main, fetched 2026-08-09), so there is no framework-reported cost to cite [8][9].

## How to use it

- Prompts: from the target task's prompt distribution, matching the SFT training set (Reddit TL;DR, AnthropicHH-helpful in the paper) [1].
- Reward/label convention: train a pairwise reward-ranking model on human preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$ first; use it both to accept/reject candidates during rejection sampling and to label the final sampled pairs [1]. The paper randomly flips response order in the pairwise model's input to remove positional bias [1].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value:

| knob | paper [1] | trl `loss_type="hinge"` in `DPOTrainer` [5] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rejection-sampling $\beta$ | 0.5 (best in ablation; swept 0, 0.05, 0.5, 5) | not applicable - trl does not implement the sampling stage |
| loss temperature $\gamma$ | 0.05 (best in ablation; swept 0.005, 0.05, 0.5) | `beta` argument, described as the reciprocal of the margin; framework default not checked here |
| candidates sampled per prompt (before subsampling) | 64 | not applicable |
| candidates kept per prompt | 8 | not applicable |
| sampling temperature / top-k for candidate generation | 0.7 / 40 | not applicable |
| learning rate | 1e-5 (Adafactor) | not checked |
| batch size | 32 | not checked |

  Because no framework ships the sampling stage, every row above except $\gamma$/`beta` is a paper-only value with no framework anchor to compare against; a builder reproducing RSO has to choose these directly from the paper.
- Trade-off from the paper's own ablation: sampling more candidates and adding tournament-style ranking is not uniformly better - `rso-8-sample-first-round-rank` (92.37% proxy reward, 71.86% AutoSxS) beats `rso-8-sample-tournament-rank` on AutoSxS (71.69%) even though tournament ranking raises proxy reward (93.35%), and scaling from 8 to 64 sampled candidates under `sft-*-sample-rank` improves AutoSxS only marginally (68.51% to 68.84% for first-round-rank) [1, Table 2].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: RSO is an offline method with no live training-loop signal defined by the paper; the ablation studies report validation-time proxy reward win rate (against the reward-ranking model) and AutoSxS win rate (PaLM 2-L few-shot judge) as the metrics used to pick $\gamma$ and $\beta$, and note that hinge loss showed reward hacking on Reddit TL;DR - higher proxy-reward win rate but lower AutoSxS than the other losses - which the paper flags as a warning sign to watch for when a loss's proxy-reward metric diverges from its judge-based metric [1].
- Published reference runs: the paper's own Table 1 (T5-large) and Table 3 (T5-XXL) numbers above are the reference curves to compare a reproduction against, both reported with Proxy Reward, Gold Reward, and AutoSxS win rates on Reddit TL;DR and AnthropicHH [1]; the paper reports raw ablation curves with 95% confidence intervals for $\gamma$ and $\beta$ sweeps in its Figure 3, but the underlying logs were not published alongside the paper as far as this card's sources show.
- Degeneracies and defaults: the $\beta\to0$ limit degenerates to best-of-N (accept only the single highest-reward candidate), which the paper explicitly warns has an unregularized reward-hacking failure mode because it trusts the reward model too much without balancing toward the SFT policy [1]; the $\beta\to\infty$ limit degenerates to plain SFT-policy sampling with no rejection at all, discarding RSO's whole contribution [1].
- Named successors: multi-step RSO (Xiong et al.) iterates the reward-model-fit-then-rejection-sample loop multiple rounds instead of once, and reports higher gold reward and win rate than single-step RSO on their benchmark (2.59 vs. 2.25 gold reward, 0.63 vs. 0.54 gold win rate) [4, Table 2].
- Known failure modes: Xiong et al.'s own analysis of RSO (not RSO's own paper) identifies a practical acceptance-rate problem: the rejection rate is $1-\exp(-\eta^{-1}(R(x)-r(x,a)))$, and for a reward gap of 1 with $\eta=0.1$ the expected acceptance rate falls to about 0.00004, meaning almost all sampled candidates are rejected and, under a fixed sampling budget, the accepted set may fail to approximate the target distribution well enough, which they report can make trained models perform worse than plain DPO [4]. RSO's own paper lists studying "online variants" as future work, implying the published method is offline-only by design and does not itself address on-policy operation [1].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own conclusion frames the contribution narrowly as a better sampling strategy for constructing preference pairs, not a new loss or a new capability - RSO's gains over DPO and SLiC in Table 1 and Table 4 come entirely from which preference pairs are trained on, holding the loss function fixed [1]. It does not claim gains from a novel objective, and the paper explicitly leaves other loss functions, larger-scale decoding, and non-human feedback as unstudied future work [1].
