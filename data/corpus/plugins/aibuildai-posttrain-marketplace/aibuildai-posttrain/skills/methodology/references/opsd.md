# OPSD

Same model plays teacher and student: the teacher conditions on the ground-truth solution, the student sees only the problem, and training matches their next-token distributions along the student's own on-policy rollout.

**OPSD** (On-Policy Self-Distillation) is a method for fine-tuning an LLM on a reasoning dataset of problem/reference-solution pairs, introduced by Zhao et al. in "Self-Distilled Reasoner: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Language Models" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18734), which frames it as "a learning algorithm where a single LLM acts as both teacher and student with different contexts" [1]. Its parent is on-policy distillation, defined by GKD (Agarwal et al., "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes") as training a student on its own sampled trajectories while a teacher model supplies dense, token-level supervision [2]; the Thinking Machines Lab blog post "On-Policy Distillation" popularized the same recipe with an external teacher in industry practice [3]. OPSD's mechanism: instantiate two conditional distributions from one shared-weight model — a teacher $p_T(\cdot\mid x,y^\star)$ that sees the problem $x$ plus a reference solution $y^\star$, and a student $p_S(\cdot\mid x)$ that sees only $x$; the student samples a rollout on-policy, and training minimizes the per-token divergence between the two distributions evaluated along that rollout, with gradients flowing only through the student [1]. The paper gives three reasons for the design: on-policy distillation normally needs a separate, often larger, teacher model, which OPSD removes by reusing the same weights under different context [1]; RLVR methods such as GRPO sample a whole group of rollouts per prompt, which is computationally expensive, gives a sparse binary reward applied uniformly across all tokens, and yields zero gradient when every sample in a group gets the same reward [1]; and reasoning datasets already carry ground-truth solutions that on-policy distillation with an external teacher does not exploit [1]. This paper was confirmed as the defining source by a top-cited check across three arXiv candidates named "On-Policy Self-Distillation" / "OPSD": it carries 215 citations against 1 and 0 for the other two, and shares its own name and acronym exactly.

Because this method was posted 2026-01-26, no landmark production system's adoption of it is documented yet; the paper's own results are the only reported gains. On Qwen3-1.7B, OPSD raises the AIME24/AIME25/HMMT25 average from a 37.1 base to 43.4, beating both +SFT (35.8) and +GRPO (37.7) trained on the same data (Table 2) [1]; at 8B the gap narrows (61.8 base to 64.8 OPSD vs. 64.0 GRPO) but OPSD still leads (Table 2) [1]. The paper's headline efficiency claim is that OPSD reaches this with a single rollout of at most 1024 tokens per prompt over 100 steps, versus GRPO's 8 rollouts of up to 16k tokens over 500 steps, because more than half of GRPO's reward-groups have zero standard deviation within 100 steps and contribute no gradient on this dataset (Figure 3) [1]. Lineage in one line: classic off-policy KD (Hinton et al., cited in [1]'s Section 2.1 background discussion, which follows a Table 1 checkmark comparison of SFT/GRPO/on-policy distillation/OPSD across four properties) / on-policy distillation (GKD, 2023 [2]) -> OPSD (2026 [1]); concurrently, SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026) and SDFT (Shenfeld et al., 2026) explore related self-distillation ideas but are described in the paper as concurrent work, not as fixes to a documented OPSD bias, so no named successor is identified here [1].

**When to pick it**: pick OPSD when every training prompt has a verified reference solution (an answer or a reference chain-of-thought), the base model is already capable enough to "rationalize" that reference when it is shown in context, and you want dense per-token supervision without paying for a second, larger teacher model or for GRPO-style multi-rollout groups [1]. Contrast with the parent, on-policy distillation via GKD [2]: OPSD needs no separate teacher checkpoint, at the cost of only working as well as the same model can rationalize privileged information [1]. Nearest offline alternative is SFT, which the paper treats as off-policy distillation on fixed expert trajectories; in its own Table 2, SFT on the same OpenThoughts data degrades performance relative to the base model, which the authors attribute to fine-tuning on concise solutions that shorten test-time generation [1]. Nearest online neighbor is GRPO [4]: also fully on-policy, but scores a whole sampled group per prompt with one binary outcome reward per rollout (8 rollouts of up to 16k tokens in the paper's baseline), versus OPSD's single rollout of at most 1024 tokens with a per-token divergence signal (Table 6) [1].

**Variant of**: on-policy distillation (GKD [2]), with the external teacher model replaced by the same weights under a different, privileged-information prompt [1].

**Data it needs**: problem/reference-solution pairs $(x, y^\star)$, where $y^\star$ may include chain-of-thought reasoning — not preference pairs and not a scalar reward per answer. The paper's main runs sample up to 30K such pairs from the mathematical-reasoning subset of OpenThoughts [1]. On-policy: the student's single rollout per prompt is sampled fresh from the current policy at every step, never replayed from a stored buffer [1].

**Extra models**: by the method's own definition (Eq. 1/8), no second model is needed — teacher and student are the same parameters $\theta$ under different conditioning [1]. In practice the paper's own implementation departs from that: it fixes the teacher to the initial policy rather than the currently-updating one, "as we find this helps stabilize training and implicitly acts as regularization to prevent excessive deviation from the initial policy" [1], which functions as a frozen reference copy despite never being described as one. In the reference LoRA implementation this frozen teacher costs no extra full model copy: a maintainer confirmed the teacher is realized by disabling the LoRA adapter on the same base weights rather than loading a second model, so the "fixed teacher" is base-model-plus-no-adapter, not a separate checkpoint (commit `7448751`, GitHub issue #21, read 2026-08-09) [5]. See Cost.

**Shipped by**: no library ships an `OPSDTrainer`. The only implementation is the paper's own repository (github.com/siyan-zhao/OPSD), which provides `opsd_trainer.py`/`OPSDTrainer`, and whose README states the code "uses trl's experimental GOLD trainer as a base" (commit `7448751`, read 2026-08-09) [5]. That base, `trl.experimental.gold.GOLDTrainer`, is itself an on-policy-distillation trainer under trl's `experimental` namespace (unpinned `main` docs) that expects a separately-loaded `teacher_model` argument and does not natively support per-example privileged-context conditioning for a self-distillation setup [6]; the nearest supported trl entry point for the parent method is `trl.experimental.gkd.GKDTrainer`, also under `experimental` [7]. Building OPSD from scratch on top of either would mean adding a second forward pass per training step that reuses the student's weights under a different prompt (the reference solution appended), plus the paper's per-token pointwise divergence clip — a loss and data-collation change on top of an existing on-policy-KD trainer, not a new sampling loop, since the on-policy rollout and teacher-forward machinery already exist in GOLD/GKD [1][5][6].

## How it works

Each step: sample a minibatch of $(x, y^\star)$ pairs; for each, the student draws one on-policy rollout $\hat{y} \sim p_S(\cdot\mid x)$; both policies evaluate that rollout to produce next-token distributions at every position; the per-token divergence between them is averaged over the rollout and over the minibatch, and $\theta$ is updated to minimize it (Algorithm 1) [1].

**The main objective** [1], Eq. 6 and 8:

$$ D\bigl(p_T \,\|\, p_S\bigr)(\hat{y}\mid x) \triangleq \frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|} D\Bigl(p_T(\cdot\mid x, y^\star, \hat{y}_{<n}) \;\big\|\; p_S(\cdot\mid x, \hat{y}_{<n})\Bigr) $$

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)\sim\mathcal{S}}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim p_S(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[D(p_T\|p_S)(\hat{y}\mid x)\bigr]\Bigr] $$

$D$ is a per-token distribution divergence, evaluated at every position $n$ of the student's own rollout $\hat{y}$, over the model's full vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$; the sequence is normalized by $1/|\hat{y}|$ [1]. The paper leaves $D$ generic and instantiates it as the generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence [1], Eq. 7:

$$ \operatorname{JSD}_\beta(p_T\|p_S) = \beta\, D_{KL}(p_T\|m) + (1-\beta)\, D_{KL}(p_S\|m), \qquad m = \beta p_T + (1-\beta) p_S $$

the paper does not derive forward/reverse KL as limiting cases of Eq. 7; instead it ablates forward KL, reverse KL, and JSD at $\beta=0.5$ as three separately-defined objectives (Table 3) [1]. On Qwen3-1.7B/AIME25, from a 36.7 base, forward KL reaches 43.9 at step 50 and 41.1 at step 100; reverse KL reaches 37.5 at step 50 and falls to 35.0 at step 100, below the base; JSD at $\beta=0.5$ reaches 36.9 at step 50 and 39.0 at step 100 (Table 3) [1]. Only reverse KL's trajectory ends below the base; forward KL gives the largest gain at either checkpoint, and the paper adopts it for all remaining experiments [1]. The reference implementation's `beta` flag follows the opposite convention from the paper's $\beta$ notation for the mixture weight — `beta=0` selects forward KL in the code — so a knob named `beta` in this codebase is not read the same direction as the paper's $\beta$ in Eq. 7 (commit `7448751`) [5].

**Per-token pointwise divergence clipping** [1]. The paper reports that token-level divergence is skewed: a small set of "stylistic" tokens (e.g. connectives) draws much higher divergence than mathematically meaningful tokens, dominating the training signal (Table 5). Writing the $f$-divergence contribution of vocabulary entry $v$ at position $n$ as

$$ \ell_{n,v}^{(f)} = p_T(v\mid\cdot)\, f\!\left(\frac{p_S(v\mid\cdot)}{p_T(v\mid\cdot)}\right), $$

the clipped divergence sums a per-entry minimum with a threshold $\tau$:

$$ D_{\mathrm{clip}}^{(f)}(p_T\|p_S) = \frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\min\!\left(\ell_{n,v}^{(f)},\,\tau\right) $$

Concretely, on Qwen3-1.7B with the paper's chosen student/teacher generation-mode pairing, the mean per-token KL is 0.85 for "style" tokens versus 0.14 for "math" tokens and 0.25 for "other" — style tokens carry roughly 6x the divergence of math tokens on that measurement (Table 5, averaged over 10 problems) [1]. The paper does not give a numeric value for $\tau$ in its main text and states it "didn't conduct tuning for the clipping parameter $\tau$" [1]; the released 1.7B training script sets `--jsd_token_clip 0.05` (commit `7448751`) [5].

**Alternative objective — sampled-token policy gradient** [1], Eq. 9, following the on-policy distillation formulation of the Thinking Machines blog post [3]: define a per-token advantage from log-probabilities at the token the student actually sampled,

$$ A_n(x,\hat{y}) = \log p_T(\hat{y}_n\mid x, y^\star, \hat{y}_{<n}) - \log p_S(\hat{y}_n\mid x, \hat{y}_{<n}) $$

and optimize $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}\bigl[\tfrac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_n A_n(x,\hat{y})\,\log p_S(\hat{y}_n\mid x,\hat{y}_{<n})\bigr]$ [1]. Full-vocabulary logit distillation (the main variant, following GKD [2]) outperforms this sampled-token variant on Qwen3-4B: 84.1 vs. 82.1 pass@8 on AIME25, 60.0 vs. 57.3 on HMMT25 with a 2048-token generation budget (Table 4) [1], at the cost of higher peak memory from storing vocabulary-sized logits at every position [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: one rollout of length $\lvert\hat{y}\rvert$ per prompt (the paper's main runs cap this at 1024 tokens), versus GRPO's group of $G$ rollouts per prompt (8 rollouts of up to 16k tokens in the paper's baseline) [1]. Each training step needs a teacher forward pass over the same rollout under the privileged-context prompt, in addition to the student's forward/backward pass; the teacher pass carries no gradient [1].
- Memory (naive reading of Eq. 8): a whole extra model's worth of weights for the teacher, since $p_T$ and $p_S$ are formally separate conditional distributions. This is the naive reading the practice layer corrects — see below.
- Memory (full-vocabulary variant): storing $D(p_T\|p_S)$ over the entire vocabulary at every position means both teacher and student logits of shape (sequence length x vocabulary size) are held at once; the paper names this as the cost of the full-vocabulary variant relative to the sampled-token one (Section 4.3.5) [1].

**In practice, per implementation:**

- Reference repo (github.com/siyan-zhao/OPSD, commit `7448751`) [5]: all reported experiments use LoRA (rank 64, alpha 128) rather than full fine-tuning [1]. With `--fixed_teacher`, a maintainer confirmed in a closed issue that the teacher is realized by disabling the LoRA adapter on the same base model, not by loading a second checkpoint, so the naive "two full models" reading of Eq. 8 does not hold for this LoRA setup [5]. The README reports the Qwen3-1.7B reproduction training in about 15 minutes on 4xH100 [5]; runs use gradient checkpointing and Flash Attention 2 (Appendix B) [1].
- No `trl`-supported (non-experimental) trainer implements this method; `trl.experimental.gold.GOLDTrainer` and `trl.experimental.gkd.GKDTrainer`, the closest shipped building blocks, both load `teacher_model` as a distinct model argument rather than the same weights under a second context, so their memory profile (a genuinely separate teacher checkpoint) does not carry over to how the reference OPSD repo actually runs [6][7].

## How to use it

- Data: a set of $(x, y^\star)$ problem/reference-solution pairs; $y^\star$ may be a final answer or a full reference chain-of-thought (the paper uses OpenThoughts math CoT solutions) [1]. There is no reward function or preference label to design — the training signal is the token-level divergence between the two conditionings of the same model, not a scalar score [1].
- Generation-mode pairing matters: on Qwen3 models with a thinking-mode switch, the paper finds that a thinking-mode-off student paired with a thinking-mode-on teacher gives the largest KL on math-relevant tokens across all three model sizes tested, and adopts this pairing for its main experiments (Table 5) [1].
- Key knobs, reference paper/repo values only (this method has one implementation, so there is no cross-framework table; GRPO's own baseline settings from the same paper are shown alongside for scale, not as a comparable knob):

| knob | OPSD (paper Table 6 / repo script) [1][5] | GRPO baseline, same paper (Table 6) [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| learning rate | $5\times10^{-6}$ | $5\times10^{-6}$ |
| effective batch size | 32 | 32 |
| LoRA rank / alpha | 64 / 128 | 64 / 128 |
| rollouts per prompt | 1 | 8 |
| max completion length | 1024 | 16,000 |
| sampling temperature | 1.1 | 1.2 |
| training steps | 100 | 500 |
| divergence clip $\tau$ | 0.05 (1.7B script; not tuned per paper) [1][5] | not applicable |

  Two readings of this table: OPSD's 100-step budget with 1024-token rollouts is the direct source of its token-efficiency claim against GRPO's 500-step, 16k-token budget (Figure 3) [1]; and $\tau$ is explicitly un-tuned by the authors' own statement, so the 0.05 value is one working setting from the released 1.7B script, not a value the paper validated as optimal [1][5].
- Generation-length trade-off: on Qwen3-1.7B, increasing student rollout length from 1024 to 4096 tokens does not give consistent gains on AIME24/AIME25 (Figure 5); the paper attributes this to later tokens becoming more predictable to the teacher once conditioned on a long student prefix, so they contribute less divergence signal, a phenomenon also noted in the on-policy distillation blog post [1][3].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's own diagnostic is the per-token-category KL breakdown (Table 5) — a large gap between "math" and "style" category KL indicates the pointwise clip is needed to prevent stylistic tokens from dominating the gradient; Figure 4 shows that without clipping, Qwen3-1.7B accuracy on AIME24 collapses over training, and clipping prevents that collapse [1]. For the RLVR comparison point, the paper tracks the fraction of GRPO reward-groups with zero standard deviation, reporting that more than half fall into this state within 100 steps on this dataset — this is a GRPO-specific degeneracy the paper uses to argue OPSD's dense signal is more sample-efficient, not an OPSD signal itself [1].
- Published reference runs: the repository's README reports its own reproduction table for Qwen3-1.7B — Avg@12 on AIME24 rising from a 51.5 base to 57.2 at step 100, on AIME25 from 36.7 to a peak of 43.9 at step 50 before falling back to 41.1 at step 100, and on HMMT25 from 23.1 to 29.2 at step 100 (commit `7448751`) [5]; these numbers match Table 2/Table 3 in the paper [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: a maintainer-confirmed numerical trap — applying the pointwise clip per-vocabulary-entry before summing (as the released code does) can make the aggregated per-token divergence negative, and the maintainer confirmed in a closed issue that "negative loss is expected with this current version" of the per-vocab clipping, calling it more granular and empirically better than clipping after the per-token sum (GitHub issue #2, commit `7448751`, read 2026-08-09) [5]. A second closed issue confirms per-vocabulary-entry ("point-wise") clipping was chosen over token-level clipping because point-wise clipping specifically suppresses cases where the student's exploratory preference for a connective token like "wait" gets penalized against the teacher's confident choice of a token like "so," a case the maintainer says would not show high total per-token KL despite carrying a large per-vocab-entry contribution (GitHub issue #24, commit `7448751`, read 2026-08-09) [5]. A third closed issue confirms the released "fixed teacher" is implemented by disabling the LoRA adapter rather than loading a frozen full copy, and that the authors did not fully explore letting the teacher track the student's updated weights, finding a moving ("dynamic") teacher unstable in later, unpublished experiments (GitHub issue #21, commit `7448751`, read 2026-08-09) [5].
- Named successors: none identified. This is a January 2026 method; the paper itself names two concurrent works, SDPO and SDFT, exploring related self-distillation ideas, but frames both as concurrent rather than as fixes to a documented OPSD bias, and no later paper revising OPSD's own biases was found in the paper's text (search: full HTML text of [1], "concurrently" paragraph in Section 5, read 2026-08-09) [1].
- Known failure modes, from the paper's own Limitations appendix [1]: experiments are limited to models up to 8B parameters, and it is an open question whether the method's gains hold beyond that scale; the framework as defined does not explicitly use correctness verification of the student's generated answers, only distribution matching to the teacher; and if a problem exceeds the base model's comprehension, the teacher cannot give meaningful supervision even with the ground-truth solution available, which the authors suggest could be addressed by curriculum learning over problem difficulty (Appendix A) [1].
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's comparison is against SFT and GRPO trained on the same 30K-problem OpenThoughts subset, where OPSD raises the AIME24/AIME25/HMMT25 average at every one of the three tested Qwen3 scales while using fewer sampled tokens (Table 2) [1]; the paper does not claim OPSD adds reasoning capability beyond what GRPO or a strong SFT run could reach with more compute, only that it reaches comparable or better accuracy at lower token cost within the 100-step budget it tests, and it explicitly disclaims scale beyond 8B parameters [1].
