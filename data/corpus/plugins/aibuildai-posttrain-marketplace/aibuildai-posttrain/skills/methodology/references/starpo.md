# StarPO

Take PPO or GRPO's update rule and apply it to a whole multi-turn agent trajectory instead of one prompt-response pair: roll out several full `<think>...</think><answer>...</answer>`-interaction episodes per starting state, score each episode's cumulative reward, and update the policy on the entire token sequence at once.

**StarPO** (State-Thinking-Actions-Reward Policy Optimization) is a trajectory-level RL framework for training LLM agents in multi-turn, stochastic environments, introduced in the paper "RAGEN: Understanding Self-Evolution in LLM Agents via Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning" as an approach meant to generalize agent RL to the level of the full trajectory rather than a single action [1]. Its parent methods are PPO [2] and GRPO [3], both defined for a single-turn objective $J_{step}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim D, a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}[R(s,a)]$ over one prompt-response pair [1]; StarPO instead maximizes $J_{StarPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{M,\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$, the expected reward of a whole trajectory $\tau = \{s_0, a_0, r_0, \dots, s_K\}$ generated by interacting with an MDP $M$ over up to $K$ turns [1]. The paper gives two reasons for treating the trajectory as the unit of optimization rather than the single action: LLM agents must make sequential decisions and adapt to stochastic environment feedback across turns, which single-turn RL does not model, and treating reasoning, actions, and environment feedback as one coherent unit lets the same framework support flexible reward assignment and prompt-rollout structures [1]. StarPO is a framework, not a single fixed loss: the paper instantiates it with a trajectory-level PPO objective (a critic estimates token-level value/advantage) and a trajectory-level GRPO objective (the trajectory reward is normalized within a group of rollouts from the same initial state, critic-free) [1]. Confirmed by top-cited match under an exact title search on the paper's own name for the framework.

The paper's own case study is the adoption result: training a Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct model with the StarPO-S variant (StarPO plus filtering, described below) on Sokoban and Frozen Lake reached 20.70% and 21.48% success, comparable to zero-shot GPT-4o (27.73% / 26.56%) and zero-shot Qwen2.5-72B (19.53% / 23.83%) on the same tasks, despite the trained model being two orders of magnitude smaller (Table 5) [1]. StarPO is implemented and used by its own reference system, RAGEN, which the paper's abstract presents as the accompanying modular codebase for training and evaluating agents with StarPO [1]; RAGEN's own GitHub page positions the project similarly, as a general-purpose RL codebase built around the StarPO framework for training reasoning agents [4]. Lineage in one line: PPO (2017 [2]) / GRPO (DeepSeekMath, 2024 [3]) -> StarPO, trajectory-level generalization for agents (RAGEN, 2025 [1]) -> paper's own stabilized variant StarPO-S, which borrows KL-removal and clip-higher from DAPO (2025 [5]) -> RAGEN-2 diagnostics for template collapse (2026, referenced but not covered by this card) [4].

**When to pick it**: pick StarPO when the training signal comes from a multi-turn, possibly stochastic environment (an agent that must act, observe a new state, and act again before a reward resolves) rather than from a single prompt-response pair [1]. Its parents PPO [2] and GRPO [3] are the right choice instead when the task is single-turn (one prompt, one scored response) — StarPO's own paper frames itself as extending exactly these two objectives from $J_{step}$ to $J_{StarPO}$ [1]. There is no offline alternative: the paper always samples trajectories on-policy or from a short-lived replay buffer under $\pi_{old}$, and does not define or discuss a variant that trains from a static, pre-collected trajectory dataset [1]. The nearest online neighbor is single-turn PPO/GRPO run per-turn instead of per-trajectory; the paper's own baseline comparison (Figure 3) shows such vanilla adaptations achieve early gains but collapse in symbolic multi-turn tasks (Bandit, Sokoban), which is the finding that motivates StarPO-S [1].

**Variant of**: generalizes PPO [2] and GRPO [3] from single-turn to trajectory-level optimization [1]; not a single fixed variant of either.

**Data it needs**: no fixed dataset of prompt-response or preference pairs. What's needed is an environment: a set of initial states $s_0$ (e.g., 8 prompts per batch in the paper's main runs) and a transition/reward function $P(\cdot|s_t,a_t)$ that returns a reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$ given an action [1]. Training scale in the paper's main experiments: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (3B for WebShop), $P{=}8$ initial states per batch, $N{=}16$ trajectories per state, up to 5 turns and 10 actions per episode, 100-200 rollout-update iterations [1]. Always on-policy: every training iteration generates fresh trajectories from the current (or very recently frozen) policy; it does not train from a static offline trajectory set [1].

**Extra models**: depends on which instantiation is used. The GRPO instantiation needs no critic — its advantage is the trajectory reward normalized by the group's own mean and std [1]. The PPO instantiation needs a critic (value network) that estimates token-level value/advantage over the trajectory [1]. A frozen reference model for a KL penalty is used in the paper's main "vanilla StarPO" baseline runs, with KL coefficient 0.001 using the k1 estimator, but the paper's own StarPO-S variant removes the KL loss term during training (tracking KL post-hoc only), following DAPO [5], as one of its three stabilizing changes [1]. The reference implementation, RAGEN, ships with `use_ref: True` but `kl_loss_coef: 0.000` and `use_kl_loss: False` as its shipped defaults — a reference model is loaded but not applied as a loss term unless configured on [6]. See Cost for the detail.

**Shipped by**: RAGEN (`ragen.trainer.agent_trainer.RayAgentTrainer`, subclassing verl's `RayPPOTrainer`) is the paper's own reference implementation and, as far as this card checked, the only shipping implementation of StarPO; it dispatches to GAE (PPO) or GRPO advantage estimation via a single `algorithm.adv_estimator` config key [7]. RAGEN's own changelog states that verl was integrated into its codebase as a submodule for the v1 release [4]; this card did not fetch or check verl's or trl's own documentation, so it makes no claim about what either project states about itself. No other library was found offering an entry point for StarPO's trajectory-level rollout-and-reward accounting; building it elsewhere would require a new multi-turn rollout loop feeding an existing single-turn PPO/GRPO update step, not just a new loss. Building StarPO on any other framework would mean writing a new rollout loop that runs multi-turn environment interaction to produce a trajectory before the framework's existing advantage/update step ever fires — a new sampling loop, not just a new loss.

## How it works

The loop in one line: from each initial state $s_0$, roll out $N$ full multi-turn trajectories under the current policy, score each trajectory, turn scores into advantages, then update the policy on the entire trajectory's tokens.

**MDP formulation.** At each turn $t$ the policy generates $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t,\tau_{<t})$, and the environment returns $(r_t, s_{t+1}) \sim P(\cdot|s_t,a_t)$, where $\tau_{<t} = \{s_0,a_0,r_0,\dots,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},r_{t-1}\}$ is the interaction history; this continues for a maximum horizon $K$, yielding a full trajectory $\tau = \{s_0,a_0,r_0,\dots,s_K\}$ [1]. Each turn's action is a reasoning-guided structured output $a_t^T = \texttt{<think>...</think><answer>}a_t\texttt{</answer>}$, where $a_t$ is the environment-executable sub-action [1].

**The trajectory objective** [1]:

$$ J_{StarPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{M,\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[R(\tau)\right] $$

$M$ is the MDP, $\tau$ is a full reasoning-augmented interaction sequence, and $R(\tau)$ is the cumulative reward over the whole trajectory; $\pi_\theta(\tau)$ decomposes into ordinary token-level likelihoods, so the objective is directly usable with autoregressive LLMs [1]. Each training loop uses $P$ initial states, $N$ trajectories per state, and updates with batch size $E$ over $L$ loops, for $S = L\cdot\frac{P}{E}\cdot N$ total gradient steps [1].

**PPO instantiation** [1], with $G$ trajectories in the batch, trajectory $\tau_i$ of $|\tau_i|$ tokens, and a critic-estimated token-level advantage $A_{i,t}$:

$$ J_{PPO}(\theta) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\tau_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_i|} \min\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(\tau_{i,(t)}|\tau_{i,<t})}{\pi_{old}(\tau_{i,(t)}|\tau_{i,<t})}A_{i,t},\ \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(\tau_{i,(t)}|\tau_{i,<t})}{\pi_{old}(\tau_{i,(t)}|\tau_{i,<t})},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)A_{i,t}\right] $$

**GRPO instantiation** [1]: a scalar reward $R(\tau_i)$ is assigned to the whole trajectory, and the group-normalized advantage is applied to every token:

$$ \hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{R(\tau_i)-\operatorname{mean}(\{R(\tau_1),\dots,R(\tau_G)\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{R(\tau_1),\dots,R(\tau_G)\})} $$

with $J_{GRPO}(\theta)$ identical in shape to $J_{PPO}(\theta)$ above but with $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ in place of $A_{i,t}$ [1]. Both objectives normalize per-token loss by trajectory length $1/|\tau_i|$, the paper's own convention for both instantiations [1].

**Worked example**, GRPO instantiation, $G{=}4$ trajectories from the same initial state with trajectory rewards $\{1,0,0,0\}$ (one succeeded, three failed): mean $0.25$, population std $\approx 0.433$; the successful trajectory's tokens all get advantage $\approx +1.73$, the three failed trajectories' tokens each get $\approx -0.58$ — the same group-normalization arithmetic GRPO applies to a single response, except here every token of an entire multi-turn trajectory shares the one trajectory-level advantage value [1].

**Appendix background objective**: the paper's appendix additionally writes a general PPO objective with a KL term, $J_{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t[\min(\rho_i A_i,\hat\rho_i A_i) - \beta D_{KL}]$, and a matching GRPO form $J_{group}(\theta) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_i \min(\rho_i A_i,\hat\rho_i A_i) - \beta D_{KL}$ [1] — this KL term does not appear in the main-text trajectory-level Eq. 4/6 shown above; whether and how it is applied is a training-configuration choice, covered in Cost and How to use it.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: because the unit of optimization is a whole trajectory, generating one training example costs up to $K$ sequential turns of environment interaction and LLM generation per rollout, not one generation call — the paper's main runs allow up to 5 turns and 10 actions per episode, times $N{=}16$ rollouts per one of $P{=}8$ initial states each iteration [1]. The forward/backward training pass then runs over all tokens of all $G$ trajectories in the batch, once per instantiation (PPO or GRPO) [1].
- Memory: the PPO instantiation holds a critic that is trained to estimate token-level value and advantage $A_{i,t}$ — an extra trained model beyond the policy [1]. The GRPO instantiation is critic-free, holding only the policy [1]. Whichever instantiation is used, a KL term against a frozen reference model — when enabled — adds one more model held in memory (forward-only, no gradients) [1].
- A naive reading of $J_{StarPO}$ implies the full trajectory's token sequence, including every intermediate environment observation string, must be held and backpropagated through at once; this is a per-implementation batching detail, not a formula consequence, and is not checked at the theory level here.

**In practice, per framework:**

- RAGEN [6][7]: `micro_batch_size_per_gpu: 1` and `ppo_mini_batch_size: 32` bound the actual forward/backward batch shape independent of the $G$ used in the paper's formula; rollout uses vLLM with `gpu_memory_utilization: 0.3` and `max_model_len: 3600` [6]. The critic is only instantiated when `algorithm.adv_estimator: gae` (PPO); it is absent under `adv_estimator: grpo` [6][7]. `actor_rollout_ref.actor.use_ref: True` loads a reference-model copy by shipped default regardless of instantiation, but `use_kl_loss: False` and `kl_loss_coef: 0.000` mean it is not used in the loss unless explicitly turned on — so the reference model's memory cost is paid by default even when its loss contribution is zero [6].

## How to use it

- Environment, not dataset: define an MDP with a reward function per turn or per episode; the paper's four environments are Bandit (single-turn, stochastic), Sokoban (multi-turn, deterministic), Frozen Lake (multi-turn, stochastic), and WebShop (multi-turn, open-domain) [1]. RAGEN ships ten built-in environments, described on its GitHub page as Sokoban, FrozenLake, WebShop, DeepCoder, SearchQA, Lean, Bandit, Countdown, MetaMathQA, and Sudoku [4].
- Reward convention: the paper adds a format penalty of $-0.1$ whenever the agent fails to produce valid `<think>...</think><answer>...</answer>` structure, on top of the environment's task reward [1]; RAGEN ships this as `es_manager.format_penalty: -0.1` [6].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value):

| knob | RAGEN shipped default [6] | paper's main-run value [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| trajectories per initial state (group size $N$) | `es_manager.train.group_size` 16 | 16 |
| initial states per batch ($P$) | `es_manager.train.env_groups` 8 | 8 |
| max turns per episode | `agent_proxy.max_turn` 5 | 5 |
| max actions per turn | `agent_proxy.max_actions_per_turn` 2 | 5 (best in the paper's own ablation, Table 2) |
| advantage estimator | `algorithm.adv_estimator` gae (PPO) | both PPO and GRPO reported (Fig. 3) |
| GAE $\gamma$, $\lambda$ | `algorithm.gamma` 1.0, `algorithm.lam` 1.0 | $\gamma{=}1.0$, $\lambda{=}1.0$ |
| entropy coefficient | `actor.entropy_coeff` 0.001 | $\beta{=}0.001$ |
| KL coefficient (vanilla) | `algorithm.kl_ctrl.kl_coef` 0.000, `use_kl_loss` False | 0.001 (k1 estimator), vanilla StarPO baseline runs |
| clip range | `clip_ratio_low` 0.2, `clip_ratio_high` 0.28 | not stated for vanilla StarPO in main text; 0.2/0.28 introduced as the StarPO-S "Clip-Higher" ablation, following DAPO [5] |
| rollout filtering (StarPO-S) | `rollout_filter_value` 1.0 (keeps 100%, i.e. off by default) | 25% kept, adopted as StarPO-S default |
| max prompt/response length | `data.max_prompt_length`/`max_response_length` null (unset); `actor_rollout_ref.rollout.response_length` 400, commented "single-turn response length" [6] | response length capped at 200 words (tokens) per the Bandit prompt template [1] |

  RAGEN's shipped clip range (0.2/0.28) matches the paper's own StarPO-S ablation value, not a separate framework choice — the repository ships the paper's stabilized settings as its baseline config, while KL and rollout filtering ship off by default even though the paper's main "vanilla StarPO" figures (Figure 3) ran with KL on and no filtering [1][6].
- Trade-off named by the paper itself: task diversity (number of distinct initial states) trades against responses per state at fixed batch size; the paper's own ablation (Table 1) found 4 responses per prompt generalized best, not the 16 used in its main runs, because more distinct prompts with multiple rollouts each let the agent contrast outcomes under similar conditions [1].
- Action budget trade-off: allowing more actions per turn extends planning horizon but the paper's own ablation (Table 2) found performance peaks at 5-6 actions per turn and degrades at 7, attributed to noisy transitions and diluted reward feedback [1].
- Rollout freshness: the paper's Online-$k$ ablation reuses one rollout batch for $k$ consecutive updates; Online-1 (fully on-policy, fresh rollouts every update) converges fastest and generalizes best, and the paper states this reflects that learning is most effective when trajectories match the agent's current behavior [1].

## While it runs

- **Signals and their healthy shapes**, all first-hand from the paper's Figure 4 analysis [1]: in-group reward standard deviation is an early collapse indicator — in FrozenLake-PPO it drops sharply at step 40, well before the reward mean collapses at step 90; in Bandit-PPO it bottoms out around step 70, preceding the reward peak (not a collapse) at step 120; in Sokoban-PPO, std and mean collapse together near step 10, which the paper reads as early saturation. Gradient-norm spikes mark irreversible collapse — observed at step 170 (Bandit), 110 (Sokoban), and 90 (FrozenLake) — after which the paper reports recovery becomes unlikely. Entropy should follow a stable decay during effective learning, as seen in FrozenLake-GRPO; rapid or erratic entropy changes instead correlate with collapsed reasoning behavior, as seen in GRPO on Bandit and Sokoban.
- **Published reference runs**: the paper's own Figure 3 (PPO vs. GRPO success rate over 100-200 steps on all four environments) and Figure 6 (StarPO-S vs. vanilla StarPO) are the paper's published known-good/known-bad curves [1]; RAGEN also links a public Weights & Biases experiment log on its GitHub page [4].
- **Degeneracies and defaults**: the paper's own central failure mode is "Echo Trap" — symbolic environments (Bandit, Sokoban) show early gains then collapse into repetitive, deterministic reasoning templates, which the paper likens to the self-consuming-generation collapse described by Shumailov et al. [1]. A trajectory group with uniform reward (all successes or all failures) drives GRPO's std to zero, the same degeneracy documented for group-relative advantages generally [3]; the paper's own remedy is filtering low-reward-variance initial states out of training (its outcome-uncertainty filter, $U(\pi_\theta,M,s_0)=\operatorname{Std}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s_0)}[R(\tau)]$), keeping the top 25% by variance by default in StarPO-S [1]. RAGEN's shipped config exposes this same filter (`rollout_filter_metric: reward_variance`) but ships it disabled (`rollout_filter_value: 1.0`, i.e. keep-all) rather than at the paper's 25% default [6].
- **Named successors**: the paper's own stabilized variant, StarPO-S, adds three fixes on top of vanilla StarPO — uncertainty-based trajectory filtering (above), a critic (used to smooth PPO's reward estimates), and two gradient-shaping techniques borrowed from DAPO [5]: removing the KL loss term, and asymmetric "Clip-Higher" clipping ($\varepsilon_{low}{=}0.2$, $\varepsilon_{high}{=}0.28$) that lets the policy move further on high-reward rollouts [1]. RAGEN's GitHub page describes a further successor, RAGEN-2 (arXiv 2604.06268, not covered by this card), adding SNR-adaptive filtering and mutual-information diagnostics for "template collapse", a failure mode the page says entropy alone cannot detect [4].
- **Known failure modes**: from the paper's results/discussion analysis of its own multi-turn runs (not its formal Limitations section, see below) — in multi-turn settings the paper finds reasoning traces shrink in length over training, and that removing the `<think>` reasoning step often performs comparably to or better than keeping it; it presents examples (its Appendix L) of models generating incoherent or hallucinated reasoning that still receives high reward [1]. The paper attributes this to sparse, delayed multi-turn reward that fails to differentiate reasoning quality from outcome success, and proposes only a partial fix (a small format penalty for missing `<think>`/`<answer>` structure), leaving finer-grained reasoning-aware reward design as future work [1]. The paper's own formal Conclusions and Limitations section separately states three scope limitations: a focus on relatively small-scale tasks, the omission of established RL practices like replay buffers, and the absence of multimodal tasks [1]. No GitHub issue search was performed for this card; this bullet reports only what the paper itself states.
- **What the gain is — and is not**: the paper's own Table 3 ablation shows reasoning traces measurably help generalization in the single-turn Bandit task (including a counter-intuitive reward-reversed variant), but in multi-turn Sokoban/Frozen Lake removing the `<think>` reasoning step gives comparable or better performance than keeping it — StarPO's trajectory-level optimization does not by itself make multi-turn reasoning emerge or persist; the paper states this requires reward design that the base method does not provide [1].
