# LAT (Targeted Latent Adversarial Training)

Item home: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15549 [1]

Perturb an LLM's residual-stream activations toward a specific unwanted output, then fine-tune the model to resist that perturbation, so the fix reaches behavior that ordinary input-space adversarial training misses.

**LAT** (latent adversarial training) here refers to the targeted variant introduced by Sheshadri et al. in "Latent Adversarial Training Improves Robustness to Persistent Harmful Behaviors in LLMs": the paper trains an adversary to modify an LLM's latent activations "to elicit specific outputs corresponding to unwanted behaviors," then fine-tunes the model under that perturbation [1]. Its direct parent is untargeted LAT from Casper et al., "Defending Against Unforeseen Failure Modes with Latent Adversarial Training," which is "conceptually the same as AT, except adversarial perturbations are applied to the model's latent state instead of its inputs" and where the adversary maximizes the target loss rather than steering toward a chosen target [2]. Casper et al.'s own parent is standard input-space adversarial training (AT) with an $L_p$-norm constraint via projected gradient descent (PGD), following Madry et al. [3][2]. The paper gives two reasons for going to targeted, latent-space perturbations rather than input-space AT: models represent unwanted concepts at a higher level of abstraction in their latent space than in raw text, so attacking the latent space can better reach the underlying circuitry; and a targeted adversary that steers toward a specific undesirable completion lets the method be pointed at a known failure mode (a backdoor's harmful response, a jailbreak's compliance, a piece of unlearned knowledge) rather than only providing generic robustness [1].

Adoption is mostly within the authors' own line of safety work rather than production training stacks: the same team's public chat demo and Hugging Face checkpoints (`LLM-LAT` org) showcase an LAT-hardened Llama-3-8B refusal model [4], and the method is presented as augmenting existing techniques rather than replacing them. In the paper's own results, targeted LAT on top of refusal training and embedding-space AT (RT-EAT-LAT) matched or beat the R2D2 jailbreak-robustness baseline on 8 of 9 non-adversarial/attack measures for Llama-2-7B-chat while using about 700x fewer forward/backward passes and roughly 36x fewer GPU-hours (Table 2) [1]; on WMDP biology/cyber unlearning, adding LAT to gradient ascent and to RMU improved unlearning and cut how much of the unlearned gap re-opened under a 20-step few-shot relearning attack, from 74.7% of the gap closing back for the non-LAT methods to 59.9% for the LAT-augmented ones (Table 5, Section 4.3.2) [1]. Lineage: AT/PGD (Madry et al., 2017 [3]) -> untargeted LAT (Casper et al., 2024 [2]) -> targeted LAT (Sheshadri et al., 2024, this card [1]) -> concurrent/related latent-space defenses the paper points to as future comparisons: representation noising (Rosati et al.) and Tamirisa et al.'s work, neither of which this card verifies further [1]. The right paper was confirmed by checking the exact-title search hit against the shortlist's top-cited-collision-pick against two unrelated same-acronym papers (a vehicle-routing "LaT" and a personality-trait "LAT" alignment paper, both 0-citation) [per shortlist row].

**When to pick it**: pick targeted LAT when you already have a training method (refusal training, DPO, gradient ascent, RMU) that is not fully removing a specific unwanted behavior (a backdoor, a jailbreak, a piece of forget-set knowledge) and you can specify what the undesired output looks like; it is added on top of that method's own loss, not a replacement for it [1]. Prefer plain input-space AT [3] when there is no reason to believe the failure survives at the representation level, since AT is simpler and needs no access to hidden layers. Prefer untargeted LAT [2] when you want generic robustness to failure classes you cannot specify a target for. There is no offline, preference-pair analogue of LAT in this literature; the nearest online neighbor by role is R2D2 (Mazeika et al.), a much more compute-expensive input-space jailbreak-robustness baseline that the paper's LAT variant matches or beats at a fraction of the compute [1].

**Variant of**: untargeted LAT (Casper et al., 2024) [2], itself a latent-space variant of PGD-based adversarial training (Madry et al., 2017) [3].

**Data it needs**: a paired dataset of prompts with a desired (harmless/correct) completion and an undesired (harmful/target) completion, $(x_i, y_i, \tilde y_i) \sim D_p$, plus a separate benign dataset $D_b$ used to stabilize training via interleaved fine-tuning or a KL penalty [1]. Scale in the paper's jailbreak experiments used a Self-Instruct-style generated dataset of harmful/harmless prompt-completion triples plus UltraChat as $D_b$ [1]; the unlearning experiments used the WMDP bio/cyber forget corpora with WikiText or Alpaca as the retain/benign set [1]. It is on-policy in the adversary: the perturbation $\delta$ is re-optimized against the current model at every training step via PGD, and only the outer model update is standard supervised/preference fine-tuning [1].

**Extra models**: a reference/base copy of the model is needed only when the benign-preservation term is a KL penalty against the original weights, which the paper uses for its Llama-3 experiments (Llama-2 experiments instead interleave fine-tuning batches on the benign set, needing no extra model) [1]. No separate value network or reward model is part of the method's own definition; whichever base method LAT augments (DPO, RMU, refusal training) brings its own model requirements, unchanged by adding LAT.

**Shipped by**: trl's documented trainer index (fetched from its `main` docs, 2026-08-09) lists no latent-adversarial-training trainer among its exported trainers (dpo, grpo, kto, reward, rloo, sft, bco, cpo, gkd, ppo, prm, xpo, and others) [7]. No other framework was checked, so this card claims only that trl does not ship it. The authors' own research repository (`aengusl/latent-adversarial-training`, MIT license, notebook-based, no pip package, read at commit `9dc3f37`) is the only public implementation and is what backs the paper's public model checkpoints [5]. Building it on top of an existing trainer requires a new sampling loop, not just a loss addition: PGD must run its own forward/backward passes to update $\delta$ against the frozen-for-that-step model at chosen layers before every outer optimizer step, a hook trl's documented trainers do not expose [7].

## How it works

Each step: run PGD to find a bounded perturbation $\delta$ to the model's latent activations that pushes the model's output toward the undesired target; then update the model parameters so the desired output is favored even under that perturbation [1].

**The objective**, viewing the LLM as $\mathrm{LLM}_\theta(x_i) = (g_\theta \circ f_\theta)(x_i)$ with $f_\theta$ a feature extractor producing latents $\ell_i = f_\theta(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{s\times d}$ and $g_\theta$ mapping latents to an output distribution, and $\alpha_{\delta_i}$ an attack function with parameters $\delta_i$ modifying either inputs or latents [1]:

$$ \min_\theta \sum_i L\big(g_\theta(\alpha_{\delta_i}(f_\theta(x_i))), y_i\big) \qquad \text{(Eq. 1, LAT training objective)} $$

Untargeted LAT (the parent) sets the inner attacker to $\max_{\delta_i} L(g_\theta(\alpha_{\delta_i}(f_\theta(x_i))), y_i)$, i.e. it maximizes loss on the desired label [1][2]. Targeted LAT instead sets:

$$ \min_{\delta_i} L\big(g_\theta(\alpha_{\delta_i}(f_\theta(x_i))), \tilde y_i\big) \qquad \text{(Eq. 2, targeted attacker)} $$

where $\tilde y_i$ is the undesired target completion; the adversary now minimizes loss toward that target rather than maximizing loss on the correct one [1]. The perturbation is constrained by $\|\delta_i\|_2 \le \epsilon$ [1]. Concretely for the refusal-training augmentation (RT-EAT-LAT), with harmful completion $c_i$ and harmless (refusal) completion $r_i$, the attacker minimizes

$$ L_{\text{attack}} = -\log P(r_i \mid g_\theta(f_\theta(x_i)+\delta_i)) + -\log(1-P(c_i \mid g_\theta(f_\theta(x_i)+\delta_i))) \qquad \text{(Eq. 3)} $$

— i.e. it steers the perturbed model toward the harmless completion's low-probability region and the harmful completion's high-probability region — after which the model is trained on the defense loss $L_{\text{defense}} = -\log P(c_i\mid\cdot) + -\log(1-P(r_i\mid\cdot))$ under that fixed $\delta_i$, summed with a benign loss $L_{\text{benign}}$ that is either standard SFT loss on $D_b$ or a KL penalty to the original model [1] (Eqs. 4-6). Worked example: with $\epsilon=1.0$ and four attacked layers, PGD takes several gradient steps on $\delta$ (bounded to $\|\delta\|_2\le\epsilon$ per layer) to make the perturbed model prefer the harmful completion $c_i$ over the refusal $r_i$; the outer step then updates $\theta$ so that, even with that same $\delta$ applied, the model prefers $r_i$ over $c_i$ — repeating this adversary/defender alternation is what the paper calls the LAT loop (Figure 1) [1].

Two configuration choices the paper treats as part of the method rather than as tunable per-run defaults: perturbing four evenly spaced layers rather than one, found empirically to outperform single-layer attacks, and choosing $\epsilon$ by a grid search over {0.5, 1.0, 2.5, 6.0, 10.0} on Llama-2 jailbreak robustness, then holding that value fixed across later experiments and models [1]. trl ships no variant of this loop [7]; there is no framework default to compare against.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: the PGD inner loop adds $T_\delta$ extra forward/backward passes per training step to optimize $\delta$, on top of the regular forward/backward pass of the outer model update [2]. Compared to input-space AT, LAT's extra passes run only from the attacked layer to the output rather than through the whole network, so it is described as "somewhat cheaper" than full-input AT per attack step, with the exact savings depending on which layer is attacked [2]. Memory-wise, LAT needs no extra trained model (no value network, no second full copy with its own optimizer state) beyond what the base method (DPO, RMU, refusal training) already needs; a KL-penalty variant needs one frozen reference copy held for forward passes only, no gradients or optimizer state on it [1].

**In practice**: the only public implementation is the authors' research code, not a maintained training framework, so there is no framework-level cost claim (batch-size defaults, GPU-hour benchmarks beyond the paper's own numbers) to cite separately from the paper. The paper's own measured cost is the relevant number: RT-EAT-LAT used about 9x the forward/backward passes of plain refusal training (RT) and about 700x fewer such passes than the R2D2 baseline, translating to roughly 36x fewer GPU-hours than R2D2 while training on a single A100 or H100 in under 12 hours per run [1].

## How to use it

Data preparation follows whichever base method is being augmented: for the jailbreak-robustness experiments the paper builds a triple dataset of (prompt, harmful completion, harmless completion) via Self-Instruct-style generation seeded from AdvBench, filtered and diversity-subsampled by clustering BERT embeddings [1]; for backdoor removal it reuses Anthropic HH-RLHF's helpful/harmless splits with DPO, attacking hidden layers 4, 12, 20, 28 [1]; for WMDP unlearning it reuses the WMDP bio/cyber forget corpora with WikiText or Alpaca as the retain/benign set, attacking the layer immediately preceding the RMU layer [1]. There is no separate framework default to contrast against the paper's values, since no framework ships this method; the paper's own settings are the only anchor:

| knob | paper's value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| attacked layers | 4 evenly spaced layers (e.g. 8, 16, 24, 30 for jailbreak; 4, 12, 20, 28 for backdoor removal) |
| perturbation bound $\epsilon$ | grid-searched over {0.5, 1.0, 2.5, 6.0, 10.0} on Llama-2, then held fixed |
| perturbation norm | $L_2$, per attacked layer independently |
| PGD algorithm | projected gradient descent, per Madry et al. [3] |
| benign-preservation method | interleaved SFT on $D_b$ (Llama-2 runs) or KL penalty to original model (Llama-3 runs), the latter found empirically better [1] |
| DPO backdoor-removal steps | 1024 steps, batch size 16 [1] |

The main trade-off a run designer faces is which benign-preservation term to use: interleaving is simpler but the paper reports the KL-penalty variant generally performed better on Llama-3 [1]. A second trade-off is layer/epsilon selection, which the paper found influential and non-trivial to tune (see While it runs) [1].

## While it runs

- **Signals and their healthy shapes**: the paper reports general-capability measures (MMLU, MT-Bench, benign-request compliance rate) alongside attack-success-rate measures per attack type (direct request, PAIR, prefilling, AutoPrompt, GCG, many-shot+GCG) as the pair to track jointly — LAT is judged as improving the Pareto frontier between these two axes, not either alone [1]. For unlearning runs, the paired signals are general performance (MMLU, AGIEval) against unlearning effectiveness (WMDP bio/cyber multiple-choice accuracy, with 25% as the random-guess floor) [1]. No framework logs these under standardized metric names, since none ships the method.
- **Published reference runs**: Table 2 (jailbreak robustness, Llama-2-7B-chat and Llama-3-8B-instruct) and Table 5 (WMDP unlearning, Zephyr-7B-beta) in the paper are the known-good numeric references, each reported as mean ± standard error over n=3 seeds [1]; there is no separate framework-published reference curve to compare against.
- **Degeneracies and defaults**: the repository maintainer, replying to an open issue asking how the perturbation bound was finally chosen, states that $\epsilon$ is a swept hyperparameter exposed as a flag to the `projected_gradient_descent` function in `lat_methods.py`, and that the sweep "often" settled on 1 in their own runs — i.e. the paper's stated grid search does not resolve to one universal value, and a user must re-sweep for their own model [6]. The paper itself reports that dataset, layer, and perturbation-size selection were all found to be influential and that interleaving benign fine-tuning and NaN handling were necessary for stable training, without giving a single safe default [1].
- **Named successors**: none identified from the paper's own text; it lists concurrent, not successor, latent-space defenses (Zou et al., Rosati et al., Tamirisa et al.) that it proposes comparing against in future work, not methods that supersede LAT [1].
- **Known failure modes**: the paper's own limitations section says LAT is also challenging to configure and tune, names layer selection, perturbation size, dataset choice, and NaN handling as the practical pain points, and restricts its own claims to residual-stream PGD perturbations on models under 10B parameters, explicitly leaving larger-model behavior unconfirmed [1]. Searching the implementation repository's issues (`aengusl/latent-adversarial-training`, all 11 issues, read 2026-08-09) surfaces no additional reported numerical trap beyond the epsilon-tuning difficulty above; two other open issues ask about RMU hyperparameters and Llama-2 model availability without maintainer replies at the time of reading [6].
- **What the gain is - and is not**: the paper's own unlearning results show that even LAT-augmented unlearning is not robust to a simple few-shot relearning attack — repeatedly training on a single 2-example batch for 20 steps recovered an average 15.7 percentage points of WMDP performance; LAT only reduces how much of the gap reopens (59.9% of the gap for LAT methods vs. 74.7% for non-LAT), it does not close the relearning vulnerability [1]. The discussion section is explicit that the results "support but do not yet confirm" the hypothesis that LAT removes the underlying neural circuitry responsible for the unwanted behavior, rather than merely suppressing it more thoroughly than input-space methods [1].
