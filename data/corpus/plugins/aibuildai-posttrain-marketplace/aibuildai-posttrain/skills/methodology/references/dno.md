# DNO

https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715

Iterated DPO with a preference-oracle-ranked, large-margin training set: each round, sample several completions per prompt from the current policy, rank them (plus a strong teacher's answer) with a pairwise preference judge, keep only the clearly-better-vs-clearly-worse pairs, and run one epoch of a DPO-style contrastive update before repeating.

**DNO** (Direct Nash Optimization) is a post-training method for aligning an LLM policy to a general (possibly non-transitive) pairwise preference function, introduced by Rosset et al. as "a provable and scalable algorithm that marries the simplicity and stability of contrastive learning with theoretical generality from optimizing general preferences" [1]. Its parent is DPO [2]: the paper's practical algorithm, DNO-Prct, is explicitly described as an iterative version of DPO, reusing DPO's binary cross-entropy contrastive loss but replacing DPO's single fixed offline pair dataset with a loop that resamples from the current policy, re-ranks with a preference oracle, and re-derives new training pairs every round [1]. The paper gives three reasons for moving past reward-maximization RLHF: a scalar (e.g. Bradley-Terry) reward cannot express intransitive or cyclic preferences between outputs; even where a scalar reward is a faithful fit, optimizing it can produce degenerate policies; and a reward model trained once goes "stale" as the policy distribution shifts under training, inviting reward hacking [1]. Conceptually, DNO's general form (Algorithm 1) is framed as approximating the Nash equilibrium (Minimax Winner) of a two-player preference game via a batched, single-timescale contrastive regression, in contrast to the two-timescale, purely on-policy no-regret updates of SPO [3] and Nash-MD [4], which the paper argues are harder to implement stably [1].

The paper reports its own gains, not third-party adoption: in a controlled setting on UltraFeedback, three iterations of DNO reached a 22.59% length-controlled (LC) AlpacaEval 2.0 win-rate against GPT-4-Turbo and 24.97% raw win-rate, versus 19.49%/18.22% for 4-epoch offline DPO on the same large-margin-filtered data, 16.18%/16.13% for a SPIN [5] reimplementation, and 10.76%/6.99% for the Orca-2.5 SFT starting point (Table 1) [1]. Scaling to ~10x more prompts (DNO-More-Data), six iterations reached a 33.05% LC win-rate and 34.38% raw win-rate, the paper's headline "33% on AlpacaEval 2.0... an absolute gain of 26% (7% → 33%)" result, beating Mistral Large, Self-Rewarding LM 70B [6], and GPT-4-0314 with a 7B model (Table 4, abstract) [1]. A search of the two frameworks this corpus tracks (trl's exported trainers and verl's algorithm docs, both checked below) found no trainer or recipe named for DNO, and the paper's own text names no later method that explicitly builds on it; lineage in one line: DPO (2023 [2]) -> DNO (2024 [1]), with no confirmed named successor found in this search.

**When to pick it**: pick DNO when you have query access to a pairwise or ranking preference oracle (a strong judge model, not a trained scalar reward model) and want DPO's training stability with fresh, on-policy self-improvement rounds instead of one fixed offline pair set [1]. Prefer plain DPO [2] when you already have a static preference-pair dataset and don't want to run a sampling-and-judging loop between training rounds. Prefer SPO [3] or Nash-MD [4] — the two-player, no-regret algorithms DNO is designed to approximate without their two-timescale instability [1] — only if you specifically need their last-iteration theoretical guarantees and can tolerate a purely on-policy, non-batched update loop.

**Variant of**: DPO [2], run iteratively with on-policy resampling and preference-oracle-based pair construction; theoretically positioned as a batched, single-timescale approximation to the soft-policy-iteration updates that SPO [3] uses to approach the Nash equilibrium of a general preference function [1].

**Data it needs**: prompts only as the fixed dataset (the paper's main runs used UltraFeedback's ~60k prompts, split into three non-overlapping ~19.6-19.9k partitions, one per iteration; the scaled run used ~600k prompts from a mixture of six datasets) [1]. No pre-existing preference labels are required beyond the oracle: each round the current policy is sampled for K completions per prompt (K = 5 in the paper's main runs, using top-p 0.95 and temperature 0.7), plus one "gold" teacher (GPT-4-Turbo) response is added, and a preference oracle (GPT-4-Turbo scoring 1-6 on a rubric of correctness/expert-knowledge/conciseness) ranks the K+1 candidates to build large-margin pairs [1]. On-policy: fresh completions are sampled from the current iteration's policy every round; it is not offline.

**Extra models**: no value network and, in the paper's own definition, no separately trained reward model — the preference judge is queried, not trained [1]. It does require (a) an external preference oracle/judge (GPT-4-Turbo in the paper, forward passes/API calls only) [1], and (b) a reference model for each round's DPO-style loss — but unlike standard DPO/GRPO, which fix one reference for the whole run, DNO-Prct's reference for iteration t's update is that iteration's own pre-update policy πt, so the "reference" snapshot is replaced every iteration rather than held fixed [1]. Details in Cost.

**Shipped by**: no library implements DNO. Checked at trl commit `dd7cbaf` (2026-07-31): the top-level `trl/__init__.py` exports only `DPOTrainer`, `GRPOTrainer`, `KTOTrainer`, `RewardTrainer`, `RLOOTrainer`, `SFTTrainer` — no Nash- or DNO-named trainer, and even trl's experimental folder (`trl/experimental/nash_md`) ships Nash-MD [4], not DNO [8][9]. verl's algorithm documentation index lists `ppo`, `grpo`, `dapo`, `spin`, `sppo`, `dro`, `gpg`, `opd`, `opo`, `otb`, `entropy`, `baseline`, `rollout_corr`, `rollout_corr_math`, `dppo` — no DNO or Nash entry [10]. Building it would take a data pipeline, not a new loss: since DNO-Prct's Eq. 13 is exactly DPO's binary cross-entropy loss with the previous iteration's policy as reference, building DNO means wrapping an existing DPO trainer (e.g. trl's `DPOTrainer` [7][8]) in an outer loop that (1) samples K completions per prompt from the current policy, (2) ranks them plus a teacher response with a preference oracle, (3) filters to large-margin winner/loser pairs with decayed replay of prior iterations, and (4) re-runs one DPO epoch with a fresh reference snapshot — a new sampling-and-filtering harness around an unmodified DPO loss.

## How it works

The loop, one line: sample K on-policy completions per prompt, rank them plus a gold/teacher response with a preference oracle, filter to large-margin pairs, run one epoch of a DPO-style contrastive update with the previous policy as reference, repeat for T iterations, and return the best checkpoint on validation data [1].

**Algorithm 1 (general form)**, the objective DNO approximates [1, Eq. 8]:

$$ \pi_{t+1} \leftarrow \operatorname*{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_1,y_2)\sim D_t}\Big[\sigma\big(r_t(x,y_1)-r_t(x,y_2)\big)\,\log\sigma\Big(\eta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_1|x)}{\pi_t(y_1|x)}-\eta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_2|x)}{\pi_t(y_2|x)}\Big) $$
$$ +\ \sigma\big(r_t(x,y_2)-r_t(x,y_1)\big)\,\log\sigma\Big(\eta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_2|x)}{\pi_t(y_2|x)}-\eta\log\tfrac{\pi(y_1|x)}{\pi_t(y_1|x)}\Big)\Big] $$

Here $r_t(x,y) := \mathbb{E}_{y'\sim\pi_t(\cdot|x)}[P(y \succ y' \mid x)]$ is the expected win-rate of $y$ against the current policy's own outputs under the general preference function $P$; $\eta$ is a learning-rate-like scale; $\pi_t$ is the current policy, used both to generate the pair-sampling distributions $\mu_{1,t},\mu_{2,t}$ (which can be on- or off-policy) and as the reference in the log-ratio terms; $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid [1]. The paper derives Eq. 8 by regressing an "internal reward" $r_{\pi,t}(x,y):=\eta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}+\eta Z_t(x)$ toward the true expected-win-rate difference $r_t$, avoiding the need to estimate the partition function $Z_t(x)$ explicitly [1, Sec. 3.1].

**Algorithm 2 (DNO-Prct, what is actually run)**, using sampled binary preferences instead of the continuous $r_t$ [1, Eq. 13]:

$$ \pi_{t+1} \leftarrow \operatorname*{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_t^+,y_t^-)\sim D_{t+1}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\tilde\eta\log\frac{\pi(y_t^+|x)}{\pi_t(y_t^+|x)}-\tilde\eta\log\frac{\pi(y_t^-|x)}{\pi_t(y_t^-|x)}\right)\right] $$

This is exactly DPO's loss form [2] with $\pi_t$ (the previous iteration's policy) standing in for DPO's fixed reference model, and $\tilde\eta$ playing the role of DPO's $\beta$ [1]. $D_{t+1}$ is built each round by: sampling K outputs $\{y_t^1,\dots,y_t^K\}\sim\pi_t(\cdot|x)$ per prompt; ranking these plus a gold teacher output by pairwise win-rate under the preference oracle; and keeping only "large-margin" $(y_t^+,y_t^-)$ pairs, because the binary $\{0,1\}$ sampled preference used in Eq. 13 is a crude proxy for the continuous $\sigma(r_t(x,y^+)-r_t(x,y^-))\approx 1$ that Eq. 8 needs, and that approximation only holds when the true win-rate gap is large [1, Sec. 4].

Worked example: take $\tilde\eta=0.1$, a prompt where a candidate scored 6/6 is paired against one scored 3/6 (margin 3, above the paper's $\geq 2$-point threshold, so a valid pair) [1]. Suppose $\log\frac{\pi(y^+|x)}{\pi_t(y^+|x)}=0.5$ and $\log\frac{\pi(y^-|x)}{\pi_t(y^-|x)}=-0.3$. The bracketed term is $0.1\times(0.5-(-0.3))=0.08$, so the loss is $-\log\sigma(0.08)\approx -\log(0.520)\approx 0.654$; raising the model's relative log-probability of $y^+$ over $y^-$ shrinks this loss, exactly as in standard DPO [2].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**:

- Time: each prompt requires K on-policy completions per iteration (K = 5 in the paper's main runs) [1], i.e. K times the generation cost of a single-sample method per prompt, plus the preference-oracle's pairwise ranking calls over up to K+1 candidates — a per-prompt judging cost that iterative DPO-style methods with a trained scalar reward model (e.g. Table 1's Offline DPO baseline) do not pay repeatedly, since DNO's oracle must be queried again every iteration on fresh samples [1]. The training pass itself is a single DPO-style forward/backward over the filtered pairs, at most one epoch per iteration [1].
- Memory: no value network is trained (unlike PPO-style RLHF) [1]. Each iteration's update needs the trained policy plus a frozen reference — but that reference is refreshed to $\pi_t$ every iteration rather than held fixed for the whole run, so across T iterations the naive reading is T reference snapshots must exist at some point (though only one need be resident at a time, sequentially) — a different cost shape from single-reference DPO/GRPO runs, which this theory layer flags but does not resolve.

**In practice, per framework**:

- No framework ships DNO end-to-end (see Shipped by), so there is no framework-specific complexity claim to report for the outer loop; the paper itself states it uses the open-source TRL library's implementation to run its experiments, for the DPO-style update step [1], meaning the per-round training cost in practice is whatever trl's `DPOTrainer` costs, which is out of scope for this card (see the trl framework skill).

## How to use it

- Prompts: from the target instruction-following distribution; no pre-collected preference pairs are required, only prompts, since pairs are generated by the loop itself. The paper used UltraFeedback's ~60k prompts (main runs) or ~600k prompts from six mixed datasets (scaled run) [1].
- Reward/label convention: the preference oracle (GPT-4-Turbo in the paper) scores each candidate 1-6 on an additive rubric covering correctness, expert knowledge, and conciseness, among other dimensions [1]. Only candidates scoring 5 or 6 may be positives; negatives must score at least 2 points lower, relaxed to 1 point lower when the positive is a fresh on-policy student sample (since teacher outputs rarely score below 5) [1]. To avoid the current policy forgetting earlier corrected behavior, each iteration also replays a decaying share of prior iterations' pairs: at most 30% from iteration t-1, 15% from t-2, and so on [1].
- Key knobs. "not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value:

| knob | paper (DNO-Prct, main runs) [1] | trl `DPOTrainer` default [8] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| completions per prompt K | 5 | not applicable (K is a DNO pipeline choice, not a trl parameter) |
| contrastive-loss scale $\tilde\eta$ (paper calls it "beta") | 0.1 or 0.05 | `beta` 0.1 |
| outer optimizer learning rate | 5e-5 (5e-6 for the SFT baseline) | `learning_rate` 1e-6 |
| effective batch size | 64 | not checked |
| large-margin threshold | $\geq$2 points (out of 6), relaxed to $\geq$1 for a student-origin positive | not applicable (pipeline choice) |
| replay decay across iterations | 30% from t-1, 15% from t-2, ... | not applicable (pipeline choice) |
| epochs per iteration | at most 1, on newly constructed pairs | not applicable (pipeline choice) |
| "alpha" | 0.005 | the paper does not state what this hyperparameter controls, and current trl's `DPOConfig` (checked at commit `dd7cbaf`) has no field literally named `alpha`, so its meaning cannot be recovered from either source [1][8] |

  Reading this table: $\tilde\eta$/beta in the paper (0.05-0.1) sits close to trl's own default of 0.1 [1][8], but the outer learning rate the paper used (5e-5) is 50x higher than trl's current default (1e-6) [1][8] — carrying trl's present-day default learning rate into a DNO-style run would not reproduce the paper's setting.
- Trade-off: larger K gives the ranking step more candidates to find a large-margin pair from (and lets more of a batch survive the margin filter), at the cost of K times the generation and judging calls per prompt each iteration; the paper's ablation shows filtering to large-margin pairs measurably helps even in the one-shot offline case (Offline DPO: 11.60 vs 9.60 AlpacaEval win-rate, filtered 42k pairs vs unfiltered 63k) [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's own Table 2 tracks, per iteration, the student's best-of-K win-rate against the teacher, the average student and teacher oracle scores, and the count of new training pairs by type (teacher-beats-student, student-beats-teacher, student-beats-student); across DNO's first three iterations the student's best-of-5 win-rate against the teacher rose from 15.9% to 34.6% to 43.6%, and average student score rose from 0.486 to 1.22 to 1.90 (out of a possible teacher-anchored scale), while student-beats-teacher pairs grew from 4.2k to 19.4k to 32.4k [1]. A useful sanity signal is simply whether student-vs-teacher pairs are being generated at all: the paper's own DNO-Restrictive variant (only student-vs-student pairs are allowed to compete, never against the teacher) produces zero teacher-vs-student and student-vs-teacher pairs at every iteration by construction, and scores lower on AlpacaEval than full DNO (Table 1: 19.21 vs 24.97 raw win-rate at iteration 3) [1].
- Published reference run: Table 1's controlled UltraFeedback run (DNO reaching 22.59% LC / 24.97% raw win-rate at iteration 3, versus 19.49%/18.22% for 4-epoch offline DPO, 16.18%/16.13% for SPIN [5], and 20.44% raw win-rate for Self-Rewarding LM 70B [6]) is the paper's own head-to-head comparison table and the reference curve to compare against [1]. Table 4's scaled run (DNO-More-Data reaching 33.05% LC / 34.38% raw win-rate by iteration 6) is the paper's larger-data reference point [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: a batch with no large-margin pairs (e.g. all K+1 candidates score similarly) contributes nothing that iteration — the paper reports that the total quantity of new large-margin pairs tends to decrease as the policy improves across iterations, without enough data to say how this affects quality [1]. The paper also flags an unexplained artifact: even though the fixed teacher's outputs don't change, the oracle scores the teacher slightly lower as the student improves across iterations, and the authors state they "are not sure if this is an innocuous artifact of preference annotations, or symptomatic of a deeper problem" [1].
- Named successors: none found that explicitly build on DNO by name; a search of the paper's own related-work section finds it instead positions DNO alongside contemporaneous, not successor, methods (SPIN [5], Self-Rewarding LM [6], and the concurrent IPO-MD) [1], and the trl/verl searches used for Shipped by turned up no DNO-derived trainer.
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own ablations — collapsing the iterative loop into a one-shot offline run over the accumulated pairs (DNO-Lookahead) made AlpacaEval win-rate worse (24.97 to 18.18) even though MT-Bench improved (7.48 to 7.70), a divergence the authors attribute partly to MT-Bench's much smaller (80-example) test set [1]; and omitting student-vs-teacher comparisons entirely (DNO-Restrictive, and by the paper's characterization also SPIN and IPO-MD) measurably hinders performance relative to full DNO (Table 1, Table 2) [1]. No maintainer-issue-tracker failure modes were searched, since no library ships DNO to have an issue tracker for it.
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own analysis targets instruction-following alignment (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench) rather than reasoning capability; it separately reports OpenLLM leaderboard reasoning-task accuracy "which we do not expect to decrease" (Table 3), i.e. the design goal is no regression on reasoning, not a reasoning improvement — DNO iterations 1-3 stayed within about 2 points of the SFT baseline's OpenLLM average (0.652 to 0.675) [1].
