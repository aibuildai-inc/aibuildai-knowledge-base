# IPO

DPO's fix for its own overfitting: replace DPO's unbounded logit-of-preference objective with a direct, bounded regression on preference probabilities, so the policy stays close to the reference model even when preferences are deterministic.

**IPO** (Identity Preference Optimisation) is an offline preference-optimization method for fine-tuning an LLM policy directly on pairwise-preference data, introduced by Azar et al. as a special case of a general objective the paper calls $\Psi$PO [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036 [1]. The paper first shows that both RLHF and Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) can be written as instances of $\Psi$PO for a particular non-decreasing map $\Psi(q) = \log(q/(1-q))$, then argues this choice is unbounded and can push the optimal policy to a deterministic one even under strong KL regularization when preferences are (near-)deterministic [1]. IPO is $\Psi$PO with $\Psi$ set to the identity map, which keeps the objective bounded and, unlike DPO, always keeps the KL term effective regardless of how skewed the observed preferences are [1]. DPO, its parent, is the method that first showed a policy can be trained directly from a pairwise-preference dataset via a closed-form reward reparameterization, without a separate reward-modelling stage, using a sigmoid-of-log-ratio loss [2]. Two reasons the paper gives for IPO's existence: DPO relies on substituting pairwise preferences with a Bradley-Terry pointwise-reward model, an approximation IPO's identity-map objective bypasses by construction [1]; and DPO's unbounded loss lets it ignore the reference policy and collapse to a deterministic policy even when the regularization strength $\tau$ is large, whereas IPO's optimal policy stays controllable by $\tau$ [1]. The paper's title was confirmed by an exact match on the arXiv id in the shortlist row (2310.12036) against the arXiv abstract page's own citation metadata [1].

The paper itself reports no LLM-scale benchmark; its own empirical evidence is two small bandit examples with 3 discrete actions, optimized with softmax-parametrised policies for 18,000 Adam steps (see While it runs and the deciding numbers there) [1]. Concrete adoption is as a loss variant inside existing preference-tuning libraries rather than as a headline method in a landmark system: Hugging Face's trl ships it as `loss_type="ipo"` inside its `DPOTrainer` [3], and the trl maintainers state they confirmed a length-normalization detail not specified in the paper directly with the IPO authors, and that the paper's own reported results correspond to that normalized form [4]. Lineage in one line: RLHF/DPO's shared $\Psi$PO framing (Azar et al., 2023 [1]) -> IPO as the identity-map instance (2023 [1]) -> shipped as one selectable loss inside trl's DPOTrainer alongside DPO and later pairwise losses such as EXO and Robust DPO [3].

**When to pick it**: pick IPO over plain DPO [2] when preference labels are likely to be sparse, imbalanced across actions, or close to deterministic (e.g. small per-prompt sample counts, one response winning or losing every comparison it appears in) - DPO's objective ignores the reference policy in these regimes and converges to a degenerate deterministic answer regardless of the KL strength, while IPO's bounded loss keeps the regularization strength $\tau$ in control of how far the policy moves [1]. Nearest offline alternative for feedback that is not paired: KTO trains directly on a binary desirable/undesirable signal per response instead of preference pairs, and the KTO paper reports it matches or exceeds preference-based methods (DPO included) from 1B to 30B scale [5]. Nearest online neighbor: PPO-based RLHF, which samples fresh completions from the current policy and optimizes them against a learned reward model rather than a fixed offline dataset [1][2].

**Variant of**: DPO [2], itself framed by the IPO paper as a special case of the same $\Psi$PO objective family that IPO belongs to [1].

**Data it needs**: a dataset of prompts with a preferred and dispreferred completion per pair, $(x, y_w, y_l)$ - the same shape DPO consumes [1][2]. No scalar reward or reward model is required, only the binary preference label. Offline: the paper's own sampled-loss experiments reuse a fixed, pre-collected set of pairwise comparisons across all 18,000 optimization steps, resampling mini-batches with replacement from that fixed set rather than drawing fresh completions from the policy being trained [1].

**Extra models**: one frozen reference policy $\pi_{ref}$, used both to initialize training and inside the loss's log-ratio term [1]; trl's `DPOTrainer` loads this reference model for the `"ipo"` loss the same way it does for `"sigmoid"` DPO, since the loss is computed from the same chosen/rejected log-ratio quantities relative to a reference model [3][4]. No value network and no separately trained reward model - the paper's stated purpose is to avoid RL and reward modelling altogether [1].

**Shipped by**: trl, as `loss_type="ipo"` inside the top-level `DPOTrainer` class exported by `trl` [3][6]; no separate `IPOTrainer` class exists, IPO is one selectable loss within the general `DPOTrainer` (module `trl/trainer/dpo_trainer.py`, commit `2396dfe` on the `main` branch) [4].

## How it works

Each step, for a batch of preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$: compute the log-probability ratio between the trained policy and the reference policy for both the chosen and rejected completion, take their difference, and regress that difference toward a fixed target set by the regularization strength $\tau$ [1].

**The objective the paper optimizes** starts from the $\Psi$PO objective with $\Psi$ the identity map [1, Eq. 8]:

$$ \max_{\pi}\; p^*_\rho(\pi \succ \mu) - \tau\, D_{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{ref}) $$

where $p^*_\rho(\pi \succ \mu)$ is the total preference of policy $\pi$ over a comparison distribution $\mu$, and $\tau$ is the regularization strength controlling closeness to $\pi_{ref}$ [1]. The paper turns this into a root-finding problem and derives an empirical, sample-based loss over a dataset $D$ of $(y_w, y_l)$ pairs [1, Eq. 17]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{IPO}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{(y_w, y_l) \sim D}\left[ \left( h_\pi(y_w, y_l) - \frac{\tau^{-1}}{2} \right)^2 \right], \qquad h_\pi(y, y') = \log\!\left( \frac{\pi(y)\,\pi_{ref}(y')}{\pi(y')\,\pi_{ref}(y)} \right) $$

$h_\pi(y_w, y_l)$ is the gap between the trained policy's log-likelihood ratio of the winner to the loser, $\log(\pi(y_w)/\pi(y_l))$, and the same ratio under the reference policy, $\log(\pi_{ref}(y_w)/\pi_{ref}(y_l))$ [1]. The loss regresses this gap toward the fixed constant $\tau^{-1}/2$ rather than pushing it to $+\infty$ as DPO's sigmoid loss does when preferences are deterministic; the paper states this is exactly what keeps IPO's solution regularized toward $\pi_{ref}$ regardless of how one-sided the observed preferences are [1]. The paper's Algorithm 1 states the procedure symmetrically: starting from $\pi = \pi_{ref}$, minimize $\mathbb{E}_{(y_w,y_l,x)\sim D}\big[(h_\pi(y_w,y_l,x) - \tau^{-1}/2)^2\big]$ [1].

**Worked example.** Take $\tau = 1$ so the target is $\tau^{-1}/2 = 0.5$. Suppose for one pair the trained policy gives $\pi(y_w) = 0.7$, $\pi(y_l) = 0.3$ and the reference gives $\pi_{ref}(y_w) = 0.5$, $\pi_{ref}(y_l) = 0.5$. Then $h_\pi(y_w,y_l) = \log(0.7/0.3) - \log(0.5/0.5) = \log(2.33) \approx 0.847$. The per-pair loss is $(0.847 - 0.5)^2 \approx 0.120$. If the policy instead pushed all the way to $\pi(y_w) \to 1$, $\pi(y_l) \to 0$, the log-ratio term diverges and the squared loss grows without bound, so gradient descent is pulled back toward keeping $h_\pi$ near $0.5$, not toward the deterministic extreme - the mechanism the paper contrasts with DPO's sigmoid loss, which keeps decreasing as the log-ratio grows and so has no such interior minimum [1].

**Length normalization is not in the paper's formula.** trl's implementation of `loss_type="ipo"` divides the chosen and rejected log-ratio terms by the number of completion tokens before taking their difference, explicitly commenting that this per-token averaging is not discussed in the IPO paper, and that trl's engineers confirmed the normalized form with the IPO authors and state it matches the paper's own reported results [4]. The formula above is the paper's own (sequence-level, unnormalized) statement [1]; trl's shipped loss is the token-averaged variant [4].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: IPO needs no policy sampling and no reward model - the loss is computed directly from log-probabilities of pre-collected completions, so per step it requires one forward pass of the trained policy and one forward pass of the frozen reference policy over the chosen and rejected sequences, plus a backward pass through the trained policy only [1]. Memory holds two model copies (trained + frozen reference); the reference model needs no gradients or optimizer state. This is the same shape as DPO's training-time footprint, since IPO changes only the loss function applied to the same chosen/rejected log-ratios, not what is computed to get them [1][2].

**In practice, per framework**:
- trl `DPOTrainer` [3][6]: setting `loss_type="ipo"` reuses the same forward/backward passes and reference-model handling as every other DPO loss variant in the trainer; `beta` (trl's name for the paper's $\tau$) defaults to `0.1` [6]. No separate cost applies beyond what trl's DPOTrainer costs for any `loss_type`; framework-level batching, PEFT, and precision options belong to the trl framework card, not here.

## How to use it

Data preparation matches DPO: a dataset of `(prompt, chosen, rejected)` triples, no scalar reward and no reward model needed [1][2]. trl's `DPOTrainer` computes chosen/rejected log-probability differences against the reference model and applies whichever `loss_type` is configured, including `"ipo"` [3][6].

Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key):

| knob | trl `DPOConfig` default [6] | paper [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| loss type | `loss_type=["sigmoid"]`; set to `["ipo"]` to run IPO | fixed to the identity-map squared loss |
| regularization strength ($\tau$ / `beta`) | `beta` 0.1 - for `loss_type="ipo"` trl states this is the paper's $\tau$ | swept over multiple values in the toy examples (Sec. 5.3-5.4); no single fixed value used |
| optimizer / learning rate | AdamW, `learning_rate` 1e-6 (trl overrides the underlying `TrainingArguments` default of 5e-5 for this trainer) | Adam, learning rate 0.01, in the 3-action toy experiments |
| batch construction | not checked | mini-batch size 9, sampled uniformly with replacement from a fixed dataset, 18,000 steps |

Trade-off a run designer faces: $\tau$ (trl's `beta`) directly sets how strongly IPO pulls the policy back toward $\pi_{ref}$ - the paper's own asymptotic example shows the optimal policy interpolates smoothly between $\pi_{ref}$ (as $\tau \to \infty$) and the deterministic BT-optimal policy (as $\tau \to 0$) [1], so unlike DPO there is no regime where $\tau$ stops mattering. Because IPO reuses trl's `DPOTrainer` end to end, batch reuse, PEFT, and precision trade-offs are the same ones documented on the trl framework card, not specific to the IPO loss [3][6].

## While it runs

- **Signals and their healthy shapes**: neither the paper nor trl's own logging guide names an IPO-specific training metric beyond the chosen/rejected log-ratio quantities the loss is built from [1][4]. The general chosen/rejected reward-margin style signals trl exposes for DPO-family losses (not IPO-specific) are documented on the trl framework card; this card does not repeat them since the IPO paper gives no first-hand monitoring guidance of its own.
- **Published reference runs**: the paper reports no LLM-scale training curve; the closest thing to a reference run is Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 of the paper, learning curves of action probabilities for IPO versus DPO on two 3-action synthetic datasets, run for 18,000 Adam steps with 10 seeds and 95% confidence intervals reported [1]. Two deciding numbers from those experiments: on the "total ordering" dataset $D_1 = \{(y_a,y_b),(y_b,y_c),(y_a,y_c)\}$, the paper reports DPO converges to the fully deterministic policy for every value of $\tau$ tested, while IPO's optimal policy stays closer to $\pi_{ref}$ as $\tau$ grows [1]. On the asymptotic 2-action example with $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2)=1$, IPO's closed-form optimal policy is $\pi^*(y_1) = \sigma(0.5\tau^{-1})$, which recovers the uniform reference policy as $\tau \to \infty$ and the deterministic policy as $\tau \to 0$, whereas the paper states DPO converges to the fully deterministic policy $\pi^*(y_1)=1$ regardless of $\tau$ in the same setting [1].
- **Degeneracies and defaults**: an unobserved action pair is where DPO and IPO diverge most sharply - on the paper's $D_3 = \{(y_a,y_b),(y_b,y_a)\}$ dataset with $(y_a,y_c)$ never observed, the paper reports DPO drives the unobserved action's probability toward 0 regardless of $\tau$, while IPO only gradually decreases it as a function of $\tau$ [1]. trl's shipped loss applies token-length normalization not present in the paper's formula (see How it works); this is a documented divergence between the paper and the default trl implementation, not a bug, per trl's own comment [4].
- **Named successors**: none identified from a check of the IPO paper's own text (it defines IPO as an instance of $\Psi$PO with no successor mentioned) and from trl's DPOTrainer documentation page, which lists sibling `loss_type` options addressing related but different problems - EXO for reverse-KL preference optimization, SPPO for iteratively solving a Nash equilibrium - rather than naming any of them a successor to IPO specifically [3].
- **Known failure modes**: the paper's own limitations are scope, not a documented failure mode - it describes its experiments as small-scale bandit illustrations and leaves scaling to language-model training on real human-preference data to future work [1]. No search of trl's issue tracker for IPO-specific maintainer replies was performed for this card; "known failure modes" from that source is therefore not stated here rather than reported as none found.
- **What the gain is - and is not**: the paper's own claim is narrow - IPO avoids the specific overfitting failure mode it identifies in DPO's unbounded objective (collapsing to a deterministic policy under sparse or one-sided preferences, ignoring $\pi_{ref}$) [1]. It is not shown in the paper to improve response quality, reasoning, or any downstream capability metric beyond this bandit-scale regularization behavior - the paper explicitly frames its LLM-scale validation as future work [1].
