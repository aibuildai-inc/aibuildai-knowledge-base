# Quiet-STaR

At every token of ordinary text, have the model silently generate a short rationale, blend the resulting next-token prediction with its normal prediction through a learned per-token weight, and use REINFORCE to reward whichever rationales made the true continuation more likely.

*A note on this card's name: the shortlist row that produced this card carries the label "STAR" and the full name "Similarity-guided Teacher-Assisted Refinement," but its arXiv ID (2403.09629) resolves to a different paper, "Quiet-STaR: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Think Before Speaking" [1]. This card follows the paper the ID actually points to; the row's name and full-name fields do not describe it and are not used below.*

**Quiet-STaR** is a self-supervised method for teaching a language model to reason before every token, not just when answering a labeled question, introduced by Zelikman et al. as a generalization of their earlier Self-Taught Reasoner, STaR [1]. STaR, its parent, is a bootstrapping loop that repeatedly samples a rationale for a labeled question, keeps the rationale only if it leads to the correct final answer, and fine-tunes on the kept rationales [2]. Quiet-STaR removes the need for labeled question-answer pairs: at every token position in an unlabeled text corpus it samples a short rationale from the model itself, computes the model's next-few-token predictions both with and without that rationale, mixes the two predictions with a learned per-token weight from a small "mixing head," and trains the rationale-generation step with a REINFORCE reward equal to how much the rationale raised the log-likelihood of the true continuation relative to the average rationale [1]. Three reasons the paper gives for the design: reasoning is implicit in almost all written text, not just the few-shot question-answering setting STaR was built for [1]; unlike STaR, no ground-truth answer is required, so training data is any unlabeled corpus [1]; and three specific engineering obstacles had to be solved - generating a rationale at every token is naively one forward pass per token, the model does not initially know when or how to use an internal thought, and predicting one token past the thought is a weak training signal for whether the thought was useful [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09629 [1].

Applied to the base version of Mistral 7B with continued pretraining on OpenWebMath, Quiet-STaR raised zero-shot direct-answer accuracy on GSM8K from 5.9% to 10.9% and on CommonsenseQA from 36.3% to 47.2%, with no task-specific fine-tuning [1]. On the more general C4 corpus, with a smaller thought budget (16 thought tokens, 4 true tokens ahead), the GSM8K gain was 5.9% to 8.1% and the CommonsenseQA gain was 36.3% to 42.6% [1]. The paper's own nearest-neighbor comparison is to "pause tokens" [3], which it describes as a constrained special case of Quiet-STaR where a token's whole rationale is a single fixed pause token; on the same base-model fine-tuning setting, pause tokens raised CommonsenseQA only from 26.9% to 28.8% and harmed GSM8K, and the pause-token paper itself reports a "lukewarm effect of pause-finetuning a standard-pretrained model," quoted here because it is the term the comparison turns on [1]. A check of 100 papers citing Quiet-STaR on Semantic Scholar, pulled 2026-08-09 in the API's default (not citation-count-sorted) order and skewed toward recent, low- or zero-citation 2025-2026 preprints, found no widely deployed production system's technical report stating it adopted Quiet-STaR; the sample is dominated by 2025-2026 research papers on latent/implicit reasoning that build on or compare against it (e.g., latent-token and pondering-depth language models), so this card treats Quiet-STaR as a research-stage method rather than a production one, while noting that a non-representative sample cannot rule out an adopter outside it [4]. Lineage in one line: REINFORCE (Williams, 1992, cited in [1]) and STaR (2022 [2]) -> Quiet-STaR (2024 [1]) -> an active but not yet consolidated line of latent/implicit-reasoning follow-ups, none confirmed here as a named, bias-fixing successor [4].

**When to pick it**: pick Quiet-STaR only for research into giving a base LM an always-on reasoning habit via unsupervised continued pretraining on raw text, where no question/answer labels exist; it is not a chat- or instruction-tuning-stage method. Prefer its parent, STaR [2], when you do have labeled questions with checkable final answers and want the bootstrapped-rationale loop rather than per-token thoughts. Prefer pause tokens [3] as a much cheaper, single-fixed-token alternative when the goal is only to test whether "more compute per token" helps at all, accepting that the paper's own numbers show pause tokens giving far smaller CommonsenseQA gains and hurting GSM8K in the comparable fine-tuning setting [1]. There is no offline (preference-pair) alternative in this family: Quiet-STaR trains on plain text with a self-generated REINFORCE signal, not on preference pairs or an external reward model [1].

**Variant of**: STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) [2].

**Data it needs**: unlabeled continuous text only - no prompts, no answers, no preference pairs, no reward-model scores. The paper trains on OpenWebMath (a math-heavy web crawl) for its headline numbers and separately on C4 (general web text) with a different thought budget [1]. Training is on-policy in the sense that every rationale is freshly sampled from the current model at each step inside the same forward pass that computes the loss; there is no offline rationale buffer [1]. Optimization uses AdamW, a 20-step warmup, learning rate 1e-6, weight decay 0.001, and batch size 8 with gradient accumulation to hold that fixed [1].

**Extra models**: none in the sense of a separate value network, reference model, or reward model - the "reward" for REINFORCE is computed from the base model's own change in log-likelihood of the true continuation, with vs. without a rationale [1]. The method does add one small extra component trained jointly with the LM: a shallow MLP "mixing head" that outputs, per token, the interpolation weight between the post-rationale and base-model next-token logits [1]. No shipping framework was found that packages this reward/mixing-head machinery as a reusable trainer; see Shipped by.

**Shipped by**: no RL or post-training library was found to ship a Quiet-STaR trainer; trl's GRPO trainer page, checked 2026-08-09, does not mention Quiet-STaR or STaR [5]. The only available implementation is the original authors' research code at github.com/ezelikman/quiet-star (739 stars as of 2026-08-09), which the repository's own README describes as "implemented by simply patching the base Mistral implementation in Huggingface `transformers`" with a custom `modeling_mistral.py` and `configuration_mistral.py`, pinned to `transformers==4.37.0.dev0`, and otherwise using the standard Hugging Face `Trainer` [6]. Building this in a modern framework means writing a custom training loop (or patching a model's forward pass, as the authors did) that performs parallel per-token thought generation, computes the mixed-logit loss, and adds the REINFORCE term - not a loss swapped onto an existing PPO/GRPO trainer.

## How it works

The loop, once per training step, follows Algorithm 1 of the paper [1]: for a sampled batch of sequences of length $l$, generate, in parallel across all $l$ token positions, a thought of length $t$ per position; mix each position's post-thought and no-thought next-token predictions; compute the ordinary language-modeling loss on the mixed prediction; compute a REINFORCE loss that rewards thoughts scoring above the batch's average reward; and take a joint gradient step [1].

**Parallel thought generation.** Naively, generating a rationale at every one of $l$ token positions requires $l$ separate forward passes. The paper's parallel sampling trick caches one forward pass and, using a diagonal attention mask, lets each generated thought token attend to the original prefix that produced it and to itself, but not across thoughts at different positions - this makes rationale generation from every position in a sequence a constant-factor overhead rather than $l\times$ the cost [1].

**Mixing.** Given the hidden states after the original text token, $h_{j:j+n_{true}}^{init}$, and after the thought, $h_{j:j+n_{true}}^{thought}$, a shallow MLP mixing head computes a weight $w_{j:j+n_{true}} \in [0,1]$ over the $n_{true}$ scored positions, and the model's actual next-token log-probability is the interpolation

$$\log p_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{talk}} = w_{j:j+n_{true}} \cdot \log p_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{init}} + (1-w_{j:j+n_{true}})\cdot \log p_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{thought}}$$

so that early in training, before the thoughts are useful, the mixing head can weight the no-thought prediction highly and avoid a hard distribution shift [1].

**Reward and REINFORCE.** For a thought $T_j$ generated at position $j$, the reward compares the mixed log-likelihood of the next $n_{true}$ real tokens with that thought against the average mixed log-likelihood across the thoughts sampled for that batch:

$$r_j = \log p_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{talk}}(X_{j+1:j+n_{true}+1}) - \log \overline{p}_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{talk}}(X_{j+1:j+n_{true}+1})$$

The paper states this gradient twice, in two slightly different forms, and this card gives both rather than merging them. Its main-text equation, without any indicator, is [1]:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_j^{\mathrm{REINFORCE}} = -\,r_j\cdot \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta}\!\big(T_j \mid [X_{:j};\texttt{<|startofthought|>}]\big)$$

Immediately after this equation the paper explains in prose that it found it useful to exclude the negative reward from the REINFORCE loss term, i.e. to zero the gradient when $r_j \le 0$ [1]. Algorithm 1's pseudocode line states that exclusion as an explicit indicator, and there it also writes the per-thought policy as $\theta_i$ rather than plain $\theta$ and the delimiter as `<start_thought>` rather than `<|startofthought|>` [1]:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_j^{\mathrm{REINFORCE}} \leftarrow -\,r_j\,\mathbb{1}[r_j>0]\cdot \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_i}\!\big(T_j \mid [X_{:j};\texttt{<start\_thought>}]\big)$$

The indicator $\mathbb{1}[r_j>0]$ zeroes out the gradient for below-average thoughts; the paper reports this improved training stability, at the cost of some bias, over using the signed reward directly [1]. The total per-token loss is $\mathcal{L}_j = \mathcal{L}_j^{\mathrm{NLL}} + \mathcal{L}_j^{\mathrm{REINFORCE}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_j^{\mathrm{NLL}} = -\log p_{j:j+n_{true}}^{\mathrm{talk}}(X_{j+1:j+n_{true}+1})$ is the ordinary mixed-prediction next-token loss, and both terms update the shared parameters $\theta$, including the mixing head [1].

**Worked example.** Suppose a group of thoughts sampled across a batch gives mixed log-likelihoods of the true next tokens of $-2.0, -1.0, -3.0, -1.5$ nats (so higher, i.e. less negative, is better). The average is $-1.875$. A thought scoring $-1.0$ gets reward $r = -1.0 - (-1.875) = +0.875 > 0$, so its REINFORCE gradient pushes the model to generate more thoughts like it; a thought scoring $-3.0$ gets $r = -1.125 < 0$, and because $\mathbb{1}[r_j>0]=0$ that thought contributes no REINFORCE gradient at all - only the ordinary next-token loss $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{NLL}}$ still applies to it [1]. This arithmetic follows the paper's own formula; whether an implementation batches the "average" per sequence, per training batch, or some other grouping is not specified in the excerpt read for this card.

**Hyperparameters that define a run.** The number of thought tokens $t$, the number of ground-truth tokens scored ahead $n_{true}$, and the number of parallel thoughts sampled per position $n_{thoughts}$ are all explicit inputs to Algorithm 1 [1]. The paper reports that increasing $n_{thoughts}$ from 1 consistently helped (about 0.5 points on GSM8K, about 3 points on CommonsenseQA), but that the exact value among 2, 3, or 4 thoughts mattered little [1]. Sampling during training uses temperature $T=1$, but the REINFORCE loss is computed by treating the samples as importance samples at $T=3$; evaluation of thoughts uses greedy decoding [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: at every one of $l$ token positions in every training sequence, the model must generate a $t$-token thought and then run at least one more forward pass to score $n_{true}$ tokens ahead with and without that thought - this is why the paper's own limitations section calls the overhead substantial, since the model generates many tokens before generating every additional real token [1]. With $n_{thoughts} > 1$, that per-position cost multiplies by $n_{thoughts}$. Memory-wise there is no second trained model (no value network, no reference model, no reward model) - the only addition to a base LM's memory footprint is the small mixing-head MLP's parameters and the extra activations from the parallel per-position thought generation and caching [1]. The naive reading that per-position generation requires $l$ independent forward passes is corrected by the parallel-sampling / diagonal-attention-mask trick described in How it works [1]; that trick is the paper's own design, not a framework optimization, so it applies wherever the algorithm is implemented, not just in a particular library.

**In practice, per framework**: no production RL/post-training framework was found to implement this method (see Shipped by), so there is no framework-level cost data (batch-size defaults, micro-batch tuning, vLLM-style generation offload) to report here. The only cost evidence available is the authors' own repository, which states the implementation patches Hugging Face `transformers`' Mistral modeling code and uses the standard `transformers` `Trainer` rather than a purpose-built distributed trainer [6]; the repository does not state throughput or memory numbers.

## How to use it

- Data preparation: plain unlabeled text, chunked into fixed-length sequences of length $l$; no reward function, no preference pairs, no answer labels to prepare. The paper trains on OpenWebMath for its main results and separately on C4 [1].
- Reward convention: internally defined by the method itself (the change in the model's own log-likelihood of the true continuation), not supplied by the user; there is no external reward model or labeled score to design [1].
- Special tokens: two learnable meta-tokens, `<|startofthought|>` and `<|endofthought|>`, mark where a thought begins and ends; the paper gives their embeddings a distinct gradient weight (1e2) from the rest of the policy gradient (weight 1e6) [1]. The authors' repository separately warns that at inference time the model has not been taught to avoid generating these tokens, so they must be masked out of generation manually [6].

| knob | paper's value [1] | shipping-framework default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thought length $t$ | 12 tokens (OpenWebMath headline run); 16 tokens (C4 run) | not applicable - no framework ships this method (see Shipped by) |
| true tokens scored ahead $n_{true}$ | 4, for both the OpenWebMath headline run and the C4 run (the paper names the headline config the "12-thought-token-4-ahead baseline" [1]) | not applicable |
| number of thoughts per position $n_{thoughts}$ | 2-4 (little difference among these; 1 clearly worse) | not applicable |
| learning rate | 1e-6 | not applicable |
| batch size | 8 (with gradient accumulation to hold this fixed) | not applicable |
| weight decay | 0.001 | not applicable |
| sampling temperature (generation / REINFORCE scoring) | $T=1$ / $T=3$ | not applicable |

  Because no framework ships this method, this table has only one populated column; it is included to show that a from-scratch implementation has real, paper-stated hyperparameters to match, not to compare a framework default against the paper.
- Trade-off a run designer faces: raising $n_{thoughts}$ buys a small, saturating accuracy gain at a directly multiplicative generation cost per position, since the paper reports the gain from 1 to more thoughts is real but the exact count among 2-4 barely matters [1] - so the paper's own evidence argues for a small, fixed $n_{thoughts}$ rather than searching upward.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: this card found no dedicated monitoring guide (no framework logging docs exist for this method, and the paper's main text does not present a "what to watch during training" checklist beyond the reward and loss terms already defined in How it works); the closest first-hand signal is the reward $r_j$ itself, which by construction is a relative, above/below-batch-average score, not an absolute quantity with a known healthy range [1].
- Published reference runs: the paper's own Figure/appendix curves for zero-shot GSM8K and CommonsenseQA accuracy over training, and a compute-normalized version of the same curves in its appendix, are the only known-good reference points; this card did not re-extract exact intermediate values from those figures beyond the headline endpoints already reported above [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the REINFORCE term only fires for above-average-reward thoughts ($\mathbb{1}[r_j>0]$), so if every thought at a position scores at or below the batch average, that position gets zero REINFORCE gradient and only the ordinary next-token loss applies - the paper states this design choice trades some bias for training stability, without characterizing how often it triggers [1].
- Named successors: none confirmed. The Semantic Scholar citation sample described above (100 papers citing arXiv:2403.09629, endpoint's default order, checked 2026-08-09) surfaced several 2025-2026 papers on latent or implicit reasoning that engage with the same problem (e.g., papers on latent-token reasoning and adaptive per-token compute), but none in that sample explicitly frames itself as fixing a specific documented Quiet-STaR bias the way DAPO or Dr. GRPO do for GRPO, so this card does not name a successor.
- Known failure modes, from the paper's own limitations section: the technique was validated only by continued pretraining of an already-pretrained 7B model, not training from scratch, so whether it works during pretraining proper is untested by the paper [1]; it was applied only to one 7B model, and the authors state a better base model would likely show disproportionately larger gains, extrapolating from a pattern the paper attributes to prior reasoning-and-scale work rather than measuring it directly [1]; and the method carries substantial computational overhead from generating many rationale tokens per real token generated, which the authors present as a cost but also frame as a controllable test-time compute knob [1]. Separately, the authors' own repository README flags an inference-time pitfall: the model is not trained to suppress `<|startofthought|>` / `<|endofthought|>` tokens on its own, so a deployer must mask them out manually or risk them leaking into generated output [6].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's comparison to pause-token fine-tuning is its own evidence for what allowing a full multi-token rationale (rather than a single fixed placeholder token) buys over just giving the model "more compute" per token: pause tokens gave a much smaller CommonsenseQA gain and hurt GSM8K in a comparably-sized fine-tuning setting, which the paper reads as evidence that the content of the rationale, not just extra computation, is doing the work [1][3]. The paper does not claim the technique adds new task-specific reasoning ability beyond what continued pretraining with rationale-generation exposes; it reports gains on the two zero-shot benchmarks it evaluates and does not claim broader transfer beyond them [1].
