# DPA

Train one multi-objective reward model, then repeatedly best-of-n sample and fine-tune on the winner scored by a user-chosen preference direction, so one model generates along an arithmetic trade-off (e.g. more helpful, less verbose) at inference time.

**DPA** (Directional Preference Alignment) is introduced by Wang et al. at https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18571 as a framework that, unlike scalar-reward RLHF, incorporates multi-objective reward modeling to represent diverse preference profiles and models user preferences as directions (unit vectors) in the reward space to achieve user-dependent preference control [1]. Its parent is Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (RSF, also called RAFT), defined by Dong et al. as fine-tuning generative models with RL algorithms guided by a reward-ranked selection of self-generated samples rather than a policy gradient [2]. DPA trains a k-dimensional reward model by regression, then iterates: sample a directional preference vector $v$ and n responses per prompt from the current policy, score each response by the scalar projection $v^\top \tilde r(x,y)$, keep only the top-ranked response, and fine-tune on that response with $v$ prepended to the prompt [1]. The paper gives three reasons for this design over PPO [3], the value-network-based policy-gradient method it forgoes: PPO is unstable and sample-inefficient for LLM alignment, it imposes a heavy GPU-memory burden, and RSF (used in the Llama 2 project) offers comparable reward gains with more simplicity and stability [1].

DPA's RSF parent is the RLHF method Llama 2 adopted for part of its alignment pipeline [1]. In the paper's own experiments (Mistral-7B, initialized from the Zephyr-beta SFT checkpoint, trained on UltraFeedback for helpfulness and verbosity), the paper reports that from iteration $t \geq 1$ onward DPA Pareto-dominates SFT, DPO, and SteerLM on the validation-reward front, and that DPA at iteration $t$ Pareto-dominates DPA at any earlier iteration $t' < t$ (Fig. 4); on AlpacaEval-2.0, DPA is reported to outperform SteerLM and be competitive with DPO while additionally giving arithmetic control (Fig. 5) [1]. Both comparisons are read off figures in the paper rather than a results table with numeric win-rates or reward scores. Lineage in one line: RAFT/RSF (2023 [2]) -> DPA (ACL 2024 [1]), with SteerLM (Dong et al., 2023b, cited in [1]) and Rewarded Soups [4] as contemporaneous multi-objective alternatives DPA is benchmarked against, not methods DPA descends from or that descend from it.

The initialism "DPA" collides with several unrelated preprints. arXiv:2402.18571 is reported at 161 citations against single-digit counts for the colliding titles; this figure is unverified by this card's own fetches (the one citation-count lookup attempted, a Semantic Scholar query, returned a 429 rate-limit error and produced no usable count). This card confirms arXiv:2402.18571 is an ACL 2024 long paper via its ACL Anthology entry, whose title and author list (Haoxiang Wang, Yong Lin, Wei Xiong, Rui Yang, et al.) match arXiv:2402.18571 [5], and confirms it has an official code/model release under RLHFlow/Directional-Preference-Alignment [6].

**When to pick it**: pick DPA when you need one deployed model that a user can steer along a continuous trade-off between two or more reward objectives at inference time (e.g., dial down verbosity without a separate model), and you already have or can train a multi-objective reward model [1]. Its parent RSF/RAFT [2] is the right choice instead when you only need to optimize a single scalar reward with best-of-n fine-tuning and do not need preference-direction control. Prefer DPO [7] as the nearest offline alternative when you have fixed chosen/rejected preference pairs and no way to score fresh samples with a reward model; DPO needs no separate reward model or on-policy generation at all [8]. Prefer PPO [3] as the nearest online neighbor when you need a single scalar reward optimized with a value-network critic and per-step policy-gradient updates rather than batched rejection-sampling rounds; the paper itself lists PPO as lacking multi-objective rewards and preference arithmetic in its Table 1 comparison [1].

**Variant of**: Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning / RAFT [2], made preference-conditional and multi-objective [1].

**Data it needs**: (a) reward-model training data: prompt-response pairs with multi-attribute human or model ratings — the paper used HelpSteer (10K prompts, 37K responses, 5 attributes, 0-4 human ratings) and UltraFeedback (64K prompts, 4 responses each, 5 attributes, GPT-4 ratings) [1]; (b) fine-tuning data: prompts only (UltraFeedback prompts in the paper), no chosen/rejected pairs needed, since responses and their rankings are generated by the policy itself at each iteration [1]. The paper's alignment run used 4 rejection-sampling/fine-tuning iterations, 5 sampled preference directions and 16 responses per preference per prompt (80 responses per prompt in the first iteration, generated by an auxiliary SteerLM model) [1]. DPA is on-policy in the RSF sense: each iteration generates fresh responses from the current policy before selecting and fine-tuning on the winners, though the fine-tuning step itself is ordinary offline SFT on that iteration's collected best-of-n dataset [1].

**Extra models**: a multi-objective reward model is required by the method's own definition — it scores every sampled response on k attributes before ranking [1]; the paper's reward model is Mistral-7B with a linear regression head [1]. No value network, no separate reference/policy-frozen model, and no preference-pair judge are used; the frozen model needed for iteration $t=1$ (before the target policy is preference-conditioned) is a separately trained SteerLM checkpoint used only to bootstrap the first round of rejection sampling [1]. See Cost for what this costs in practice.

**Shipped by**: no library ships DPA as a named trainer. The paper's own fine-tuning used PyTorch with HuggingFace's TRL framework [1], almost certainly via `SFTTrainer`, TRL's top-level exported class for next-token-prediction fine-tuning on a fixed dataset [9][10]; TRL does not export any directional-preference or multi-objective-reward trainer. Building DPA requires two things beyond a stock SFT trainer: (1) a rejection-sampling loop outside the trainer — generate n responses per prompt/preference-vector, score with the multi-objective reward model, keep the top-ranked response, repeat for T iterations — and (2) a multi-objective reward model, trained separately by regression rather than the pairwise classification loss most reward-model trainers implement [1]. The authors' own repository provides the multi-objective reward-model training code and the paper's trained checkpoints, but not a packaged end-to-end DPA trainer [6].

## How it works

Each iteration: sample a prompt and a preference direction, generate n candidate responses from the current policy conditioned on that direction, score and rank them with the reward model, keep the top response, then fine-tune the policy on the collected top responses with ordinary next-token-prediction loss [1].

**Multi-objective reward model** [1], Eq. 1, trained by regression rather than pairwise preference loss:

$$ \min_{\tilde r} \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y,r)\sim \mathcal{D}_r} \lVert \tilde r(x,y) - r(x,y) \rVert_2^2 $$

$r(x,y) = \langle r_1(x,y), \dots, r_k(x,y)\rangle \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is the vector of $k$ ground-truth attribute ratings for response $y$ to prompt $x$ (e.g. helpfulness, verbosity), and $\tilde r$ is the trained model that predicts this vector for any new $(x,y)$ [1].

**Directional preference and preference-conditioned reward** [1], Eq. 2: a user preference is a unit vector $v = \langle v_1,\dots,v_k\rangle \in \mathbb{S}^k$ on the $k$-dimensional unit sphere, and the scalar reward for a given direction is the linear scalarization

$$ R(x,v,y) = v^\top r(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} v_i\, r_i(x,y) $$

Generation is conditioned on both prompt and direction, $y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, v)$, and the training objective (Eq. 4) is to maximize the expected preference-conditioned reward over a target distribution of directions $\mathcal{P}_v$:

$$ \max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{v \sim \mathcal{P}_v} \big[ J(v,\pi_\theta) \big], \qquad J(v,\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_x,\, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,v)}\big[R(x,v,y)\big] $$

**Iterative rejection-sampling update** [1]: for $t=1,\dots,T$: (1) generation uses the previous iteration's fine-tuned policy $\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$ — for each sampled $(x,v)$, generate $n$ responses $\{y_1,\dots,y_n\}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}(\cdot\mid x,v)$, score each by $R(x,v,y_i)=v^\top \tilde r(x,y_i)$, keep the highest-scoring $y^\star$, and add $(x,v,y^\star)$ to a fresh dataset $\mathcal{D}_t$; (2) fine-tuning does not continue from $\theta_{t-1}$ — the paper's own Dataset Splitting section states that in each iteration the policy is instead reinitialized from the original SFT checkpoint before optimizing $\theta_t \leftarrow \arg\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,v,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_t}\big[\pi_\theta(y\mid x,v)\big]$, i.e. ordinary next-token-prediction maximum likelihood on $\mathcal{D}_t$ starting fresh from the SFT weights each time [1]. The two disjoint prompt subsets are alternated across iterations for the same reason: so the generating policy in iteration $t+1$ has not seen the prompts its own previous fine-tuning data came from [1].

Worked example with the paper's own two objectives, helpfulness $r_1$ and verbosity $r_2$ [1]: for direction $v=\langle v_1,v_2\rangle$ with $v_1=\sqrt2/2\approx0.707$, $v_2=-\sqrt2/2\approx-0.707$ (the paper's most-verbosity-averse setting), a candidate response with reward model output $\tilde r(x,y)=(80, 40)$ scores $R = 0.707\times80 + (-0.707)\times40 \approx 28.3$; a more verbose but equally helpful response with $\tilde r(x,y)=(80,90)$ scores $R \approx 0.707\times80-0.707\times90\approx-7.1$ — under this direction the less-verbose response is ranked higher and is the one kept for fine-tuning, even though both have the same helpfulness. The paper argues it is unnecessary to ever set $v_2>0$ (explicitly reward verbosity) because helpfulness and verbosity ratings correlate positively in their data, so it samples $\arctan(v_2/v_1) \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-\pi/4, 0)$, i.e. $v$ uniformly between $\langle1,0\rangle$ (pure helpfulness) and $\langle\sqrt2/2,-\sqrt2/2\rangle$ (balanced, verbosity-averse) [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: each RSF round needs $n$ generated responses per prompt-direction pair before any gradient step, and the reward model must score all $n$; only the single winning response per prompt-direction is used for the fine-tuning backward pass. Fine-tuning itself is one forward/backward pass per kept example, no critic, no reference-model KL pass, and no policy-gradient importance-ratio computation, unlike PPO or GRPO-style methods, which train a value network and compute a per-token probability ratio against the sampling policy [3].
- Memory: no value network and no frozen reference model are part of the method's definition, so DPA trains one policy model plus a separately-hosted multi-objective reward model used only at generation-scoring time (forward passes, no gradients on the reward model during fine-tuning) [1]. A naive reading might assume $n$ full model copies must coexist for the $n$ candidate generations; the paper's own implementation instead generates the $n$ candidates through inference batching, not model replication (see practice below).

**In practice, per framework:**

- The paper's own run: 8x A6000 GPUs, PyTorch + HuggingFace TRL for fine-tuning, vLLM for the rejection-sampling generation step, and about 60 GPU-hours per DPA iteration, run for 4 iterations [1]. Generation used temperature 1.0 during rejection sampling and temperature 0.7 at evaluation; fine-tuning used a 4096-token context with sample packing, 2 epochs, global batch size 64, AdamW at learning rate $10^{-5}$ with a cosine schedule and 20 warmup steps [1]. Because TRL's `SFTTrainer` is a standard supervised fine-tuning trainer, its own memory/throughput behavior (gradient checkpointing, packing, DeepSpeed/FSDP support) is documented on the TRL framework card, not repeated here [9].
- No other framework's cost profile is documented for DPA, since none ships it; a from-scratch build inherits whatever generation/training framework it is built on (e.g. vLLM for the sampling loop, TRL's `SFTTrainer` for the fine-tuning step, as the paper did) [1][9].

## How to use it

- Reward-model data: prompt-response tuples with a $k$-dimensional numeric rating each; the paper rescaled every attribute to a 0-100 range and trained on the union of HelpSteer and UltraFeedback, adding a standard language-modeling loss alongside the regression loss because it improved accuracy at no extra observed cost [1].
- Fine-tuning data: prompts only, split into two disjoint prompt subsets so that each iteration samples from prompts the previous iteration's policy has not been fine-tuned on, which the paper does specifically to keep the RSF loop from memorizing and repeating responses [1]. About 15% of each iteration's fine-tuning data is replayed original UltraFeedback responses, following InstructGPT and Llama 2's alignment-tax mitigation practice [1].
- Preference direction convention: the paper prepends the chosen $v$ to the system prompt at fine-tuning and inference time so the model conditions generation on it [1]; the official repository's release format expresses this as integer percentage weights (`weight_helpfulness`, `weight_verbosity`) satisfying $(\text{weight\_verbosity}/100)^2+(\text{weight\_helpfulness}/100)^2\approx 1$, with a suggested `weight_helpfulness` floor of 71 [6].
- Knobs table (paper's own values are the only published anchor; no framework ships defaults for this method):

| knob | paper value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| iterations $T$ | 4 |
| responses per prompt-direction $n$ | 16 (80 in the bootstrap iteration $t{=}1$) |
| directions sampled per prompt per iteration | 5 |
| rejection-sampling temperature | 1.0 |
| evaluation temperature | 0.7 |
| fine-tuning learning rate | $10^{-5}$ (AdamW, cosine, 20 warmup steps) |
| fine-tuning epochs per iteration | 2 |
| global batch size | 64 |
| context window | 4096 tokens (sample-packed) |
| replay fraction of original data | ~15% |

- Trade-off a run designer faces: larger $n$ gives a sharper best-of-n selection (better winner per prompt-direction) at the cost of $n\times$ generation calls per prompt-direction before any gradient step; more directions sampled per prompt spreads coverage across the preference sphere but multiplies the same generation cost again, since the paper generates $n$ fresh responses for every sampled direction independently [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper's own live diagnostic is the validation reward front across iterations (Fig. 4) — it reports that DPA at iteration $t$ should Pareto-dominate DPA at iteration $t' < t$ across the sampled range of preference directions, and that from $t\geq1$ onward it should Pareto-dominate the SFT, DPO, and SteerLM baselines it compares against [1]. No loss curve, entropy, or clip-fraction metric is reported, since there is no policy-gradient or KL term in the method.
- Published reference runs: the paper's Fig. 4 (validation reward vs. preference direction, by iteration) and Fig. 5 (AlpacaEval-2.0 win rate and response length vs. preference direction) are the only published reference curves for this method; both are read from figures, and the paper reports no corresponding numeric table [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper flags that its own approach of not needing $v_2>0$ (never explicitly rewarding verbosity) is a data-dependent design choice, valid because helpfulness and verbosity happened to correlate positively in HelpSteer/UltraFeedback [1] — a deployment on data where objectives trade off more sharply or negatively correlate would need to reconsider the sampled range of $v$. No batch-size-1 or reward-model-collapse degeneracy is discussed in the paper.
- Named successors: none found. Searched Semantic Scholar's citation listing was attempted but blocked by rate limiting during this check; no successor paper naming DPA as its predecessor was located in the sources fetched for this card (the DPA paper's own GitHub repository and arXiv page) [1][6].
- Known failure modes, from the paper's own Limitations section: DPA's efficacy is intrinsically linked to the precision and discriminative capability of the multi-objective reward model, and if that reward model does not adequately capture the subtleties of specific preferences or is biased, DPA can exacerbate those shortcomings through fine-tuning; if the reward model fails to recognize harmful content, the aligned model could produce such content at inference [1].
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's own claim is a better performance trade-off across reward objectives and arithmetic, user-controllable steering along that trade-off, benchmarked as Pareto-dominating SFT, DPO, and SteerLM on its own reward model's validation score [1]. The paper also notes a discrepancy between its reward-model validation results and GPT-4-turbo-judged AlpacaEval-2.0 results, attributing it to the reward model having different behavior/preferences than GPT-4-turbo, and stating that closely fitting the reward model does not necessarily guarantee generalization to GPT-4-turbo evaluation [1] — i.e., the reported gain is specific to the reward model used and does not guarantee an equivalent gain under a different judge.
