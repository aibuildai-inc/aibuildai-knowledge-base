# SFT

Take a pretrained language model and continue training it with ordinary next-token cross-entropy, but only on curated (instruction, output) examples, so the model's outputs shift from "continue this text" to "follow this instruction."

**SFT** (supervised fine-tuning), also called instruction tuning (IT), is reviewed by a survey (https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10792) that defines it as further training LLMs on a dataset consisting of (instruction, output) pairs in a supervised fashion, which bridges the gap between the next-word prediction objective of LLMs and the users' objective of having LLMs adhere to human instructions [1]. SFT has no single originating paper the way GRPO or DPO do: it is the direct application of ordinary supervised fine-tuning - training on labeled input/output pairs with cross-entropy - to instruction-formatted data, and the survey used here is the paper the underlying literature search ranked highest by citations for the name "SFT" among 357 candidate papers, confirmed as the top-cited match under that search [1]. It has no parent method in the RL sense; it is the supervised-learning stage that later preference- and reward-based methods are built on top of (see Variant of). Mechanically, given the instruction and an optional input, the model is trained by predicting each token in the output sequentially [1], i.e. ordinary teacher-forced cross-entropy on the output tokens. The survey gives three reasons SFT is used: it bridges the mismatch between the pretraining objective (next-word prediction) and the user's objective of instruction-following; it makes model behavior more controllable and predictable, since the instructions constrain outputs toward desired characteristics; and it is computationally efficient, letting an LLM adapt to a domain without extensive retraining or architectural changes [1].

Landmark instruction-tuned systems built directly on SFT include Alpaca, a 7B LLaMA fine-tuned on 52K instructions generated by InstructGPT, which the survey reports as reaching comparable human-evaluation performance to 175B InstructGPT [1]; Vicuna, a 13B LLaMA fine-tuned on 70K ShareGPT conversations [1]; and LIMA, a 65B LLaMA fine-tuned on only 1,000 curated (instruction, output) pairs, which the survey reports outperforming InstructGPT and Alpaca by 17 and 19 percentage points respectively in human evaluation, a result the paper attributes to its "superficial alignment hypothesis" that most knowledge is already present after pretraining and alignment mainly teaches output format [1]. InstructGPT itself started by collecting labeler demonstrations of desired behavior and used them to fine-tune GPT-3 with supervised learning, before separately collecting output rankings and further fine-tuning that supervised model with reinforcement learning from human feedback [2]. TÜLU 3 lists supervised finetuning (SFT) as the first of its three post-training algorithms, alongside DPO and its own RLVR method [3]. The survey's own analysis concludes that both RLHF and DPO pipelines almost universally incorporate an initial SFT stage, and that practitioners generally fine-tune on good example responses before DPO to establish a robust initial policy [1]. Lineage in one line: pretrained base model -> SFT [1] -> reward-model/preference stage (RLHF [2] / DPO [4]) or verifiable-reward RL (GRPO [5]) built on top of the SFT checkpoint.

**When to pick it**: pick SFT whenever you have (or can curate) demonstrations of the exact behavior you want and no further preference or reward signal is available or needed; it is also the near-universal first stage before any RL or preference method, because RLHF and DPO pipelines almost universally start from an SFT-tuned model to establish a reasonable base policy [1]. The survey describes DPO [4] as a way to move past demonstrations once you instead have pairwise preference judgments over pairs of outputs to the same prompt, casting alignment as a supervised objective on the preferred-vs-dispreferred pair without training a separate reward model [1]. It likewise describes GRPO [5] and other RL-based methods as alternatives or complements once you have a way to score fresh policy samples with a reward signal and want the policy to explore beyond the demonstrations in the SFT set, rather than replacements for the initial supervised stage [1].

**Variant of**: none in the RL-method sense - SFT is the base supervised-learning technique that other post-training methods (RLHF, DPO, GRPO) are layered on top of, typically starting from an SFT checkpoint [1].

**Data it needs**: (instruction, optional input, output) pairs, i.e. demonstrations of the desired behavior, not preference pairs or scalar rewards [1]. trl's `SFTTrainer` accepts this as one of four column layouts: a single `text` field (plain language modeling), a `messages` field (conversational, chat-template applied automatically), a `prompt`/`completion` pair, or a conversational `prompt`/`completion` pair where each is a list of role/content turns [6]. Training-scale examples from the survey range from 1K curated examples (LIMA) to 70K (Vicuna) to full NLP-dataset mixtures reaching millions of instances - the survey's own appendix table lists P3 at 12M instances [1]. SFT is offline: it trains once on a fixed, pre-collected dataset and takes no fresh samples from the model being trained, unlike GRPO's on-policy rollouts [1][5].

**Extra models**: none. SFT trains the policy model alone with plain cross-entropy on labeled targets; it requires no value network, no reference model, and no reward model [6]. (Contrast GRPO, which needs a frozen reference model whenever its KL term is enabled [5].)

**Shipped by**: trl, `SFTTrainer` / `SFTConfig`, contributed by Younes Belkada [6]; verl, `verl.trainer.sft_trainer.SFTTrainer`, launched via Hydra with `config_name="sft_trainer_engine"` (read at commit reflected in the file fetched 2026-08-09) [7].

## How it works

The loop in one line: tokenize each (instruction/prompt, output/completion) pair, concatenate prompt and completion if given separately, and take a gradient step on the cross-entropy of the completion tokens conditioned on everything before them [6].

**The objective**, as trl's docs state it [6]:

$$ L_{SFT}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}) $$

$y_t$ is the target token at position $t$ in the output sequence, $y_{<t}$ is every token before it (instruction/prompt tokens plus any completion tokens already generated), and $p_\theta$ is the model's predicted probability for the true next token; the sum runs over the $T$ tokens being trained on [6]. During training the labels are shifted right by one position relative to the inputs (the standard teacher-forcing setup), and any padding position is excluded from the loss with an ignore index (default $-100$) [6]. When the dataset is prompt/completion rather than a single text field, trl computes the loss only on the completion tokens by default (`completion_only_loss=True` for that format), masking out the prompt tokens from the sum entirely [6].

**Worked example**: take the pair prompt = "The sky is", completion = " blue." [6]. After concatenation and tokenization the model sees the full sequence, but the loss sum in $L_{SFT}$ only includes the tokens of " blue." - each of those tokens contributes $-\log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})$ using everything before it (including "The sky is") as context; the prompt tokens "The", "sky", "is" contribute nothing to the loss because `completion_only_loss` masks them [6]. There is no advantage, no reward, and no baseline to compute - every kept token's target is simply the next token that actually appeared in the demonstration.

**Variant losses.** The survey notes Vicuna's authors used a loss function adapted for multi-turn conversations and expanded the trained context length from 512 to 2048 tokens [1]. trl ships an additional loss variant beyond the plain NLL above: `loss_type="dft"` (Dynamic Fine-Tuning), which trl's docs describe as aiming to improve generalization by rectifying the reward signal, following a paper trl's docs point to in their Paper Index [6]; the plain formula above is trl's default computation path (`loss_type="chunked_nll"`, mathematically identical to `"nll"` but chunked to save activation memory) [6]. This card does not further evaluate the DFT paper, which was not fetched.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: one forward and one backward pass per training example through the policy model only - no rollout generation, no second model's forward pass, no advantage computation. Memory holds exactly one trained model's weights, gradients, and optimizer state; there is no frozen reference model and no reward model resident during training, since the loss in $L_{SFT}$ above needs only the policy's own token probabilities and the fixed target labels [6]. This makes SFT the cheapest of the post-training stages in this corpus by construction: it is the only one that needs neither extra forward-pass models nor online sampling.

**In practice, per framework**:
- trl `SFTTrainer` [6]: `gradient_checkpointing` defaults to `True` (trading compute for activation memory) and `bf16` defaults to `True` if `fp16` is not set - both defaults differ from the base `TrainingArguments` class, which defaults `gradient_checkpointing` to `False` and `bf16` to `False` [6]. `packing` (default `False`) concatenates multiple short examples into one training sequence up to `max_length` to raise token throughput per batch; `max_length` defaults to 1024 tokens, with sequences truncated per `truncation_mode` (`"keep_start"`) when it is set [6]. The default loss path, `loss_type="chunked_nll"`, processes the cross-entropy in chunks so peak activation memory does not scale with the full vocabulary-size by sequence-length logits tensor [6].
- verl `SFTTrainer` [7]: memory/throughput is governed by `data.micro_batch_size_per_gpu` (per-GPU micro-batch, default 4) and `data.max_token_len_per_gpu` (default 8192) under `use_dynamic_bsz: True`, with a global `train_batch_size` of 256 [7]. `data.max_length` defaults to 1024 with `truncation: error` (the run fails rather than silently truncating) [7]. Do not carry trl's packing/chunked-loss defaults onto verl's run, or verl's dynamic-batching defaults onto trl - these are per-framework knobs read from each project's own config file [6][7].

## How to use it

- Data: assemble (instruction, output) demonstrations in one of trl's four supported layouts - plain `text`, conversational `messages`, `prompt`/`completion`, or conversational `prompt`/`completion` [6]. If your raw data has separate question/answer/reasoning columns, map them into a `prompt`/`completion` pair (trl's own example reshapes a medical reasoning dataset this way) [6].
- Loss convention: with a `prompt`/`completion` dataset, trl trains on completion tokens only by default (`completion_only_loss=True`); to train on the full concatenated sequence including the prompt, set it to `False` explicitly [6]. `assistant_only_loss=True` restricts the loss further to assistant turns in a multi-turn conversational dataset, and requires the chat template to carry `{% generation %}`/`{% endgeneration %}` markers - trl auto-patches known model families' templates for this, and asks you to check the template yourself for others [6].
- Key knobs, with each source's own default (paper values are anchors from the survey's reviewed systems, not one paper's stated defaults, since SFT has no single defining hyperparameter table):

| knob | trl default [6] | verl default [7] | survey systems [1] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| learning rate | `2e-5` | not stated in fetched config | not stated (framework-level detail per system) |
| sequence length | `max_length` 1024 | `data.max_length` 1024 | Vicuna expanded 512 -> 2048 for multi-turn context [1] |
| batch size† | `per_device_train_batch_size` 8 | `train_batch_size` 256, `micro_batch_size_per_gpu` 4 | not stated |
| epochs | `num_train_epochs` 3.0 | `trainer.total_epochs` 4 | not stated |
| packing / truncation-on-overflow | `packing` False; truncation on overflow | `truncation: error` (fails rather than truncating) | not stated |

  † Not the same quantity: trl's `per_device_train_batch_size` is a per-device figure, while verl's `train_batch_size` is the global batch size across all devices and `micro_batch_size_per_gpu` is verl's separate per-GPU figure; the row lists both verl numbers rather than forcing a false one-to-one match [6][7].

  Two further readings of that table: trl's and verl's `max_length` defaults happen to agree at 1024 tokens, but their overflow behavior is opposite - trl truncates by default, verl raises an error - so the same dataset that trains silently on trl can crash a verl run until `max_length` or a longer setting is chosen deliberately [6][7]. Epoch counts (3 vs 4) are unrelated framework defaults, not a tuned comparison; do not read the gap as evidence either way.
- Trade-off: dataset size versus curation quality is the central design choice for SFT specifically, since the survey's own comparison shows a 1,000-example curated set (LIMA) beating 52K-70K auto-generated sets (Alpaca, InstructGPT distillation) by double-digit points in human evaluation [1] - more raw instruction-output pairs is not automatically better than fewer, carefully filtered ones.
- Framework install, launch commands, and distributed-training setup are framework-skill content, not this card's; this card stops at the knobs that define the SFT stage itself.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: trl's `SFTTrainer` logs `loss` (average cross-entropy over non-masked tokens in the logging interval), `entropy` (average entropy of the predicted token distribution over non-masked tokens), `mean_token_accuracy` (fraction of non-masked tokens where the top-1 prediction matches the target), `grad_norm` (L2 norm of gradients before clipping), and `num_tokens` (cumulative tokens processed) [6]. A falling loss with rising `mean_token_accuracy` is the basic healthy shape; trl's SFT documentation names these metrics but does not state numeric healthy ranges for them [6].
- Published reference runs: none found in the sources checked for this card - the survey [1] reports final task-benchmark scores per system (e.g. LIMA's human-eval win rates over InstructGPT and Alpaca) but no training-curve logs, and neither trl's [6] nor verl's [7] SFT documentation links a published loss/accuracy curve for a reference SFT run.
- Degeneracies and defaults: the two framework defaults that most commonly bite are `completion_only_loss` (silently including or excluding prompt tokens from the loss depending on dataset format) [6] and the `max_length`/truncation mismatch above, where trl truncates silently and verl errors out [6][7]. For vision-language datasets, trl's docs warn that truncating can remove image tokens and cause errors, and recommend setting `max_length=None` only after verifying no example in the dataset needs truncation [6].
- Named successors / fixes to documented biases: the survey names the "superficial alignment hypothesis" as the main critique specific to SFT - that improvements from instruction tuning are often superficial, meaning the model learns output formats and stylistic cues rather than the underlying task, a finding LIMA's authors used as their premise and that several follow-up studies reached independently [1]. It does not name a single successor method that "fixes" this bias the way DAPO or Dr. GRPO fix GRPO's length bias; the responses reviewed are alternative alignment stages (RLHF, DPO, prompt engineering) layered after SFT, not replacements for it [1].
- Known failure modes: from the survey's own limitations discussion - SFT only reliably improves on tasks well represented in the training set, a concern the survey attributes to Gudibande et al. [1]; and there is intense criticism that SFT captures only surface-level patterns and style rather than the underlying task, a claim the survey attributes to Kung and Peng [1]. No maintainer-issue search was performed against trl's or verl's issue trackers for this card; "known failure modes" above are limited to what the survey itself states.
- What the gain is - and is not: a comparative study cited as an alternate in this corpus's search finds that SFT tends to memorize training data and struggles to generalize to out-of-distribution variants, while reinforcement learning trained with an outcome-based reward generalizes better across both textual and visual rule variants - but the same study finds SFT remains essential for stabilizing the model's output format so that subsequent RL training can succeed [8]. Consistent with this, the survey's own review states that empirical evidence shows SFT remains necessary even in RL-based pipelines, since RLHF and DPO both typically start from an SFT-tuned model [1]. Do not expect SFT alone to add capabilities the base model cannot already express in some form; its own reviewed literature frames its gain as controllability and instruction-following, not new task capability beyond what fine-tuning data covers [1].
