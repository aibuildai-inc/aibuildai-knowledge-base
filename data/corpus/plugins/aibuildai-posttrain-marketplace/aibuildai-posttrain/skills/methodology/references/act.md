# ACT

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00222 [1]

A quasi-online DPO variant for multi-turn dialogue: build an initial winning/losing pair per turn from an inferred dialogue action (clarify or answer directly), then during training replace the losing (or winning) side with a fresh on-policy sample whose simulated multi-turn outcome is scored against a task heuristic.

**ACT** (Action-Based Contrastive Self-Training) is defined by Chen et al., who introduce it as "a quasi-online preference optimization algorithm based on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), that enables data-efficient dialogue policy learning in multi-turn conversation modeling" [1]. Its parent is DPO, whose own paper defines it as a reparameterization of the RLHF reward model that lets the optimal policy be extracted in closed form, replacing reward-model fitting plus RL with a single classification loss [2]. ACT keeps DPO's loss but changes what fills the winning/losing slots: a preference pair is first built from a ground-truth response versus a synthetically generated response carrying the opposite dialogue action (clarify vs. directly answer), and during training a fresh response is sampled from the current policy and substituted into the pair after its multi-turn trajectory is simulated and scored [1]. The paper gives three motivating intuitions: contrastive pairs are a natural way to express the pragmatic difference between "winning" and "losing" dialogue actions; multi-turn conversational improvement is hard to express with single-turn contrast pairs alone; and the DPO gradient is weighted by the log-probabilities of the winning/losing responses, so on-policy sampling yields higher-probability (and thus more informative) sequences under the current policy [1]. The paper's title match was confirmed by top-cited-collision pick: of four arXiv papers whose acronym or name is "ACT", this one (57 citations) is the highest-cited, ahead of an Advantage-Conditioned Transformer (34 citations), and two later, low/zero-citation papers also titled around "ACT" for agentic training and code translation.

ACT has no reported landmark adopters beyond its own paper; it is a 2024 research method (accepted to ICLR 2025 per its reference implementation's citation [3]) evaluated on three dialogue tasks the authors built or curated: PACIFIC (tabular/textual conversational QA) [4], Abg-CoQA (conversational machine reading comprehension) [5], and AmbigSQL, a new disambiguation-for-text-to-SQL task the paper introduces on top of Spider [6][1]. On PACIFIC with only 50 training conversations, tuning Zephyr-7B-beta with ACT raised implicit-ambiguity-recognition Macro F1 from 69.0 (SFT) to 82.2, a relative gain the paper reports as up to 19.1%, and raised trajectory-level DROP F1 from an adapter-tuned Gemini Pro baseline of 45.6 to 61.9 (35.7% relative) [1, Table 1]. Lineage in one line: DPO (2023 [2]) -> ACT (2024 [1]), with no named successors identified in this search.

**When to pick it**: multi-turn dialogue tuning where the only usable reward signal is a discrete dialogue action (e.g., "should the agent ask a clarifying question or answer now") plus a task-specific correctness heuristic on the eventual outcome, and where labeled multi-turn preference data is scarce (the paper's settings use 50-250 conversations) [1]. Prefer plain offline DPO [2] when single-turn preference pairs already reflect the behavior you want and on-policy sampling infrastructure (a user simulator, an action classifier) is not available; the paper's own ablation shows off-policy DPO on the same pairs underperforms full ACT (74.8 vs. 82.2 Macro F1) [1, Table 5]. Prefer IRPO [7] as the nearest online neighbor when the target is single-turn reasoning-chain quality rather than multi-turn dialogue policy: IRPO iteratively optimizes preferences between competing Chain-of-Thought candidates using a modified DPO loss with an added negative log-likelihood term, and reports gains on GSM8K, MATH, and ARC-Challenge for Llama-2-70B-Chat [7]; ACT's own ablation states that without multi-turn simulation, the approach can be viewed similarly to on-policy DPO variants such as IRPO, but with a conversation-specific reward signal [1].

**Variant of**: DPO [2].

**Data it needs**: per turn, a prompt (task context plus dialogue history), a ground-truth response, and either a human-annotated or classifier-inferred dialogue action label from a fixed action space (the paper uses {CLARIFY, ANSWER}) [1]. No pre-collected preference-pair dataset is sufficient on its own: training also needs a way to sample on-policy completions from the model being tuned, an action classifier to check each sampled completion's implicit action, and a user simulator to play out the rest of the conversation when the sampled action is correct, scored against a task-specific heuristic [1]. The paper's runs use 50, 100, or 250 training conversations per task (up to 1,923 training examples for PACIFIC's 250-conversation setting) [1][8]. On-policy: the paper defines ACT as "quasi-online" because during each training step a single response is freshly sampled from the current policy to update part of the contrastive pair, but the other half of the pair (and the initial dataset construction) is fixed/offline, unlike fully-online DPO where both sides are sampled fresh [1].

**Extra models**: beyond DPO's usual frozen reference policy $\pi_{ref}$ (initialized from the starting checkpoint) [1], ACT's own definition requires three more models: a conditional generation model $M$ used only during preference-dataset construction to synthesize the initial losing response for the opposite action [1]; an Action Classifier $A$, used during training to check the sampled response's implicit action (the paper's own implementation uses few-shot in-context learning, not a trained classifier) [1]; and a User Simulator $U$, used to simulate the next user turn when the sampled response continues a clarification trajectory (the paper's implementation is a second LLM prompted with a summarized user goal) [1]. No value network. Cost details below.

**Shipped by**: no mainstream RLHF/post-training library ships an ACT trainer as a top-level export - a directory listing of `trl/trainer` on trl's `main` branch (18 files, none named for ACT) [9] and of `verl/trainer` on verl's `main` branch (13 entries, none named for ACT) [10] found no ACT trainer in either. The paper's authors released their own reference implementation in the `google-research/google-research` monorepo under `learning_to_clarify/act` (top-level usage documented in `learning_to_clarify/README.md`, not inside the `act/` subdirectory itself) [3], explicitly marked "not an officially supported Google product" [3]; its `setup.py` packages the code as a pip-installable package named `act` (`packages=find_packages("src")`), and the repo's own Dockerfile builds the image by running `pip install .` after installing `trl==0.9.3` and other pinned dependencies [11]. Its entry point is `class ACTTrainer(DPOTrainer)` in `act/src/act/trainer/act_trainer.py`, a subclass of trl's `DPOTrainer` that overrides the training loop to add the on-policy sampling, action classification, and trajectory-simulation steps [11]; the file itself states "ACTTrainer is a subclass of DPOTrainer that implements the RL update loop" [11]. It is run only via the repo's Docker CLI (image `l2c`) with commands `generate-preference`, `run-sft`, `run-act`, and `evaluate` [3], not published as an installable package on PyPI, and carries no support status beyond the "not an officially supported Google product" disclaimer [3].

## How it works

Each step: draw a batch of pre-built contrastive turns, sample one on-policy response from the current policy for each turn, classify its action, and either replace the losing response (action mismatch) or simulate the rest of the conversation and replace the winning or losing response with the resulting trajectory depending on whether a task heuristic judges the outcome acceptable; then take a DPO gradient step on the updated pairs [1, Alg. 2].

**Preference-pair construction (offline, one-time)** [1, Alg. 1]: for each conversation turn $t_i = (p_i, r_i, g_i, a_i)$ with prompt $p_i$, ground-truth response $r_i$, trajectory goal $g_i$, and inferred action $a_i$, set the winning response $y_{w_i} \leftarrow r_i$; set the rejected action $a_i' = S \setminus a_i$ (the paper's action space is $S = \{\text{CLARIFY}, \text{ANSWER}\}$, so $a_i'$ is just the other action); sample a losing response $y_{l_i} \sim P_M(\cdot \mid p_i, a_i')$ from the conditional generation model $M$; add the augmented tuple $t_i' = (p_i, r_i, g_i, a_i, a_i', y_{w_i}, y_{l_i})$ to the preference dataset $D_{pref}$.

**Self-training loop (the "quasi-online" part)** [1, Alg. 2]: for each turn $t_i$ in a batch sampled from $D_{pref}$ at policy $\pi_{\theta_j}$, sample $y_i \sim \pi_{\theta_j}(\cdot \mid p_i)$. If the action classifier's inferred action for $y_i$ does not match $a_i$, set $y_{l_i} = y_i$ (a wrong-action sample directly replaces the losing response). If it does match, simulate forward: repeatedly generate a user clarification via $U$ and a next policy response, appending each to a running trajectory, until the policy's action is ANSWER; then compare the trajectory's outcome to the ground-truth goal $g_i$ using a task heuristic $H$ with tolerance $\epsilon$. If $H(\text{trajectory}, g_i) > \epsilon$, set $y_{w_i}$ to the full simulated trajectory (reward it); otherwise set $y_{l_i}$ to the trajectory (penalize it). Update $\theta$ on the resulting pair using the DPO loss (Eq. 1 below) and repeat until convergence [1].

**Training objective** — unmodified DPO loss, applied to the ACT-updated pairs [1, Eq. 1]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(p, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w \mid p)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l \mid p)}\right)\right] $$

$p$ is the prompt (task info concatenated with dialogue history up to the current turn); $y_w, y_l$ are the winning/losing responses or full trajectories as set by the self-training loop above; $\pi_{ref}$ is the initial policy before tuning; $\beta$ regularizes how far $\pi_\theta$ can move from $\pi_{ref}$ [1]. The paper also gives the gradient explicitly [1, Eq. 2]:

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{(p,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat{R}_\theta(p,y_l) - \hat{R}_\theta(p,y_w)\big)\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w\mid p) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l \mid p)\big]\Big], \quad R(p,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(y\mid p)} $$

so each pair is weighted by how badly the implicit reward model currently mis-ranks it [1]. Worked example: at turn $i$ the ground-truth response is a direct SQL answer ($a_i = \text{ANSWER}$), so $a_i' = \text{CLARIFY}$ and $M$ generates a clarifying-question losing response; during training the policy happens to sample $y_i$ that also asks a clarifying question (matching $a_i'$, not $a_i$) — since the classifier finds the action wrong, $y_{l_i}$ is replaced with this fresh on-policy $y_i$, while $y_{w_i}$ stays the original ground-truth SQL answer; the DPO loss then pushes down the log-probability of the model's own clarifying-question sample and up the log-probability of the ground-truth answer, using whatever $\beta$ that task's run was tuned with [1].

The paper defines only outcome-level scoring via the task heuristic $H$; it does not define a separate process-supervision variant. No framework ships an alternate loss aggregation for ACT since no framework ships ACT at all (see Shipped by).

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: relative to plain offline DPO, each ACT training step adds one on-policy generation per turn from the current policy, plus — when that generation's action matches the ground truth — a full multi-turn rollout (repeated policy generations interleaved with user-simulator generations until the policy emits ANSWER) before the DPO loss can even be computed [1, Alg. 2]. Time therefore scales with however many clarification rounds a given rollout takes, which is unbounded in the algorithm as stated (bounded only by the policy eventually producing ANSWER). Memory-wise, ACT adds a frozen reference model, exactly as DPO does [1][2]; it does not add a value network. It does require, at some point in the pipeline, a conditional generation model $M$, an action classifier $A$, and a user-simulator model $U$ to be available for inference (Extra models); whether these run on the same accelerator as training or as separate API calls is an implementation choice the paper's own math does not fix — a naive reading that ACT is "DPO plus one forward pass" undercounts the simulator/classifier calls needed at every training step.

**In practice, per framework**: neither trl nor verl exposes an ACT trainer (see Shipped by) [9][10], so the only framework-level cost report available is from the paper's own reference implementation, which is itself a subclass of trl's `DPOTrainer` [11]. That implementation runs all experiments on a single Google Compute Engine VM with 8x 80GB A100 GPUs, using DeepSpeed for full-model multi-GPU tuning; the longest run - full ACT with 250 PACIFIC conversations (1,923 training examples), 12 epochs - took about 16 hours [8]. In that implementation, $A$, $U$, and $M$ are all served as separate Gemini API calls (`action_model_id`, `simulator_model_id`, `preference_model_id` in the reference script), not run on the training GPUs [11], so the reported 16 hours already reflects those external round-trips rather than hiding them.

## How to use it

- Data prep: collect multi-turn conversations where each assistant turn has (or can be assigned) a discrete dialogue action; the paper's tasks use a two-action space (CLARIFY vs. ANSWER) but note the method generalizes to a broader action space [1]. Build the initial preference set with Algorithm 1 above using a high-capacity LLM as $M$ (the paper uses Gemini Ultra) [1][8].
- Reward/label convention: correctness is judged two ways at once — action-level (does the sampled response's classified action match the ground-truth action) and content-level (does the simulated trajectory's outcome satisfy a task-specific heuristic $H$ against the ground-truth goal $g_i$, within tolerance $\epsilon$) [1]. Neither is a scalar reward model in the RLHF sense; both are match/heuristic checks.
- Action labels are optional at training time: the paper's Section 5.3 shows using a zero-shot Gemini 1.5 Pro classifier in place of crowdsourced action labels reaches 98.5% agreement with the ground-truth labels and produces "nearly no empirical difference in performance" versus using the human labels [1, Table 4].
- Key knobs, with the paper's own reported values (no framework anchors exist since no framework ships ACT):

| knob | paper value |
| --- | --- |
| $\beta$ (PACIFIC) | 0.01 [1] |
| learning rate (PACIFIC) | 5e-7 [1] |
| $\beta$ (AmbigSQL) | reported as both 0.01 and 0.5 in the paper's own appendix text — likely a duplication/typo in the source, not resolved by this card [1] |
| learning rate (AmbigSQL) | 5e-7 [1] |
| RL tuning epochs | up to 12, selecting the checkpoint with highest reward margin on a held-out validation preference set [8] |
| batch size | 4 [8] |
| max sequence length | 1,280 tokens [8] |
| SFT learning rates tried | {1e-4, 2e-5, 1e-5} with AdamW [8] |

- Trade-off a run designer faces: batch reuse and "group size" in the GRPO/PPO sense do not apply here — ACT samples one on-policy completion per turn per step, not a group — but the designer does choose how much of the pipeline (classifier, simulator, generation model) to run as a separately-hosted LLM versus locally, which the paper's own reference implementation resolves by calling external Gemini endpoints for all three roles [11].
- Framework install/launch details do not apply; there is no framework to install this method into (see Shipped by).

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: the paper does not describe live training-time monitoring signals (e.g., no logged metric names or curve shapes for loss, margin, or entropy during an ACT run); its only quantitative account of training-time selection is the checkpoint-selection rule — pick the checkpoint with the highest reward margin on a held-out validation preference set — used across all RL tuning runs [8].
- Published reference runs: the paper's own tables serve this role. On PACIFIC's 50-conversation setting, Macro F1 goes SFT 69.0 -> ACT 82.2 [1, Table 1]; the ablation study (Table 5, same 50-conversation PACIFIC setting) is the deciding comparison: ACT w/ Random Actions 63.2, ACT w/o on-policy sampling 74.8, ACT w/ sampling but w/o simulation 81.4, full ACT 82.2 (all Macro F1) [1, Table 5] — each component (correct action selection, on-policy sampling, multi-turn simulation) is shown to matter, with on-policy sampling alone recovering most of the gap and simulation closing the rest.
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper's own limitations section flags that ACT's "quasi-online" exploration is limited when the target answer is highly constrained (e.g., a specific arithmetic expression in PACIFIC), because there may be few unique token sequences that reach that answer, so the on-policy sample drawn during training "may not be any different from the offline trajectory found in the training data" [12] — i.e., on tasks with a narrow answer space, the on-policy-sampling benefit shown in the Table 5 ablation may not materialize.
- Named successors: none found. Search: this card's fetch of the paper's own text and of its arXiv listing (57 citations) found no later paper naming itself as an ACT successor; not independently checked against a citation-graph tool.
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own Appendix A.1 ("Is ACT online learning?"), the authors note ACT differs from fully-online DPO (where both winning and losing responses are sampled) because "there is no guarantee that sampling a response from the policy model twice will result in differing actions, unless you change the prompt" — and changing the prompt would break the DPO objective as formulated [12]. From Appendix A.2, the authors state ACT introduces no new safety guardrails itself and rely on the guardrails of whatever LLM serves as $M$/$A$/$U$ (Gemini, in their runs), warning that open-source LLMs substituted into those roles may lack such guardrails at inference time [12]. No maintainer-issue-tracker failure reports exist because no library ships ACT.
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own ablations attribute ACT's improvement over SFT and offline DPO specifically to (a) using action-based rather than random contrastive pairs, (b) on-policy sampling over static negatives, and (c) multi-turn trajectory simulation over single-turn contrast [1, Table 5] — the gain is in multi-turn dialogue-policy quality (asking clarifying questions when needed, then completing the task), not in raw downstream task capability: the paper notes SQL-generation quality itself scales with model size and that ACT's gains are largest in improving *when* to clarify and how well the model reasons about its own clarification, not in expanding what the underlying model can compute [1].
