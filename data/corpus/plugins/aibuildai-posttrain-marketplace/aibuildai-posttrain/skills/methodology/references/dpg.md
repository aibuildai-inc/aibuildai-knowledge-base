# DPG

REINFORCE aimed at a target density instead of a reward: sample from a fixed proposal policy and push a trained autoregressive policy to match an unnormalized target distribution `P(x)`, using the importance-weighted ratio `P(x)/q(x)` as the pseudo-reward.

**DPG** (Distributional Policy Gradient) is a method for fitting an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$ to approximate a target distribution $p(x) \propto P(x)$ that is known only up to a normalizing constant, introduced by Parshakova, Andreoli and Dymetman as "a distributional variant of the Policy Gradient technique (Distributional Policy Gradient: DPG)" [1]. Its parent is the Policy Gradient / REINFORCE technique of Williams, cited by the DPG paper as the classical result the derivation leans on [1]. The mechanism: minimizing the forward KL divergence $KL(p\|\pi_\theta)$, equivalently maximizing the cross-entropy $CE(p,\pi_\theta)$, has a gradient that can be estimated by importance sampling from any proposal $q$ whose support covers $p$'s, giving an update that replaces REINFORCE's reward $R(x)$ with the ratio $P(x)/q(x)$ [1]. The paper gives two reasons for the design: unlike REINFORCE-style RL with a single scalar reward per full sequence, DPG is built to approximate a whole target distribution rather than just maximize an expectation, which the paper frames as giving it "wider applicability than distillation" while working even when distillation is infeasible (e.g. the target is defined only implicitly, as an energy-based model) [1]. Confirming the source: an arXiv title search for "Distributional Policy Gradient" surfaces Go et al.'s 2023 paper (arXiv:2302.08215, 127 citations) as the top-cited hit, but that paper explicitly attributes the name and the method to Parshakova et al., calling its own contribution "the original DPG (Distributional Policy Gradient) approach introduced in (Parshakova et al., 2019)" and treating it as a special case of its own generalization [2]. Parshakova et al. is the paper whose text contains the defining phrase quoted above, so it is cited here as the source of the method; the paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08517 [1].

DPG's main adopter is Khalifa, Elsahar and Dymetman's GDC (Generative Distributional Control), which calls DPG "suitable for our objective" of sampling from a sequential energy-based model with an autoregressive component, and uses a KL-adaptive variant of it as its core training algorithm for controlled text generation [3]. In GDC's own sentiment-control experiment, DPG-trained GDC reaches $\mathbb{E}[\phi(x)]=0.56$ at perplexity $13.53\pm3.18$ for positive sentiment, against PPLM's $0.52$ at $29.26\pm22.07$ and CTRL's $0.28$ at $76.52\pm90.51$ (Table 3) [3]. In the original DPG paper's own synthetic-data experiments (binary sequences filtered by a fixed motif, dataset size $|D|=500$), a DPG-trained policy reaches cross-entropy within 0.8% of a distillation-trained policy on the same target ($CE_{dpg}/CE_{dis}=1.008$) while showing a motif frequency 25% higher than distillation's ($1.252\times$) and a 24% cross-entropy reduction relative to the untrained autoregressive model ($CE_{dpg}/CE_r=0.76$) (Table 1) [1]. Lineage: REINFORCE (Williams, 1992, cited in [1]) -> DPG (Parshakova et al., 2019 [1]) -> adaptive DPG in GDC (Khalifa et al., 2021 [3]) -> CDPG, extending DPG to conditional targets (Korbak et al., 2022a [4]) -> f-DPG, generalizing DPG's forward-KL objective to any f-divergence with KL-DPG as the name for the original DPG special case (Go et al., 2023 [2]).

**When to pick it**: pick DPG when the thing you want the policy to match is a distribution given as an unnormalized potential $P(x)$ (an energy-based model or a set of pointwise/distributional constraints) rather than a per-sample scalar reward to be maximized, and you can sample from a reasonable proposal policy $q$ [1][3]. The nearest offline alternative is distillation, which the DPG paper directly compares against on the same synthetic task and finds comparably effective when the target can be sampled directly, but distillation requires being able to draw exact samples from the target and cannot be applied when the target is given only as an unnormalized potential [1]. The nearest online neighbor is RL with a KL penalty (the algorithmic core of RLHF): the f-DPG paper frames this contrast directly, stating that RLHF corresponds to minimizing a reverse KL, $KL(\pi_\theta\|p)$, from an implicit target arising from a KL penalty in the RL objective, while GDC/DPG minimizes a forward KL, $KL(p\|\pi_\theta)$, from an explicit target [2]. Do not use plain on-policy DPG (see How it works): the paper's own experiments with it did not converge reliably [1].

**Variant of**: Policy Gradient / REINFORCE (Williams, 1992), as cited by the DPG paper [1].

**Data it needs**: no labeled dataset in the conventional sense; the paper's setup needs an unnormalized target potential $P(x)$ and an initial proposal policy $q$ (in the paper's synthetic experiments, $q$ is a standard autoregressive model $r$ pretrained on the same domain) [1]. GDC's LM-alignment experiments build $P(x)$ from pointwise constraints (single words, word lists, classifier scores) or distributional constraints (target feature moments, e.g. gender balance), each evaluable on a sampled sequence [3]. Training scale in the paper: synthetic binary-sequence experiments with datasets ranging from 500 to 20000 sequences of length 30 [1]; GDC's LM experiments fine-tune GPT-2 small for 3K gradient-update steps on a NVIDIA V100 pair, up to about 72 hours for the longest run [3]. DPG is off-policy by design in its retained form: the paper's own on-policy variant (sampling from the policy being trained) was unstable, so the paper's algorithm (DPG$_{off}$) samples from a fixed proposal $q$ that is only periodically updated [1].

**Extra models**: none beyond the trained policy and the proposal $q$ used for sampling — $q$ is not gradient-trained, only used for sampling and occasionally replaced by a copy of the current policy [1]. No value network, no separate reward model is required by the method's definition (the reward-like signal is the ratio $P(x)/q(x)$, computed directly from the target potential); when the target itself is defined via a classifier or discriminator (as in some of GDC's constraints), that classifier plays the role the target-density evaluator, not a trained critic [3]. See Cost for what this means for memory relative to PPO-style methods.

**Shipped by**: no packaged training library implements DPG. Checked trl's trainer directory listing (`base_trainer.py`, `dpo_trainer.py`, `grpo_trainer.py`, `kto_trainer.py`, `reward_trainer.py`, `rloo_trainer.py`, `sft_trainer.py` — no DPG entry) [5] and verl's own documentation site index, which lists its algorithm pages as baseline, dapo, dppo, dro, entropy, gpg, grpo, opd, opo, otb, ppo, rollout_corr, rollout_corr_math, spin and sppo, with no "dpg" entry [6], both as of 2026-08-09; neither exports a DPG trainer. The only public implementation is research code accompanying GDC, CDPG and the RM-vs-DM paper at github.com/naver/gdc, not a pip-installable trainer [7]. Building it on top of an existing on-policy trainer would mean replacing the reward term with the importance-weighted ratio $P(x)/q(x)$ (or the pseudo-reward $r_\theta(x)=-f'(\pi_\theta(x)/p(x))$ for the f-DPG generalization) in a REINFORCE-style loss, and adding the periodic proposal-update logic of Algorithm 1/2 — a loss change on top of an existing sampling loop, not a new one, since no value network or PPO-style clipping machinery is required [1][3].

## How it works

The loop in one line: sample from a fixed proposal $q$, weight each sample by $P(x)/q(x)$, take a REINFORCE-style gradient step, and periodically refresh $q$ if the trained policy has gotten closer to the target.

**The objective.** DPG minimizes the forward KL divergence between the target $p$ and the policy, equivalently maximizing cross-entropy $CE(p,\pi_\theta)=-\sum_x p(x)\log\pi_\theta(x)$ [1][3]. Its gradient is derived by importance sampling from a proposal $q$ (Eq. 6 of the paper) [1]:

$$ \nabla_\theta CE(p,\pi_\theta) = \sum_x p(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\frac{P(x)}{q(x)}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)\right] $$

$P(x)$ is the unnormalized target potential, $Z$ its (unknown) normalizing constant, and $q$ a fixed proposal distribution whose support covers $p$'s [1]. The paper notes $1/Z$ can be dropped since during SGD it only rescales the learning rate [1]. This is REINFORCE with the reward $R(x)$ replaced by the importance-weighted ratio $P(x)/q(x)$ [1].

**On-policy vs. off-policy.** The on-policy variant, $DPG_{on}$ (Eq. 5), samples $x$ from the policy $\pi_\theta$ currently being trained: $\nabla_\theta CE(p,\pi_\theta)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\frac{P(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)\right]$ [1]. The paper reports that $DPG_{on}$ "had difficulty converging," attributed in part to instability from the constantly-changing sampling distribution — the same phenomenon that motivates TRPO and PPO in standard RL [1]. The paper's retained algorithm is $DPG_{off}$ (Algorithm 1): sample from a fixed $q$, update $\theta$ by the ratio $P(x)/q(x)$, and replace $q$ with the current $\pi_\theta$ only when $\pi_\theta$ is measured superior to $q$ on a validation set [1]. Khalifa et al.'s GDC uses a KL-adaptive version of the same loop (Algorithm 2), swapping the validation-perplexity criterion for a direct check on $D_{KL}(p\|\pi_\theta) < D_{KL}(p\|q)$ [3].

**Worked micro-example.** Take a target with $P(x_1)=2$, $P(x_2)=1$ (so $p(x_1)=2/3$, $p(x_2)=1/3$ once normalized) and a proposal $q$ that assigns $q(x_1)=0.4$, $q(x_2)=0.6$. Sampling $x_1$ from $q$ gives importance weight $P(x_1)/q(x_1)=2/0.4=5$; sampling $x_2$ gives $P(x_2)/q(x_2)=1/0.6\approx1.67$. Each SGD step multiplies $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)$ by that sample's weight, so a step on $x_1$ pushes $\pi_\theta(x_1)$ up roughly three times as hard as a step on $x_2$ pushes $\pi_\theta(x_2)$ up — reflecting that $q$ under-samples $x_1$ relative to how much the target favors it.

**Baseline (f-DPG's addition, not in the original paper).** The 2023 generalization of DPG to any f-divergence writes the per-sample gradient term as a pseudo-reward $r_\theta(x)=-f'(\pi_\theta(x)/p(x))$, of which DPG's ratio $P(x)/\pi_\theta(x)$ is the special case for $f(t)=-\log t$ ("KL-DPG") [2]. That paper shows subtracting a constant baseline $B$ from $r_\theta(x)$ does not bias the gradient, since $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)=0$ [2]:

$$ \nabla_\theta D_f(\pi_\theta\|p) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r_\theta(x)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}\big[(r_\theta(x)-B)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x)\big],\quad B\doteq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi_\theta}[r_\theta(x)] $$

The technique is credited to Williams, Baxter & Bartlett, and Schulman et al., and to Korbak et al.'s RM-vs-DM paper for applying it specifically to DPG [2][8]. The original 2019 DPG paper does not include this baseline [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: DPG's update is a single REINFORCE-style gradient on the policy being trained; there is no value network and no second trained model in the objective [1], unlike PPO-family methods that hold a comparable-size critic. Each step needs one forward pass of the policy to compute $\log\pi_\theta(x)$ on sampled sequences and one evaluation of the target potential $P(x)$; the proposal $q$ is only used for sampling, not backpropagated through, so its cost is inference-only except when it is periodically overwritten with a copy of $\pi_\theta$ [1]. A naive reading might assume off-policy sampling from a stale $q$ is "free" relative to on-policy generation, but the paper's own account shows the opposite pressure drove the design: on-policy sampling from the model being trained was tried first and abandoned for instability, not for cost [1] — the off-policy choice is a stability decision, not a cost optimization, and the theory layer does not show whether generation or gradient computation dominates wall-clock time.

**In practice, per framework**: no shipping framework implements DPG (see Shipped by), so no framework-level cost claims can be made here; the reference implementation is the unpackaged research code at github.com/naver/gdc, pinned at commit `a59c5b7e` (2022-12-07) [7], which this card did not profile for wall-clock or memory cost.

## How to use it

Data preparation is defining the target $P(x)$, not curating labeled examples: in GDC's pointwise setting this is as simple as a single word or a wordlist/classifier score to hit; in its distributional setting it is a target feature-moment vector (e.g., a desired gender balance in generated biographies) that the potential is built to match [3]. The reward/label convention of the reference implementation is the ratio $P(x)/q(x)$ computed per sampled sequence, not a per-token or human-labeled reward [1].

| knob | Parshakova et al. (synthetic data) [1] | Khalifa et al. GDC (LM alignment) [3] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| proposal $q$ | pretrained autoregressive model $r$ | pretrained GPT-2 small (117M) |
| learning rate | not stated | 0.0000141 (Adam) |
| batch size | not stated | 2048 |
| gradient steps per iteration ($K$) | not stated | 20480 (pointwise); 20480, same $K$, for distributional |
| proposal-update criterion | validation-set cross-entropy comparison, $\pi_\theta$ vs. $q$ | $D_{KL}(p\|\pi_\theta) < D_{KL}(p\|q)$ |
| total training steps | not stated | 3,000 gradient updates |
| sampling for generation | not stated | nucleus sampling, top-$p$=0.9 |

  "not stated" above means the value was checked in the cited source and is absent, not merely unchecked. The two rows are not directly comparable settings of one knob: Parshakova's paper works with short (length-30) binary sequences and small synthetic datasets, while Khalifa's GDC fine-tunes a 117M-parameter LM, so the batch size and step-count columns describe different problem scales, not two tunings of the same task [1][3].

Trade-off a run designer faces: how close the proposal $q$ starts to the target $p$ directly controls efficiency — Khalifa et al. note that in the limit where $q$ equals $p$ the algorithm becomes equivalent to standard supervised learning, so a better-informed initial proposal (a domain-pretrained model rather than a generic one) reduces the number of off-policy correction steps needed [3]. The alternative to periodic proposal refresh is to never refresh $q$, trading faster iterations for a proposal that drifts further from $\pi_\theta$ and gives noisier importance weights over training.

## While it runs

- **Signals and their healthy shapes**: neither DPG's defining paper nor GDC report a first-hand live-monitoring guide comparable to a framework's logging docs; the closest first-hand signal is the paper's own tracked metric, cross-entropy of the trained policy against a held-out target sample $CE(T,\pi_\theta)$, which should decrease toward the entropy of the true target process $H(p_{true})$ as training proceeds [1].
- **Published reference runs**: Khalifa et al.'s Table 3 sentiment-control run is the closest to a published reference curve available in the sources read: DPG-trained GDC reaches $\mathbb{E}[\phi(x)]=0.56$, perplexity $13.53\pm3.18$ for positive sentiment and $\mathbb{E}[\phi(x)]=0.51$, perplexity $13.59\pm3.84$ for negative sentiment, both well below PPLM's and CTRL's perplexity at comparable or lower alignment scores [3]. Neither source read for this card links raw training logs.
- **Degeneracies and defaults**: the on-policy variant $DPG_{on}$ is a documented failure mode, not a tunable option — the paper reports it had difficulty converging and retained only the off-policy variant for all experiments [1]. A proposal $q$ that never gets refreshed leaves the importance weights $P(x)/q(x)$ increasingly mismatched to the policy actually being trained, which is exactly the condition the proposal-update rule in Algorithm 1/2 exists to correct [1][3].
- **Named successors**: CDPG extends DPG from unconditional to conditional target distributions, addressing the original method's restriction to unconditional distributions [4]. f-DPG generalizes DPG's forward-KL objective (named "KL-DPG" as the DPG special case) to any f-divergence, and reports that KL-DPG shows "low alignment but high diversity" compared to reverse-KL and Jensen-Shannon variants, with Jensen-Shannon ("JS-DPG") recommended as the best-balanced default across its experiments [2].
- **Known failure modes**: from the f-DPG paper's own discussion — the forward-KL objective that defines original DPG "can be sensitive to sampling noise in the finite sample approximation" of the importance-weighted gradient [2]. The RM-vs-DM paper's own analysis states that DPG gradient estimates suffer from the same high-variance problem as standard Policy Gradient estimates, since DPG must estimate a PG-style term from sampled sequences despite its distribution-matching objective differing from reward maximization; this motivated its baseline technique described in How it works [8]. No maintainer-issue-tracker failure modes were found: DPG has no shipping library (see Shipped by), so there is no issue tracker to search; this card did not search GitHub issues on naver/gdc specifically.
- **What the gain is - and is not**: the original paper's own analysis shows DPG's gain is data efficiency in matching a target distribution — the trained policy reaches target-matching quality with far less data than the base autoregressive model needs to reach the same quality on its own, and does at least as well as distillation when distillation is feasible [1]. It is not a capability-adding method: nothing in the cited sources claims DPG lets a policy reach behavior outside what the target potential $P(x)$ itself specifies.
