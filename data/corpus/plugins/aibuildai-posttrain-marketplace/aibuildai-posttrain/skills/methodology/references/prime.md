# PRIME

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01456

Skip the process-label collection step: turn an outcome-only reward model into a token-level process reward model for free by reading it as a log-probability ratio against a reference model, then update that reward model online alongside the policy and feed its per-token score into a standard leave-one-out policy-gradient update.

**PRIME** (Process Reinforcement through IMplicit rEwards) is an online-RL method for LLM reasoning that trains dense, token-level reward signals without ever collecting a step-level label, introduced to make process rewards practical inside online RL [1]. The defining paper states PRIME "enables online PRM updates using only policy rollouts and outcome labels through implicit process rewards," and that it "combines well with various advantage functions and forgoes the dedicated reward model training phase that existing approaches require" [1]. Its parent building block is implicit process reward modeling: Yuan et al. show that training an outcome reward model (ORM) while parameterizing the reward as the log-likelihood ratio of a policy model over a reference model yields, at inference, a token-level process reward model at no extra label cost [2]. PRIME layers this trick onto an online RL loop: the policy and the implicit PRM are both initialized from the same SFT (or base) checkpoint, every rollout is scored by a rule-based outcome verifier, and the implicit PRM is retrained online with a cross-entropy loss on those same outcome labels before its per-token scores are turned into advantages for a PPO-clipped policy update [1]. The paper gives three reasons for this design: annotating step-level correctness labels is expensive and often ambiguous, since a locally wrong step can still lead to a correct final answer; a static (offline) reward model degrades under distribution shift as the policy moves, so the reward model must be updated online to resist overoptimization and reward hacking; and building a dedicated PRM training pipeline beforehand is itself a costly stage that PRIME removes entirely [1].

PRIME's own flagship result is Eurus-2-7B-PRIME: starting from Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base with a lightweight SFT warm-up, PRIME lifts average accuracy across seven competition math/coding benchmarks by 15.1% over the SFT model, with the abstract stating it "surpasses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on seven reasoning benchmarks" using 10% of Qwen-Math's training data, while the paper's own introduction paragraph instead reports the surpassed count as five key mathematical benchmarks with the same 10%-of-data claim — the two counts inside the same paper disagree [1]. Against the paper's own outcome-only RLOO baseline at the same 240 training steps, PRIME reaches 41.0 average accuracy versus RLOO's 36.9 (Table 1), and reaches RLOO's final training reward in 40% of the steps, a reported 2.5x sample-efficiency gain [1]. The recipe was folded into the verl framework as an optional, separately-versioned recipe rather than a core supported trainer [3][4]. Lineage in one line: implicit PRM reward parameterization (Yuan et al., 2024 [2]) + RLOO advantage estimation (Ahmadian et al., 2024 [5]) + PPO's clipped surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017 [6]) -> PRIME (2025 [1]); no named successor method that revises PRIME's own design was found in a Semantic Scholar citation-list scan for titles containing "PRIME," "process reward," or "implicit" among 100 fetched citing papers (2026-08-09) [7].

**When to pick it**: pick PRIME over plain outcome-only online RL when you have a cheap, per-response outcome verifier (e.g., an exact-match math checker or unit tests) and want denser, token-level credit assignment without collecting step labels or running a separate PRM training stage first; the paper's own ablation shows it beating outcome-only RLOO at matched training steps [1]. Prefer plain RLOO [5] (no dense reward) when you lack budget for the extra PRM forward/backward pass per step, or when outcome-only credit assignment is already adequate for the task. Prefer a classic, separately-trained step-labeled PRM (e.g., Lightman et al.'s process supervision) when you can afford human or MCTS-derived step annotations and do not need online reward updates. Prefer offline DPO [8] when you only have static preference pairs and no fresh-rollout budget at all, since DPO trains on pairs of completions to a prompt without further sampling [9].

**Variant of**: not a single-parent variant — it is a reward-shaping layer usable with several policy-gradient algorithms; the paper's default instantiation is RLOO's leave-one-out advantage estimator [5], and the same paper reports plugging the same reward machinery into REINFORCE, GRPO, and PPO as well [1].

**Data it needs**: prompts with a machine-checkable outcome verifier — LaTeX-matched answers for math or unit-test pass rates for code, no step-level labels — plus the rollouts sampled from the current policy each step [1]. The paper's RL stage draws from a curated pool of 457k math and 27k coding problems collected and filtered from NuminaMath-CoT, APPS, CodeContests, TACO, and Codeforces [1]. Training is on-policy: each iteration samples K fresh responses per prompt from the current policy (K=4 in the main experiments) and updates both the policy and the implicit PRM on those same rollouts [1]; no pre-collected response set can be substituted.

**Extra models**: no value network. Two extra models are structurally required by the method's own definition: a trainable implicit PRM (initialized from the same SFT/base checkpoint as the policy, updated online by a cross-entropy loss on outcome labels) and a frozen reference model used inside the implicit-reward log-ratio, also initialized from the SFT/base checkpoint and kept fixed for reference log-probabilities [1]. The paper sets the policy-loss KL coefficient to 0 in all experiments, so no KL-driven reference term is added to the policy loss itself, but the reference model is still needed for the implicit-reward computation regardless of that KL setting [1]. See Cost for the resulting per-step cost breakdown.

**Shipped by**: verl, as an optional recipe (`recipe/prime`, entry point `main_prime.py` calling `RayPRIMETrainer`) hosted in the separately versioned `verl-project/verl-recipe` repository and pulled into verl as a git submodule, rather than one of verl's `examples/`-listed core trainers (PPO, GRPO, RLOO, DAPO are also recipe-only or core-listed on the same page, and PRIME sits in the recipe list) [3][4]. The paper's own authors also released a standalone reference implementation, PRIME-RL/PRIME, an Apache-2.0-licensed research repository (not a pip-installable package) that bundles its own fork of verl under `training/verl` [10]. trl's `__init__.py` exports no PRIME-named trainer [11].

## How it works

Each iteration: sample K responses per prompt from the current policy; score them with the outcome verifier and an online-updated implicit PRM; turn both scores into leave-one-out advantages; update the policy with a PPO-clipped loss [1].

**Implicit process reward.** The Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$` is trained exactly like an ORM (cross-entropy on outcome labels), but its reward is parameterized as a log-probability ratio against the reference model `$\pi_{ref}$`, which lets the same model be read as a per-token process reward at inference time [1][2]:

$$ r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t \mid y_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(y_t \mid y_{<t})} $$

`$\beta$` is a temperature-like scaling coefficient (set to 0.05 for PRM training in the main experiments) [1]. Every training iteration, `$\pi_\phi$` is updated by cross-entropy loss against the ground-truth outcome label `$r_o(y) \in \{0,1\}$` on the same on-policy rollouts used for the policy update [1]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{CE}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,r_o(y)) \sim T}\left[ r_o(y)\cdot \log \sigma(r_\phi(y)) + (1-r_o(y))\cdot \log(1-\sigma(r_\phi(y))) \right] $$

**Advantage: leave-one-out on both reward streams.** With K samples per prompt, the process-reward return and the outcome-reward return are each computed with an RLOO leave-one-out baseline and then summed [1]:

$$ A_{i,t} = \underbrace{\sum_{s=t}^{|y_i|} \gamma^{s-t}\left(r_\phi(y_i^s) - \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j \neq i} r_\phi(y_j)\right)}_{\text{RLOO with implicit process rewards}} + \underbrace{\left(r_o(y_i) - \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j \neq i} r_o(y_j)\right)}_{\text{RLOO with outcome rewards}} $$

The paper is explicit that the two return streams are computed separately and then added, rather than mixed inside one baseline, because directly mixing their values "may lead to numerical instability" [1]. Worked example with K=4 and outcome rewards `$\{1,0,0,0\}$` (identical to the GRPO-style group case): the outcome-reward RLOO term for the correct response is `$1 - \tfrac{1}{3}(0+0+0) = 1$`, and for each wrong response it is `$0 - \tfrac{1}{3}(1+0+0) = -\tfrac{1}{3}$`; the paper does not state whether its `$K-1=3$` example rollouts in practice ever include ties, so this arithmetic is illustrative only, following the formula's own convention.

**Policy update.** The policy is updated with the same PPO clipped surrogate used by PPO itself, with `$\epsilon$` the clip range [1][6]:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{CLIP}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|y_{<t})} A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_t|y_{<t})}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\right) A_t\right)\right] $$

**Variant advantage estimators the paper itself tries.** Section 5.3 swaps the advantage-estimation function while leaving the clip loss unchanged, and reports that PRIME "boosts" REINFORCE, GRPO, and PPO as well as RLOO [1]; Section 5.4 additionally compares using the Implicit PRM as a value baseline (`$A_t = r_o(y) - v_\phi(y_{<t})$`) against using it as a reward stream, and finds the reward-stream use (PRIME's default) clearly outperforms both a linear-head value model and an Implicit-PRM-as-value baseline [1]. Neither trl nor verl's `recipe/prime` code was checked in this card for whether they expose these non-default advantage estimators; verl's shipped recipe uses the RLOO-based advantage above [4].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**

- Time: relative to outcome-only RLOO, PRIME adds one full forward pass of the reference model and one forward+backward pass of the implicit PRM per training iteration, both over the same rollout tokens already generated and scored by the verifier — there is no extra generation cost from the reward side, since the implicit PRM scores existing rollouts rather than sampling new ones [1].
- Memory: relative to outcome-only RLOO, PRIME holds two more model copies of roughly policy size — the trainable implicit PRM (optimizer state and gradients, since it is updated online) and the frozen reference model (weights only, forward pass only) — while never holding a PPO-style value network at all [1].
- A naive reading of the advantage formula suggests the leave-one-out sums over K samples require holding K full response activations simultaneously; the practice-layer entry below shows the actual implementations reduce this to K reward/return scalars at advantage-computation time, not K sets of activations.

**In practice, per framework:**

- The paper's own veRL-based reference implementation reports a measured, not just theoretical, step-time breakdown at K=4 (Table 2): PRIME's per-step time is Rollout 281.7s + Policy update 156.6s + PRM update 150.9s + Others 91.1s = 680.3s total, versus RLOO's Rollout 282.4s + Policy update 157.9s + PRM update 0s + Others 90.4s = 530.7s total, which the paper states is a 24% increase in per-step time from PRIME [1]. The paper also states that veRL's single-controller design lets the policy and rollout engine offload to CPU during the PRM update, so the extra PRM-update pass adds no extra GPU memory in that implementation [1].
- The paper directly states that because PRIME reaches RLOO's training reward in about 40% of the training steps (Figure 3), PRIME would still be 2x more efficient than RLOO when estimated by training time, despite the 24% per-step overhead above [1].
- verl's `recipe/prime` config splits a rollout batch into `ppo_mini_batch_size` gradient-update sub-batches and further into `ppo_micro_batch_size_per_gpu` forward-pass chunks, the general verl mechanism for bounding per-forward-pass memory independent of K; this card did not verify whether `recipe/prime`'s own config file sets these to values different from verl's core PPO/GRPO defaults [4].

## How to use it

- Reward: a rule-based outcome verifier, `$r_o(y)=1$` on exact match for math and `$r_o(y) = \frac{\#\text{passes}}{\#\text{test cases}}$` for coding in the main experiments [1]; the same verifier's binary/fractional label also supervises the implicit PRM's cross-entropy update.
- Prompt filtering: the paper describes filtering prompts to "a certain median-level difficulty range" based on the policy's own rollout accuracy, and reports this online filter substantially lowers RL training variance (Figure 2) [1]; the paper's own main text does not give the numeric bounds of that range. The project's GitHub README states the filter keeps only prompts on which the policy achieves an accuracy between 0.2 and 0.8 [10] — this specific number is a repository claim, not one this card verified against the paper's own text.
- PRM initialization: the paper finds that initializing the implicit PRM from the same SFT/base checkpoint as the policy outperforms initializing from a separately, more heavily trained PRM (EurusPRM, trained on an extra 500K samples), because same-checkpoint initialization reduces the distribution-shift gap between PRM and policy (Figure 4) [1].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give the value; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key):

| knob | paper's main experiment [1] | verl `recipe/prime` default | trl default |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| policy learning rate | 5e-7 (constant, AdamW) | not checked | n/a — no PRIME trainer |
| PRM learning rate | 1e-6 | not checked | n/a |
| samples per prompt (K) | 4 | not checked | n/a |
| batch size | 256 prompts, micro-batch 8 | not checked | n/a |
| implicit-reward temperature $\beta$ | 0.05 | not checked | n/a |
| policy-loss KL coefficient | 0 (in all experiments) | not checked | n/a |

  This card only verified verl's `recipe/prime` entry point and submodule pin (Cost, Shipped by); it did not open `recipe/prime`'s own config YAML to confirm whether its shipped defaults match the paper's hyperparameters above, so the verl column above is left "not checked" rather than guessed.
- Trade-off in K (samples per prompt): the RLOO leave-one-out baseline used for both reward streams needs `$K \geq 2$` to be non-degenerate (see While it runs); larger K gives a steadier baseline for both the outcome-reward and process-reward RLOO terms at the cost of more generation and more implicit-PRM scoring per prompt, at a fixed token budget meaning fewer distinct prompts per batch.
- Install, launch, and cluster-parallelism detail for verl's `recipe/prime` belongs on the verl framework card, not here.

## While it runs

- PRM classification accuracy: the paper's own diagnostic for reward-hacking risk is to hold out paired correct/incorrect samples and track the implicit PRM's classification accuracy over training; the paper reports that an offline (frozen) PRM starts with high accuracy but "gradually drops" over RL training due to distribution shift, while online-updated PRMs show "the reverse curve," rising over training (Figure 5) [1] — this is first-hand, paper-stated guidance, not a framework-logged metric name.
- Published reference run: the paper's own Figure 4 gives training-reward and test-accuracy curves comparing three PRM initialization/update strategies (online SFT-initialized PRM, offline EurusPRM, online EurusPRM) over 200 steps, and Table 1 gives the full step-by-step benchmark table for Eurus-2-7B-PRIME versus outcome-only RLOO up to step 592/240 respectively [1]; both are the known-good curves to compare a new PRIME run against. This card checked only the verl README, the verl-recipe `prime/` directory listing and its README, and the PRIME-RL/PRIME repository README for a separately published reference run or logged benchmark curve, and found none in those three sources [3][4][10]; a broader search of verl's issue tracker or external blog posts was not performed.
- Degeneracy: with K=1 (a single sample per prompt) the leave-one-out baseline for both the outcome-reward and process-reward RLOO terms is undefined, since the sum `$\sum_{j\neq i}$` over the other K-1 samples has no terms; the paper's own formula requires `$K \geq 2$` to produce any baseline at all [1]. Uniform outcome rewards within a group (all rollouts equally right or wrong) drive the outcome-reward RLOO term to zero for every sample in that group, removing that prompt's outcome-side training signal for the step, matching the same degeneracy the paper's own online prompt filter is designed to reduce by excluding easy/hard prompts (see How to use it) [1].
- Numerical-precision failure mode from the maintainers: in a closed GitHub issue on the official repository, a maintainer states that loading the PRM in bf16 caused convergence issues in their own experiments, and that the newer code defaults to fp32 for that reason; in a follow-up reply on the same issue the maintainer adds that this fp32 default is not necessary for the actor/rollout/reference workers, that the bf16-vs-PRM question was still being validated generally, but that on larger (32B) PRMs bf16 was in fact observed to converge well [12].
- Named successors: none found. A Semantic Scholar citation-list scan of the 100 most recent papers citing the PRIME paper, filtered for titles containing "PRIME," "process reward," or "implicit," returned five 2026 papers on related process-reward topics but none presented as a direct, bias-fixing successor to PRIME's own design in the way DAPO or Dr. GRPO revise GRPO [7]; this is a title-level scan of one citation page, not a full-text search, and does not rule out an unlisted or differently-titled successor.
- What the gain is — and is not: the paper's own ablations attribute PRIME's improvement over outcome-only RLOO specifically to the dense, per-token reward signal and its online update (Sections 4.3 and 5.1), and separately show that an added value-model baseline does not help over plain REINFORCE (Section 5.4) [1]; the paper does not present a Maj@K-vs-Pass@K style capability-ceiling analysis for PRIME itself (unlike GRPO's own paper for GRPO), so this card cannot state from PRIME's own text whether PRIME only reranks the base model's existing samples or extends what it can reach.
