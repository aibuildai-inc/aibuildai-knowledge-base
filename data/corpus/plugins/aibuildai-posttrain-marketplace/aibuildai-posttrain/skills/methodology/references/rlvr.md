# RLVR

RLHF's PPO loop with the learned reward model swapped for a deterministic checker: sample a completion, give it a fixed reward if it is verifiably correct (e.g. matches a math answer or satisfies a constraint) and zero otherwise, then optimize with PPO against a KL penalty to a frozen reference.

**RLVR** (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) is a post-training method introduced by the Tülu 3 paper as the fourth stage of its post-training recipe, for tasks with verifiable outcomes such as mathematical problem-solving and instruction following [1]. Its parent is the KL-constrained RLHF objective that the paper attributes jointly to Ziegler et al. 2019, Stiennon et al. 2020, and Ouyang et al. 2022, which optimizes a policy against a learned reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ minus a $\beta$-weighted KL penalty from a reference policy [1], with InstructGPT one instance of it, fine-tuned via reinforcement learning from human feedback on a dataset of rankings of model outputs [2]; RLVR leverages the existing RLHF objective but replaces the reward model with a verification function [1], and the paper trains it with PPO [1][3], the same policy-optimization algorithm it uses for standard RLHF against a learned reward model [1]. The paper gives two reasons beyond simplicity: it targets domains such as mathematics and verifiable instruction following where a deterministic answer-matching or constraint-checking function is available, and it produces targeted gains on benchmarks like GSM8K without needing a trained reward model for those skills [1]. The correct paper was confirmed as the top-cited collision match among candidates sharing the name: Spurious Rewards [4], RLPR [5], and The Invisible Leash [6] all use the term as defined by Tülu 3 without redefining it, and R1-Omni applies the same method to an omni-multimodal model rather than proposing a different one [7].

Tülu 3 itself is the landmark system: RLVR is the final training stage for the released Llama-3.1-Tülu-3 8B/70B/405B models [1]. DeepSeek-R1 also incentivizes reasoning through pure reinforcement learning, reaching superior performance on verifiable tasks such as mathematics, coding competitions, and STEM fields [8]; trl's documentation states that its example reward function for `GRPOTrainer` is inspired by the accuracy-reward function from that same DeepSeek-R1 paper [9]. In the originating paper's own 405B ablation (Table 4), the RLVR stage raised MATH from 59.9% (post-DPO) to 67.3%, GSM8K from 94.2% to 95.5%, and IFEval (loose prompt) from 85.0% to 86.0%, while the safety-inclusive average (the "Avg w/ Safety" row, which folds the safety benchmark into the averaged score) rose from 79.6% to 80.7% [1]. Lineage in one line: RLHF-PPO with a learned RM (as formalized in [1], with InstructGPT, 2022 [2], one instance of it) -> RLVR swaps in a verifier (Tülu 3, 2024 [1]) -> paired in practice with GRPO in open-instruct's later recipes [10][11] -> studied/limited by Spurious Rewards, RLPR, and The Invisible Leash (2025 [4][5][6]).

**When to pick it**: online RL for tasks where a fixed, deterministic checker can grade a completion as correct or not (exact-match math, code execution, format/constraint following) and you want to skip training a reward model for that skill [1]. Prefer standard RLHF-PPO with a learned reward model [1][3] when correctness cannot be checked mechanically and you only have human or model preference judgments. Prefer DPO [12] when you have static preference pairs and no way (or budget) to sample fresh completions and score them: DPO is offline and optimizes directly on chosen/rejected pairs without an RL loop or reward model [12]. The nearest online neighbor is GRPO [10]: RLVR names the reward source (a verifier, not a learned RM) and is agnostic to the RL algorithm, while GRPO is a specific way to compute the advantage (group-relative, no value network); the two are combined in open-instruct's current RLVR training script, which runs on a GRPO trainer rather than the PPO trainer the original paper used [11].

**Variant of**: the KL-constrained RLHF objective with a learned reward model, trained with online RL via PPO [1], with InstructGPT one instance of that RLHF objective [2], and with the reward model term $r_\phi(x,y)$ replaced by a deterministic verifier $v(x,y)$ [1].

**Data it needs**: prompts paired with a binary verifier function, not a scalar reward model or preference pairs. The paper's own mixture used 7,473 GSM8K training prompts (exact-match on the extracted final answer), 7,500 MATH training prompts (exact-match via "flex" MATH evaluation logic), and 14,973 instruction-following prompts checked against per-constraint verifier functions from the IFEval taxonomy, for 29,946 prompts total [1]. On-policy: completions are sampled fresh from the current policy each PPO step, following preference tuning in the pipeline (the paper trains RLVR after DPO) [1].

**Extra models**: as PPO's objective, RLVR needs a value network (critic) and a frozen reference model for the KL term; no reward model is required for the verified reward itself, since $v(x,y)$ replaces it [1]. The paper's own ablation found initializing the value model from a general reward model trained on preference data gives the highest GSM8K and average scores, better than initializing it from the anchored DPO policy [1] — so a reward model is commonly present anyway, as a value-network initializer rather than as the reward source. Details in Cost.

**Shipped by**: no framework ships a trainer literally named "RLVR" — it is a reward-function convention (return 1/α for a verified-correct completion, 0 otherwise) applied on top of an existing on-policy trainer. AI2's own open-instruct repository is the reference implementation: its README documents RLVR training via `open_instruct/grpo_fast.py`, i.e. a GRPO trainer, not the PPO trainer the paper used [11]. trl ships this pattern as a documented reward-function example for `GRPOTrainer`, returning 1.0/0.0 by regex-matching a boxed answer against `ground_truth` [9]; trl's `PPOTrainer` instead takes a trained reward-model checkpoint via `--reward_model_path`, not an arbitrary Python reward function [13]. verl calls the same pattern a "rule-based reward model" in its GSM8K example, assigning reward 1 for a correct final answer, 0.1 for incorrect, 0 for no answer, and runs it through `verl.trainer.main_ppo` [14].

## How it works

Each step: sample a completion from the current policy given a prompt with a known verifier; run the verifier to get a binary correctness signal; scale it to a fixed reward; optimize the policy with PPO against that reward plus a KL penalty to a frozen reference [1].

**The objective** [1]:

$$ \max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(x)} \left[ R_{RLVR}(x, y) \right] = \left[ v(x, y) - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{ref}(y \mid x)\right] \right] $$

$$ v(x, y) = \begin{cases} \alpha & \text{if correct,} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} $$

$v$ is the verifiable reward function: it checks whether the answer inside completion $y$ to prompt $x$ is correct and returns a fixed scalar $\alpha$ if so, else 0 [1]; $\pi_{ref}$ is the frozen reference policy and $\beta$ controls how far the trained policy $\pi_\theta$ is allowed to drift from it [1]. This is the same KL-constrained form used for the standard RLHF objective with a learned reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ in place of $v(x,y)$ [1]. The paper trains against this objective with PPO [1][3], setting $\alpha = 10$ from pilot experiments without further tuning [1].

Worked example: a math prompt sampled once, checker regex-extracts the boxed answer and compares to ground truth. If it matches, $v(x,y) = \alpha = 10$ (the paper's fixed value [1]); if not, $v(x,y) = 0$. With $\beta = 0.05$ (the paper's final 8B setting [1]) and a completion whose average per-token KL to the reference is, say, 2 nats, the realized reward for that completion is $10 - 0.05 \times 2 = 9.9$ if correct, or $0 - 0.05 \times 2 = -0.1$ if not — PPO then turns this scalar into a per-token advantage the same way it would for a learned reward model's score, via its value network baseline.

**PPO implementation details the paper found necessary** [1]: initialize the value model from a general reward model rather than from scratch or from the DPO checkpoint; disable dropout during RM and RL training so token log-probabilities are deterministic across the rollout and learning phases (otherwise the probability ratio can start away from 1 and get clipped everywhere, killing the gradient); train past one epoch over the verifiable-prompt set, shuffling between epochs (their ablations ran roughly 100,000/7,473 ≈ 13 epochs over GSM8K); apply a -10 penalty when a sampled response does not end with an EOS token; and whiten/normalize advantages (subtract mean, divide by std) as in standard PPO practice.

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math:**
- Time: one PPO rollout (generation) and one PPO update pass per step, exactly as in standard PPO — RLVR changes only where the scalar reward comes from, not PPO's control flow [1][3]. Unlike a learned reward model, the verifier itself is typically cheap (regex/exact-match or a rule check) rather than a forward pass through a second network, but this is a property of the verifier chosen, not of the RLVR objective.
- Memory: the objective still needs a policy, a value network, and a frozen reference model held at once — the same three-model footprint as standard PPO-based RLHF [1][2]; RLVR removes the reward model's role as the reward source but the paper's own best recipe still keeps one around, to initialize the value network [1]. A naive reading — "no reward model, so RLVR is cheaper than PPO-RLHF" — is not correct in the paper's own best-performing configuration.

**In practice, per framework:**
- open-instruct [1][11]: the 8B reward model trains in 9 hours on 8 H100s; the final 8B RLVR run itself takes 65 hours on 8 GPUs, the 70B run 60 hours on 48 GPUs, and the 405B run 46 hours on 256 GPUs [1]. The codebase adapts distributed-RLHF model-allocation techniques and runs generation asynchronously from training to raise throughput [1]; its current RLVR entry point (`grpo_fast.py`) runs the reward on a GRPO trainer rather than the PPO trainer the paper describes, which removes the value network from the held models entirely — policy and frozen reference only, no critic — in exchange for sampling several completions per prompt to build the group baseline [11][10].
- verl [14]: runs the rule-based-reward pattern through `verl.trainer.main_ppo`, i.e. the standard PPO trainer, which by its own config defaults holds a policy, a critic, and a reference model, with `algorithm.adv_estimator` defaulting to `gae` (GAE-based PPO, not GRPO's group baseline) [15]; complexity and memory knobs (micro-batch sizes, etc.) are the PPO trainer's, not specific to the verifiable-reward substitution.
- trl [9]: the reward function shown above is a plain Python callable passed to `GRPOTrainer`, whose own `beta` defaults to `0.0`, in which case the frozen reference model is not loaded, reducing memory usage and improving training speed [9]; at `beta` > 0 a reference model is loaded, and generation samples `num_generations` completions per prompt for the group baseline [9]. It is not usable with `PPOTrainer`, which takes a trained reward-model checkpoint via `--reward_model_path` instead of a reward-function callable [13].

## How to use it

- Data prep: pair each prompt with a verifier, not a scalar label. The paper's own recipe used exact-match on extracted final answers for GSM8K and MATH (with few-shot CoT prompting to encourage chain-of-thought at generation time) and per-constraint checker functions from an IFEval-style taxonomy for instruction-following prompts [1].
- Reward convention: return a fixed positive value ($\alpha$) for a verified-correct completion and 0 otherwise [1]; trl's documented example instead uses 1.0/0.0 [9], and verl's GSM8K example uses 1 / 0.1 / 0 for correct / incorrect / no-answer [14] — the exact scale is a per-implementation choice, since PPO's advantage normalization removes most of its effect.
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give it; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key):

| knob | paper (Tülu 3, final RLVR configs) [1] | trl reward-function pattern [9] | verl rule-based-reward example [14] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reward for correct ($\alpha$) | 10 (fixed by pilot experiments) | 1.0 | 1 |
| reward for incorrect | 0 | 0.0 | 0.1† |
| KL coefficient $\beta$ | 8B: 0.05; 70B: 0.07 (swept over [0.1, 0.05, 0.03, 0.01] in ablations) | `GRPOTrainer` `beta` default 0.0 (KL off; reference model not loaded at this setting) [9] | `use_kl_in_reward` default `False` [15] |
| discount $\gamma$ | 1.0 | not applicable (GRPO has no value network, so no GAE) [9] | `algorithm.gamma` default 1.0 [15] |
| GAE $\lambda$ | 0.95 | not applicable | `algorithm.lam` default 1.0 [15] |
| PPO clip $\varepsilon$ | 0.2 | `GRPOTrainer` own `epsilon` default 0.2 (clips the policy/old-policy ratio; no critic involved) [9] | `actor.clip_ratio` default 0.2 [15] |
| PPO update iterations $K$ | 4 (vs. 1 when optimizing a general RM) | not checked | not checked |
| response length | 2,048 (1,024 for GSM8K only) | not stated | not stated |
| total episodes | 100,000 | not stated | not stated |

† verl's 0.1 is not the same quantity as the paper's and trl's 0/0.0: the paper and trl pay nothing for an incorrect completion, while verl's GSM8K example gives an incorrect-but-attempted answer partial credit (0.1) and reserves 0 for no answer at all, a three-way scale rather than a binary one [14].

- Trade-off the paper's own ablations surface: lowering $\beta$ increases KL from the reference and typically improves the targeted verifiable-reward score but can hurt the broader average score — the paper calls this overoptimization, and shows overoptimized IFEval outputs at higher KL budgets in its appendix [1]. Initializing the value network from a general preference-trained reward model beat initializing it from the anchored DPO policy in their ablation, on both the GSM8K test score and the average score [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes, from the paper's own ablation figures: verifiable reward on the training set (fraction of sampled completions the checker accepts) should rise; KL divergence to the reference grows with training and with lower $\beta$; response length is tracked alongside both because it can drift as a side effect of optimization pressure — the paper plots all three against training episodes for GSM8K, MATH, and constrained-instruction prompts [1].
- Published reference runs: Figure 19 in the paper reports these three curves for GSM8K, MATH, and instruction-constraint prompts across $\beta \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1\}$, starting from a DPO checkpoint, as the known-good shape to compare against [1].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper explicitly reports "overoptimization happens" — as $\beta$ is lowered, KL from the initial model grows, and higher KL typically corresponds to lower average scores even while the targeted verifiable reward keeps rising, with IFEval showing visibly overoptimized outputs at high-KL settings in their qualitative appendix [1]. The paper also found that adding reward-model scores on top of the verifiable reward introduces more noise into training than using the verifiable reward alone [1] — do not naively combine the two reward sources. A contributor to open-instruct (GitHub `author_association: CONTRIBUTOR`, not a repository member/owner) notes that the PPO/GRPO policy-gradient loss itself is not a reliable training signal for RLVR: it can be flat or fluctuate, and can even rise early in training, because the objective shifts as the online samples' advantages shift, so a non-decreasing pg_loss does not by itself indicate a broken run [16].
- Named successors / follow-on studies that qualify the method, not the definition: Spurious Rewards reports that RLVR training with GRPO can still improve MATH-500 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points using randomly assigned rewards, versus a 29.1-point gain from ground-truth rewards, and attributes much of this to GRPO's clipping bias amplifying pretraining-time behaviors rather than to the reward signal itself — a model-family-dependent effect that failed to reproduce on Llama3 or OLMo2 [4]. RLPR extends RLVR to domains without a hand-built verifier by using the policy's own token probability on a reference answer as the reward signal instead of an external checker [5].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own limitations, overoptimization at low $\beta$ (above) [1]; from The Invisible Leash's empirical study, RLVR's pass@1 gains come from a support-constrained optimization that concentrates probability mass on outputs already reachable by the base model, and under larger sampling budgets this shrinkage of empirical support outweighs any expansion, so RLVR can fail to recover correct answers the base model could previously reach at a wider sampling budget [6]. From the open-instruct GitHub issue tracker (repo allenai/open-instruct, searched for "RLVR" in closed issues, 24 results, 2026-08-09): issue #836 ("RLVR pg_loss goes up over training") got the contributor reply above about pg_loss not being a reliable signal [16]; issue #1473 reports vLLM-rollout-vs-local-policy logprob divergence growing over training even with the learning rate fixed to 0, and was closed as completed on 2026-02-25 with no comments recorded at the time of check [17].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own Table 4/5 ablations show RLVR produces targeted improvements on the domains it verifies (GSM8K, MATH, IFEval) while not guaranteeing an increase in the broader average score [1]. The Invisible Leash's analysis goes further: RLVR reliably improves precision (pass@1) but narrows exploration, so it should not be expected to expand what the base model is fundamentally capable of sampling, only to make already-reachable correct outputs more likely to be picked [6].
