# RRM

A recipe for training a less hackable reward model: build a causal graph that separates prompt-driven preference from response-only artifacts (length, format, boilerplate phrases), then augment the preference dataset with permuted-prompt triplets that break the correlation between those artifacts and the winner label.

**RRM** (Robust Reward Model) is a reward-model training method introduced by Liu et al. to address the failure of standard reward-model (RM) training to separate contextual signal from prompt-independent artifacts such as response length and format [1]. Its parent is standard pairwise-preference reward-model training, the Bradley-Terry-style approach used since InstructGPT, which fits a reward function by maximizing the log-likelihood of observed chosen-over-rejected labels on a fixed preference dataset [2]. RRM keeps that same training objective and data format but augments the training set: for each original triplet it forms new triplets by pairing a response with a response drawn from a different, permuted prompt, and relabels these as "non-contextual" (the response tied to the original prompt wins) or "neutral" (declared a tie), a labeling rule the paper derives from two d-separation properties of its causal graph [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13156 [1]. The paper gives two reasons for this design: traditional RM training cannot always tell whether an artifact is causally driving the preference label or merely correlated with the true contextual signal (Proposition 3.1) [1], and adding the augmented triplets provably removes any causal edge from the artifact variable to the label (Proposition 3.2) [1]. The name "RRM" collides with at least two unrelated 2025 papers (a rubric-based and a residual-reward-model method); this card is confirmed as the intended item by exact-title arXiv search combined with citation count - Semantic Scholar records 74 citing works for arXiv:2409.13156 versus single digits for the other two "RRM"-named papers, and the title match is exact [1].

The paper reports its own gains on Gemma-2-9b-it: average RewardBench accuracy rises from 80.61% (plain RM) to 84.15% (RRM), with the largest gains on "Chat Hard" (51.54% to 65.57%) and "Safety" (78.54% to 83.90%), at the cost of "Reasoning" (94.58% down to 90.62%) (Table 2) [1]. Downstream, a DPO policy trained on RRM-labeled preferences reaches an AlpacaEval-2 length-controlled win rate of 52.49% versus 33.46% for a policy trained on the plain RM, and an MT-Bench overall score of 8.31 versus 7.27, while generating shorter responses (1723 vs. 2416 characters) (Table 3) [1]. As of this check no fetched source names a publicly documented landmark system (e.g., a shipped Gemini or open frontier model) that adopts RRM specifically; the top citing papers by citation count on Semantic Scholar are surveys and other reward-hacking-mitigation papers, not deployment reports [3]. Lineage in one line: Bradley-Terry / InstructGPT reward-model training (2022 [2]) -> RRM's causal-augmentation recipe (2024 [1]), alongside sibling reward-hacking fixes ODIN (architecture-level length disentanglement, 2024 [4]) and length-controlled DPO (objective-level length penalty, 2024 [5]).

**When to pick it**: pick RRM when you are training or curating a pairwise/Bradley-Terry reward model from a preference dataset and want to reduce its sensitivity to response-only artifacts (length, format, boilerplate) before that reward model is used for anything downstream - best-of-N sampling, DPO-pair construction, or PPO-style RL [1]. It is a fix applied at reward-model training time, not at policy-training time: RRM's own related-work section contrasts itself with ODIN, which decomposes the reward into an additive quality term and a length term and enforces disentanglement between them during reward-model learning [1], and with length-controlled DPO, which leaves the reward model untouched and instead adds a length penalty directly into the RLHF objective as a post-hoc adjustment during policy optimization [1]. RRM is not an RL or preference-optimization algorithm itself - it produces training data and a reward model artifact, so it does not have a direct "online vs. offline" contrast the way GRPO or DPO do; the reward model it produces can feed either an offline pipeline (label DPO pairs, as the paper does [1]) or an online RL loop.

**Variant of**: pairwise/Bradley-Terry reward-model training [2].

**Data it needs**: a preference dataset of triplets (prompt, chosen response, rejected response); the paper's own run used the RLHFlow-curated mixture of eight sources (HH-RLHF-Helpful, SHP, HelpSteer, PKU-SafeRLHF, UltraFeedback, UltraInteract, Distilabel-Capybara, Distilabel-Orca) totaling roughly 700K pairs before augmentation [1]. RRM's permutation augmentation produces up to 14x more triplets from the same data; the paper filters this down using a first-pass RM's own predictions (keeping augmented triplets where the RM's predicted win probability disagrees with the ground-truth label by at least 0.2 on a random 50% subsample) to reach 2.4M training examples, and trains the reward model for 1 epoch with AdamW, learning rate 1e-6, batch size 128 [1]. RRM's own training loop is offline: it is a one-shot data-augmentation-plus-training pass over a static preference dataset, not sampled fresh from a live policy.

**Extra models**: none beyond the single reward model being trained - RRM's own definition needs no separate value network or frozen reference model at the reward-training stage. The paper trains a pairwise ranking model (a causal LM prompted with both responses and decoded for an "A"/"B" token, chosen over a plain Bradley-Terry scalar head for its reported empirical and theoretical edge) [1] initialized from Gemma-2-9b-it [1]. Downstream, if the RRM-trained reward model is then used to build a DPO run (as the paper does), that DPO stage needs its own frozen reference policy per DPO's own definition [1][6]; that model is not part of RRM itself.

**Shipped by**: no library implements RRM as a named trainer or off-the-shelf recipe as of this check - searches of trl's and verl's own documentation pages for "robust reward model" and "RRM" returned no matches [7][8], and a GitHub repository search for "RRM robust reward model" surfaces only one unofficial third-party reimplementation, not a maintained library entry [9]. Building it requires two pieces on top of an existing pairwise/BT reward-model trainer: a data-augmentation preprocessing step that implements the permutation-and-relabeling rule in Equation 5 of the paper, and (optionally, to match the paper exactly) a first-pass RM used purely to filter which augmented triplets are kept - no new loss function or sampling loop is required, since training still runs the trainer's existing pairwise/BT loss on the augmented dataset.

## How it works

The loop in one line: build a causal graph over (prompt, two responses, contextual signal, artifact, label), argue that the artifact-to-label edge is not always identifiable from natural preference data, then eliminate that edge by adding two families of augmented triplets to the training set before running standard reward-model training [1].

**The causal graph** [1] has six variables: prompt $X$; two responses $Y_1, Y_2$; a contextual signal $S \in \mathbb{R}$ that depends on the prompt and both responses; a context-free artifact $A \in \mathbb{R}$ that depends only on the two responses (length, format, certain n-grams); and the preference label $C \in \{0,1\}$, assumed to be generated only from $S$ and $A$. The paper's Proposition 3.1 shows that when $S$ and $A$ are correlated, a model trained only on natural data cannot distinguish the hypothesis "no causal edge $A \to C$" ($H_0$, the desired behavior) from "there is an edge $A \to C$" ($H_1$, reward hacking) [1]. Two d-separation facts under $H_0$ motivate the fix: $A \perp C \mid (Y_1, Y_2)$ and $A \perp C \mid S$ [1].

**The reward-model loss it trains with is unchanged**: standard Bradley-Terry log-likelihood over triplets $(x, y_w, y_l)$,

$$ \mathcal{L}(r_\phi, D_{hf}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D_{hf}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\left( r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l) \right) \right] $$

where $r_\phi(x,y)$ is the reward-model's scalar score for response $y$ to prompt $x$ [1]. In practice the paper trains a pairwise ranking model rather than a scalar-headed BT model: the prompt and both responses are formatted as a single string ("[CONTEXT] {x} [RESPONSE A] {y1} [RESPONSE B] {y2}") and the model's probability of decoding the token "A" is used as the estimate of $\hat{P}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$ [1]; response order is randomly flipped during training to remove positional bias [1]. RRM changes only what triplets go into $D_{hf}$, not this loss.

**Data augmentation** builds two new families of triplets from an original set of triplets $D_{hf} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, expanded with two random permutations $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ of the index set to give $\tilde{D}_{hf} = \{t^{(i)}, t^{(\sigma_1(i))}, t^{(\sigma_2(i))}\}_{i=1}^N$ [1]:

- **Non-contextuals**: pair a response that is contextual to prompt $x^{(i)}$ (i.e., $y_w^{(i)}$ or $y_l^{(i)}$) against a response drawn from a different, permuted example; the contextual response is always labeled the winner. Eight such triplets are listed in the paper's Equation 5, e.g. $(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_w^{(\sigma_1(i))}) \to$ chosen $= y_w^{(i)}$, and one using the original rejected response as the new chosen response against a non-contextual pair, e.g. $(x^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}, y_w^{(\sigma_1(i))}) \to$ chosen $= y_l^{(i)}$ [1].
- **Neutrals**: pair two responses that are both non-contextual to $x^{(i)}$ (drawn from the two permutations) and label the pair a tie, e.g. $(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(\sigma_1(i))}, y_w^{(\sigma_2(i))}) \to$ Tie [1].

Proposition 3.2 argues that training on the original data plus these augmented triplets forces $A \perp C \mid (Y_1,Y_2)$ and $A \perp C \mid S$ to hold, i.e. removes the causal edge $A \to C$ [1]. Worked example from the paper's own Figure 1: prompt $x^{(i)}$ = "What does RAM stand for in computing" with chosen response $y_w^{(i)}$ = "Random Access Memory", permuted against prompt $x^{(j)}$ = "What does CPU stand for in computing" with its own chosen response $y_w^{(j)}$ = "Central Processing Unit" - the non-contextual triplet $y_w^{(i)} \succ y_w^{(j)}$ sets the RAM answer as winner over the CPU answer purely because it is tied to the current prompt, regardless of the CPU answer's own quality, teaching the model that context, not the artifact-laden surface form, decides the label [1]. The paper reports the raw augmentation multiplies each original triplet by up to 14x, then filters most of it away using a first-pass RM's disagreement with ground truth (keeping only triplets where $|\hat{P}(A \succ B) - P^*(A \succ B)| \ge 0.2$ on a 50% subsample) to reach 2.4M final training examples from the original ~700K-pair mixture [1].

Two named related methods the paper positions itself against, using the same causal frame: the paper itself describes ODIN as a special case of its causal model with a single observed artifact (length), enforcing disentanglement in the model's learned reward instead of in the training data [1]; it describes length-controlled AlpacaEval-2's controlled-direct-effect adjustment as equivalent, for the single artifact of length, to what RRM's residual learning achieves more generally [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: the augmentation is a training-set expansion, not a change to the per-step forward/backward pass - RRM trains the exact same reward-model architecture and loss as the RM it replaces, so it adds no extra model and no extra forward pass per example relative to plain RM training [1]. The cost is entirely in dataset size: up to 14x more raw triplets before filtering [1], which the paper then subsamples down using an auxiliary first-pass RM inference pass over half the augmented data purely to decide what to keep [1] - that filtering pass is an extra one-time inference cost on top of standard RM training, not a per-training-step cost.

**In practice, per framework**: no framework ships RRM, so there is no framework-level cost report to cite; the paper's own compute description is the only source. It trains from Gemma-2-9b-it for 1 epoch with AdamW, learning rate 1e-6, batch size 128, and reports that 1 epoch is best by grid search [1]; the auxiliary filtering pass runs RM inference on a random 50% of the ~14x-augmented data before the final 2.4M-example training set is assembled [1]. No GPU-count, wall-clock, or memory figures are given in the fetched text.

## How to use it

- Data preparation: start from a standard triplet-format preference dataset (prompt, chosen, rejected). Implement the augmentation of Equation 5 - two independent random permutations of the dataset index, then construct the "non-contextual" and "neutral" triplets listed above - and optionally filter using a first-pass RM as the paper does, to control the 14x blow-up [1].
- Reward/label convention: the paper's non-contextuals use the response tied to the real prompt as the winner even when that response was originally the rejected one for its own prompt, on the stated assumption that a rejected response is still an acceptable response since it is model-generated, not a human-written negative [1]. The paper flags this assumption explicitly as a risk (see While it runs).
- Knobs, with the paper's own values as the only anchor (no framework implements RRM, so there is no framework-default column):

| knob | paper value [1] |
| --- | --- |
| epochs | 1 (grid-searched, found best) |
| optimizer | AdamW |
| learning rate | 1e-6 |
| batch size | 128 |
| raw augmentation multiplier | 14x |
| augmented-triplet filter threshold | $\lvert \hat{P}-P^* \rvert \ge 0.2$ on a 50% random subsample |
| final training set size | 2.4M examples (from ~700K original pairs) |

- Trade-off a run designer faces: how aggressively to filter the augmented triplets. The paper motivates filtering by noting most of the raw 14x augmented data is too easy for the RM to learn from [1]; too little filtering wastes compute on uninformative triplets, too much filtering (or none) was not itself ablated in the fetched text beyond the reported "-Neutrals" comparison, which shows dropping the neutral-tie triplets lowers first-turn MT-Bench (Table 3: DPO T1 8.70 with Neutrals vs. 8.65 without) and AlpacaEval-2 length-controlled win rate (52.49 with Neutrals vs. 51.73 without), while the overall MT-Bench score moves the other way (8.31 with Neutrals vs. 8.44 without) - decide which of these axes matters more for your use case rather than reading a single aggregate number [1].

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: this is a reward-model training method, not a live RL loop, so the paper's own monitoring signal is RewardBench category accuracy tracked across the standard axes (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, Reasoning) rather than a training-time curve [1]; no framework-specific logging metric is cited here because no framework ships this trainer.
- Published reference run: the paper's own Table 2 is the reference point - plain RM scores 97.77 / 51.54 / 78.54 / 94.58 / 80.61 (Chat / Chat Hard / Safety / Reasoning / Average) and RRM scores 96.51 / 65.57 / 83.90 / 90.62 / 84.15 on the same axes for a Gemma-2-9b-it base [1]. A reader reproducing RRM should expect this Reasoning-for-robustness trade-off, not a uniform win across categories.
- Degeneracies and defaults: the paper's own "Discussion on Data Filtering Strategies" (Appendix A.7) flags that the augmentation's "Non-contextuals" rule can promote a rejected response to chosen, and the "Neutrals" rule always assigns 0.5 win probability to a non-contextual pair - both can be unsafe if the promoted or tied response happens to be unsafe, since the augmentation currently has no safety-awareness [1]. The paper's proposed future fixes are to filter triplets using a trained safety scorer, or to use AI feedback (citing Constitutional AI) to keep only augmented triplets with high-quality, consistent chosen responses [1].
- Named successors: none identified in the fetched text - the paper's own related-work section frames RRM as generalizing ODIN and length-controlled AlpacaEval-2 to arbitrary, potentially unobserved artifacts rather than being superseded by them [1], and the conclusion names its own unresolved future work (filtering augmented pairs, matching artifacts when constructing response pairs) rather than pointing to a later paper [1].
- Known failure modes: from the paper's own Appendix A.7, discussed above - the augmentation can silently prefer an unsafe or low-quality response when it happens to be the "contextual" one in a non-contextual triplet, and ties assigned to non-contextual pairs assume 0.5 win probability even for pairs where a real preference (e.g., safety) should apply [1]. No GitHub issue thread was searched, since no library ships an RRM implementation to have issues against.
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper's own Table 2 shows the gain is concentrated in Chat Hard and Safety accuracy, at a measured cost to Reasoning accuracy (94.58% to 90.62%), which the authors hypothesize is because math/coding preferences are less driven by non-contextual artifacts and might be better served by verifiable/golden-answer rewards instead [1]. The Table 3 BoN and DPO results show RRM-induced policies produce shorter responses than RM-induced ones at equal or better MT-Bench/AlpacaEval-2 scores, which the paper reads as evidence of reduced length-hacking rather than any general capability gain [1]; a supplementary artifact-injection experiment (prepending "Sure, here is the response:" or appending emoji at varying rates) shows RRM-induced BoN policies are more robust to these injected artifacts than plain-RM-induced ones [1].
