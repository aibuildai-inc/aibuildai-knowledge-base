# PM

Swap standard RLHF's KL-to-reference penalty for a Shannon-entropy ("PM") regularizer, derived so that the exact optimum reproduces the reward model's own Bradley-Terry-Luce preference distribution instead of collapsing to the single highest-reward response.

**PM** (preference matching, also called PM RLHF) is a regularizer for the reward-maximization step of RLHF, introduced by Xiao, Li, Xie, Getzen, Fang, Long and Su to remove what the paper calls the algorithmic bias of KL-regularized RLHF [1]. Its parent is standard KL-regularized RLHF, the objective the paper writes as maximizing expected reward under a policy $\pi_\phi$ subject to a $\beta D_{KL}(\pi_\phi\|\pi_{ref})$ penalty against a frozen reference model, attributing this RLHF pipeline to InstructGPT [1][2]. PM RLHF keeps that same reward-maximization scaffold but replaces the KL penalty with a regularizer $R(\pi)=-\log\pi(y|x)$, obtained by solving a second-order ODE that is the necessary condition for the optimal policy to satisfy the preference-matching property [1]. Three reasons the paper gives for existing: unregularized reward maximization provably collapses to outputting only the single highest-reward response for every prompt, discarding minority preferences [1]; the paper proves standard KL RLHF's optimal policy does not equal the reward model's own Bradley-Terry-Luce/Plackett-Luce preference distribution, so it cannot satisfy the PM property [1]; and in an extreme case where the reference model's preference between two responses is near 0%/100%, KL RLHF's optimum is shown to converge to that same near-deterministic split regardless of what the reward model's preference actually is, a phenomenon the paper names preference collapse [1].

No adoption by a named production system was found in the paper or in a check of its citing papers (Method: Semantic Scholar's citations endpoint for arXiv:2405.16455, 50 results by relevance, titles scanned for a fix or extension of PM RLHF specifically; none found, see While it runs) [3]. The paper's own results, on a practical "conditional" variant defined in Section 4 (see How it works), are given as a PM-divergence metric (defined below) on held-out prompts: fine-tuning Llama-2-7B on the full-hh-rlhf dataset, conditional PM RLHF lowers PM divergence from 2.23 (KL RLHF) to 1.57, and fine-tuning OPT-1.3B lowers it from 1.16 to 0.68 - the paper reports these as roughly 29% and 41% reductions respectively (Table 1, Section 5.2) [1]. Lineage in one line: KL-regularized RLHF / InstructGPT (2022 [2]) -> PM RLHF (2024 [1]) -> no named successor identified as of this check.

**When to pick it**: a research or fairness-auditing setting where the reward model itself encodes graded, non-unanimous preferences (e.g. a 60%/40% split between two responses) and the goal is for the fine-tuned policy's own output probabilities to reproduce that split rather than collapse to the majority answer [1]; the paper positions this against ordinary KL RLHF [2], which it proves does not have this property [1], and against MaxMin RLHF [4], which targets fairness across distinct preference *subpopulations* rather than the reward model's own graded distribution within one population [1]. It is not a drop-in efficiency or capability upgrade over PPO-style RLHF; it targets a specific fairness/diversity property, and the paper itself only validates it on models up to 7B parameters (see How to use it).

**Variant of**: standard KL-regularized RLHF, which the paper attributes to InstructGPT [1][2], with the KL-to-reference term replaced by the PM regularizer [1].

**Data it needs**: the same data RLHF's reward-maximization stage needs - a prompt set plus a trained scalar reward model $r_\theta(x,y)$; the paper's own experiments split the human-preference dataset 20% for SFT, 40% for reward-model training, and 40% for the reward-maximization stage, and use PPO to run that stage [1]. On-policy: like the RLHF it modifies, PM RLHF optimizes $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|x)}[\cdot]$ over responses sampled from the policy being trained, and the paper's implementation uses PPO for this optimization [1].

**Extra models**: one frozen reference model, used two ways in the practical (conditional) variant - to define which responses get the ordinary PM penalty versus the pull-toward-reference penalty (via the threshold $\alpha$), and as the resampling baseline $\pi_{ref}(y|x)$ inside that penalty [1]; plus the reward model needed for any RLHF reward-maximization stage. The paper's own objective (Definition 3.5/4.1) contains no value-network term [1]; neither pm_full.txt nor the InstructGPT abstract uses the phrase "value network," so whether a value network is held by the PPO stage the paper's experiments run on top of is not stated by either source and is not covered by this card.

**Shipped by**: no library implementation was found. A check of trl's exported trainer list (`docs/source/_toctree.yml` at trl's `main` branch) finds `PPOTrainer`, `GRPOTrainer`, `DPOTrainer` and others but no PM/preference-matching trainer [5]; a check of verl's documentation index likewise found no reference to preference matching [6]. Building it would mean writing a custom regularizer term inside an existing PPO-style reward-maximization loop (replacing the loop's KL penalty with the conditional PM penalty of Definition 4.1 in [1]) - a loss modification on top of an existing on-policy RLHF trainer, not a new sampling loop.

## How it works

The loop is ordinary PPO-based RLHF reward-maximization with one term swapped: instead of penalizing $D_{KL}(\pi_\phi\|\pi_{ref})$, penalize $-\log\pi_\phi(y|x)$ (per response, above a probability threshold) or $-\log(\pi_\phi(y|x)/\pi_{ref}(y|x))$ (below it) [1].

**Deriving the regularizer.** The paper first asks: for a fixed prompt $x$ and reward $r(x,y)$, what functional form $R(\cdot)$ makes the exact maximizer of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)+R(\pi(y|x))]$ equal to the reward model's own Bradley-Terry-Luce/Plackett-Luce distribution $\pi^\star(y|x)=\exp(r(x,y))/\sum_{y'}\exp(r(x,y'))$ (Eq. 3.1-3.2, under two working hypotheses: the policy can represent any distribution, and training finds the exact optimum) [1]? Writing out the Lagrangian first-order condition over a multinomial policy on $k$ responses and requiring it to hold at $\pi^\star$ gives a second-order ODE, the "PM differential equation" [1]:

$$ \pi R''(\pi) + 2R'(\pi) + \frac{1}{\pi} = 0 $$

Solving it gives, up to constants that may depend on the prompt $x$ [1]:

$$ R(\pi) = -\log\pi + C_{1,x} + \frac{C_{2,x}}{\pi} $$

The paper's Theorem 1 states this form is necessary and sufficient for $R$ to be a PM regularizer [1]. Because $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[C_{2,x}/\pi(y|x)] = k\,C_{2,x}$ does not depend on the decision variable $\pi$, that term can be dropped without changing the optimum, and $-\log\pi(y|x)$ is exactly the (conditional) Shannon entropy of the policy at $x$; the paper's Definition 3.5 fixes PM RLHF as maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)-\log\pi_\phi(y|x)]$ (with $C_{1,x}=C_{2,x}=0$) [1].

**Why standard KL RLHF fails this property.** The paper shows the KL penalty $\beta D_{KL}(\pi_\phi\|\pi_{ref})$ is an instance of a more general response-dependent regularizer family that, by the same ODE argument (Theorem 2), *cannot* take the required $-\log\pi+C_{1,x}+C_{2,x,y}/\pi$ form for any choice of constants - so standard KL RLHF does not yield the preference-matching policy (Proposition 3.6) [1].

**Conditional PM RLHF, the version used in experiments.** A direct implementation of the plain PM regularizer produces text with very high perplexity - 7.15 versus 5.48 for KL RLHF when fine-tuning Llama-2-7B - because it does not penalize probability mass placed on ill-formed responses [1]. The paper's fix restricts the entropy penalty to a "regular" response set $\mathcal{M}(x):=\{y:\pi_{ref}(y|x)\ge\alpha\}$ and adds a KL-like pull toward the reference model outside it (Definition 4.1) [1]:

$$ \max_\phi\ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|x)}\Big[r(x,y) - \log\big(\pi_\phi(y|x)\big)\,\mathbb{1}(\pi_{ref}(y|x)\ge\alpha) - \log\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi_\phi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\Big)\,\mathbb{1}(\pi_{ref}(y|x)<\alpha)\Big] $$

The paper's Theorem 3 proves that for any choice of $\alpha$, the exact optimum restricted to $\mathcal{M}(x)$ still equals the reward model's own preference distribution renormalized over $\mathcal{M}(x)$: $\pi^\star(y\mid\mathcal{M}(x),x)=\exp(r(x,y))/\sum_{y'\in\mathcal{M}(x)}\exp(r(x,y'))$ [1]. $\alpha$ has one explicit tuning knob; the paper suggests setting it to $1-\text{top\_p}$, the nucleus-sampling cutoff used at SFT time, on the intuition that low-probability tail tokens are typically unnatural [1].

**Worked example.** Take two responses $y_1,y_2$ to one prompt with rewards $r(x,y_1)=1,\ r(x,y_2)=0$ (both $\ge\alpha$, so plain PM applies). The reward model's own preference is $\pi^\star(y_1)=e^1/(e^1+e^0)\approx 0.731$, $\pi^\star(y_2)\approx 0.269$ - a 73%/27% split, not a 100%/0% collapse. Under unregularized reward maximization (Proposition 3.3), the optimal policy instead puts all mass on $y_1$ [1]; the PM regularizer's entropy term is what pulls the optimum back to the 73%/27% split that mirrors the reward model's own comparison. The paper treats $\beta$ as a hyperparameter by using $r(x,y)=r_\theta(x,y)/\beta$ as the reward inside this same regularizer, since the theory itself requires $\beta=1$ [1].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: the objective (3.9)/(4.1) needs one forward pass through $\pi_\phi$ to compute $\log\pi_\phi(y|x)$ (already required for the reward-maximization step it modifies) and, for the conditional variant, one forward pass through the frozen reference model $\pi_{ref}$ to evaluate the indicator $\pi_{ref}(y|x)\ge\alpha$ and the ratio term [1]. Standard KL RLHF's own objective likewise needs a $\pi_{ref}$ forward pass to compute its KL penalty [1]; both objectives therefore require the same reference-model call, so the PM regularizer changes which scalar penalty is computed from an already-needed log-probability, not which models are held in memory.

**In practice, per framework**: not applicable - no framework implementation was found (see Shipped by), so no framework-specific cost claim can be made.

## How to use it

- Prompts and preference data: same as RLHF's reward-maximization stage - a prompt set and a trained scalar reward model $r_\theta(x,y)$. The paper trains the reward model with the standard Bradley-Terry negative log-likelihood on a comparison dataset $\{x^i, y_w^i, y_l^i\}$, with early stopping to limit over-optimization [1].
- Two tunable knobs, both defined only in this paper (no framework ships defaults to compare against):

| knob | role | paper's values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ (regular-set threshold) | responses with $\pi_{ref}(y\vert x)\ge\alpha$ get the plain PM penalty; below it, a KL-like pull to $\pi_{ref}$ | tested at 0.1, 0.3, 0.5; suggested default $1-\text{top\_p}$ [1] |
| $\beta$ (reward scale, $r=r_\theta/\beta$) | rescales the reward inside the same regularizer; theory requires $\beta=1$, treated as a hyperparameter in ablations | tested at 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 [1] |

  Across all three tested $\beta$ values, the lowest PM divergence in Table 1 comes from $\alpha=0.1$ or $\alpha=0.3$, not $\alpha=0.5$ [1] - so the paper's own sweep argues for a smaller regular-set threshold, not a larger one.
- Trade-off a run designer faces: raising $\alpha$ trades PM divergence for fluency, but the trade is not a clean monotonic slider. In Table 1, as $\alpha$ rises from 0.1 to KL RLHF's implicit $\alpha=1$, perplexity falls monotonically at every $\beta$ (e.g. at $\beta=1.0$: 10.73 at $\alpha=0.1$, 5.64 at $\alpha=0.3$, 5.08 at $\alpha=0.5$, 4.82 for KL RLHF), while PM divergence at the same $\beta=1.0$ row first drops from 1.71 ($\alpha=0.1$) to 1.57 ($\alpha=0.3$) and only then rises through 1.62 ($\alpha=0.5$) to 2.23 (KL RLHF) - the paper reports $\alpha=0.3$ (with $\alpha=0.1$ close behind) as the minimum-PM-divergence point across all three tested $\beta$ values, not the endpoint of a monotonic trend [1].
- Reward maximization itself is run with PPO in the paper's experiments, following the DeepSpeed-Chat framework's standardized configuration for each RLHF stage [1]; this card does not re-derive PPO's own knobs (see a PPO-specific card).

## While it runs

- **Signals and their healthy shapes**: the paper's own metric, PM divergence, is the quantity to watch. For a prompt with two sampled responses $y_1,y_2$, the LLM's own softmax-normalized preference $p_{llm}(y_i)=\pi_{llm}(y_i|x)/(\pi_{llm}(y_1|x)+\pi_{llm}(y_2|x))$ is compared against the reward model's implied preference $p_{reward}(y_i)$ via $D_{KL}(p_{llm}\|p_{reward})$ per instance, averaged over the test set; a perfectly preference-matching policy scores 0, and lower is better [1]. The paper explicitly argues win rate is the wrong signal here: a model that always predicts the majority response with 100% confidence can score a perfect win rate while still exhibiting total preference collapse, so win rate should not be read as evidence PM is working [1]. Perplexity, average generation length, output entropy $\mathbb{E}[-\log\pi_\phi(y|x)]$, and $D_{KL}(\pi_\phi\|\pi_{ref})$ are the paper's secondary diagnostics, reported alongside PM divergence in Tables 1-2 [1].
- **Published reference runs**: Table 1 (Llama-2-7B, full-hh-rlhf, $\beta\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$ against KL RLHF) and Table 2 (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B on the TL;DR summarization dataset) give known-good PM-divergence, length, perplexity, entropy and KL numbers to compare a run against [1]. At $\beta=1.0$: KL RLHF scores PM divergence 2.23 versus conditional PM RLHF's 1.57 ($\alpha=0.3$) - the gap that shows the method is doing something, analogous to a no-regularizer baseline in other methods' ablations [1].
- **Degeneracies and defaults**: on Llama-3.2-1B fine-tuned for summarization, PM divergence stays nearly flat across settings (1.75-1.76) regardless of $\alpha$ or whether KL RLHF is used instead - the paper's own reading is that a 1B-parameter model may simply be too small for the regularizer to move the outcome, and it explicitly leaves confirming this on larger models to future work [1]. The un-conditioned plain PM regularizer (no $\alpha$ threshold) is itself a documented failure mode in practice: it drives perplexity up to 7.15 (vs. 5.48 for KL RLHF on Llama-2-7B) by putting probability mass on ill-formed text, which is exactly why the conditional variant with $\mathcal{M}(x)$ and the $\pi_{ref}$-ratio penalty exists [1].
- **Named successors**: none identified. Method: the paper's Semantic Scholar citation list (50 papers, most recent/relevant order) was scanned by title for any paper naming or extending PM RLHF specifically; none of the 50 titles refer to preference matching or PM RLHF by name, though several address adjacent output-diversity or fairness topics [3].
- **Known failure modes**: from the paper's own Discussion/limitations - the choice of using the reference model's probability to define which responses count as "regular" natural language is called "somewhat arbitrary," and the paper states a more principled approach would likely improve empirical performance [1]; the paper also notes it did not test PM RLHF on industrial-scale LLMs (its largest model is 7B) and can only conjecture, not show, that larger models would show a bigger effect [1]. Separately, the paper's own root-cause discussion for KL RLHF's preference collapse (the problem PM RLHF is built to fix) is left unresolved: it observes the reference model's per-pair preference is very often near 0 or 1 rather than graded, and offers response-length differences as a possible but unconfirmed explanation, citing evidence from other papers rather than a settled cause [1].
- **What the gain is - and is not**: the paper's own claim is narrower than "better alignment" - it is that the conditional PM RLHF policy's own output probabilities more closely track the reward model's *existing* preference distribution (lower PM divergence), which the paper argues protects minority-preferred responses from being suppressed; it does not claim PM RLHF improves the reward model's accuracy, the policy's task capability, or win rate against KL RLHF, and it explicitly says win rate is the wrong lens for judging this method [1].
