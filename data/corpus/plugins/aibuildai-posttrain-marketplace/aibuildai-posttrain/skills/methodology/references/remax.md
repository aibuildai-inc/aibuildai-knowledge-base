# ReMax

REINFORCE with the baseline swapped for a single greedily-decoded response per prompt: no value network, no clipping, just reward-weighted log-likelihood minus a same-prompt greedy-decode reward.

**ReMax** is an online-RL method for fine-tuning an LLM policy against a per-response scalar reward, introduced in "ReMax: A Simple, Effective, and Efficient Reinforcement Learning Method for Aligning Large Language Models," which states it is "built upon the well-known REINFORCE algorithm" and does not require training an additional value model as PPO does [1]. The paper is at https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10505 [1]. Its parent is REINFORCE (Williams, 1987; 1992), cited by the paper itself rather than fetched independently for this card [1][2]. The mechanism: for each prompt, sample one response from the policy and, separately, greedily decode a second response from the same policy; subtract the reward of the greedy response from the reward of the sampled response to form the baseline-subtracted return, and take an ordinary policy-gradient step on that single scalar per prompt, with no importance ratio and no clipping [1]. The paper gives three reasons ReMax exists: RLHF for LLMs has fast simulation, deterministic transitions and a trajectory-level (single, end-of-sequence) reward, three properties PPO does not exploit; a value network is hard to fit well when only the last token carries a reward signal; and plain REINFORCE's gradient variance grows with the reward scale, which varies widely across prompts and destabilizes training without a baseline [1].

Landmark adoption is limited: the paper's own reference implementation is a research repository rather than a maintained library [3], and the method is shipped as one advantage estimator inside verl's PPO-family trainer, with its own example directory `examples/remax_trainer` [4]. The paper's own gains: applying ReMax to Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 with the UltraRM-13B reward model reached 94.78% AlpacaEval win rate against text-davinci-003 and a 7.739 MT-bench score, versus 92.78%/7.516 for the SFT baseline before RL (Table 3/Table 4) [1]; on Llama-2-7B with full-hh-rlhf, ReMax gave the largest win-rate improvement over the SFT baseline among REINFORCE/PPO/DPO/ReMax at 31.4 points, and initializing from a DPO checkpoint and then fine-tuning with ReMax reached the paper's highest win rate of 84.7% (Figure 7) [1]. Lineage in one line: REINFORCE (Williams, 1987/1992 [2]) -> ReMax (2023 [1]), positioned as a lighter-weight alternative to PPO (Schulman et al., 2017 [5]) rather than a fork of it -> shipped inside verl's PPO-family trainer as one more `adv_estimator` choice alongside GRPO, RLOO and others [4]. The paper was confirmed as the correct match by the shortlist's own top-cited-multi-candidate check against one alternate candidate (an unrelated 2026 exploration paper with 6 citations), and independently by verl's `core_algos.py` docstring, which cites this exact arXiv ID as the basis of its `compute_remax_outcome_advantage` function [6].

**When to pick it**: pick ReMax when responses can be scored with a scalar reward (a reward model or a programmatic scorer) and you want an online, on-policy method without a value network and without PPO's clipped-ratio machinery — its own cost is one extra greedy-decode generation per prompt, not a second trained model [1]. Prefer its parent, REINFORCE without a baseline, only as a simpler-still fallback: the paper's own Llama-2-7B experiments show plain REINFORCE's mini-batch rewards ranging from -14.25 to 7.25 early in training and still -8.125 to 7.56 after a full epoch, i.e., without ReMax's baseline the paper's own numbers show REINFORCE remains high-variance across prompts [1]. Prefer PPO [5] when you want a trained per-token value network and a clipped, potentially multi-epoch update on each rollout batch; the ReMax paper positions itself as the lighter-weight alternative for RLHF specifically because PPO's three general-RL assumptions (stochastic transitions, dense reward, no fast simulator) do not hold for LLM fine-tuning [1]. Prefer DPO [7] as the nearest offline alternative when you only have static preference pairs and no reward model to sample against: DPO's objective is derived under a Bradley-Terry-Luce preference assumption plus an explicit KL penalty against a reference model, updates entirely offline from a fixed pair dataset, and the ReMax paper reports that this offline dataset creates an out-of-distribution generalization problem that ReMax, by sampling fresh from the current policy every step, avoids [1]. The nearest online neighbor is RLOO [8]: its baseline is the leave-one-out mean of G−1 other sampled completions to the same prompt drawn in one batch, so it needs a group of G≥2 samples and no extra generation call, whereas ReMax needs exactly one extra, deterministic greedy-decode generation per prompt independent of any group size [9].

**Variant of**: REINFORCE (Williams, 1987; 1992), as the paper itself states [1][2]; proposed as a lighter-weight, non-PPO alternative for the same RLHF Step-3 slot that PPO [5] traditionally fills, not as a modification of PPO's own objective.

**Data it needs**: a prompt-only dataset from the target distribution, plus any scoring function that returns one scalar reward per complete response — the paper's own RL stage used the full-hh-rlhf preference dataset's prompts (Llama-2-7B/OPT-1.3B experiments) and a mix of ultrafeedback, lmsys-chat-1m and sharegpt-en prompts at 10k/20k/40k scale (Mistral-7B experiments) [1]. On-policy: both the scored response and the greedy baseline response are sampled fresh from the current policy every step; the method cannot train on a pre-collected response set [1].

**Extra models**: no value network, ever — this is the method's headline saving over PPO [1]. A reward model (or equivalent scorer) is required to score both the sampled and the greedy response. A frozen reference model for KL regularization is optional in the method's own definition: the paper's Eq. (1) objective omits KL for simplicity, and Appendix B shows how to fold a KL term against a reference model back into the reward before it enters Algorithm 1's loss, using the coefficient 0.1 the paper's own Llama-2-7B experiments used for both PPO and ReMax [1]. verl's shipped example keeps this KL term structurally on (`algorithm.use_kl_in_reward=True`) but its script sets the coefficient to 0.0, so no KL penalty is actually applied in that particular run [10] — see Cost and While it runs for the framework's own defaults.

**Shipped by**: verl, via `algorithm.adv_estimator=remax`; `compute_remax_outcome_advantage` in `verl/trainer/ppo/core_algos.py` implements the paper's advantage exactly and cites the paper's own arXiv ID in its docstring [6]. The verifiable, currently-existing launch path is the shared `verl.trainer.main_ppo` module (`run_ppo`) [11]; one of the two example scripts in `examples/remax_trainer/`, `run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh`, instead launches via `python3 -m verl.trainer.main_ppo_sync` [10], a module that GitHub's contents API returns "Not Found" for on the current `main` branch [12]. That module's own commit history shows it was created by PR #5401 ("feat: add new trainer with TransferQueue," merged 2026-04-10) [13], later modified — not created — by PR #6340 to add ReMax support [14], and removed by PR #6710 ("unify trainer abstraction for sync and async training," 2026-06-12) [15] — copying that script's launch command as written will fail with `ModuleNotFoundError`; use `main_ppo` instead. The directory's other script, `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh`, is listed first in the README's own canonical-scripts table and already launches via the working `python3 -m verl.trainer.main_ppo` invocation, making it the safe default over the broken sync script [16]. Beyond the examples directory, unlike GRPO, DAPO or PPO, ReMax has no dedicated page under verl's `docs/algo/` documentation tree — only the examples README, a comparison-table entry, and the source itself [4][17]. trl exports no ReMax or REINFORCE-family trainer: its top-level package and `trainer/` directory list only DPO, GRPO, KTO, reward, RLOO and SFT trainers, with no `remax`/`reinforce` entry point [18]. The paper's own reference implementation exists as a research repository (202 stars, not archived, last pushed 2023-12-16) but is not positioned as a maintained library [3].

## How it works

The loop, per step: for each prompt, sample one response from the current policy and separately greedily decode a second response from the same policy; score both with the reward model; take the sampled response's reward minus the greedy response's reward as a single per-prompt advantage; take one gradient step on the reward-weighted negative log-likelihood of the sampled response [1].

**Objective (Eq. 1)**: ReMax maximizes the same expected-reward objective as REINFORCE, $\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\,\mathbb{E}_{a_{1:T}\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(x, a_{1:T})\big]$, where $x$ is the prompt, $a_{1:T}$ the sampled response, $\pi_\theta$ the policy, and $r(x,a_{1:T})$ a single scalar reward returned only once the full response is complete [1]. The paper omits an explicit KL term from Eq. (1) for simplicity and treats it separately in Appendix B [1].

**Score function and REINFORCE gradient (Eqs. 6-7)**: with score function $s_\theta(x,a_{1:t}) = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid x, a_{1:t-1})$, the plain REINFORCE gradient estimator over a batch of $N$ prompts is $\hat g(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T} s_\theta(x_i, a^i_{1:t})\, r(x_i, a^i_{1:T})$, with $a^i_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i, a^i_{1:t-1})$ [1].

**Baseline-subtracted estimator (Eq. 8) and ReMax's baseline (Eq. 9)**: $\tilde g(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T} s_\theta(x_i, a^i_{1:t}) \big(r(x_i, a^i_{1:T}) - b_\theta(x_i)\big)$, and ReMax sets $b_\theta(x_i) = r(x_i, a^i_{1:T})$ where $a^i_t \in \operatorname*{argmax}\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i, a^i_{1:t-1})$ — the reward of a greedily decoded response to the same prompt [1]. The method's name comes from combining REINFORCE with this argmax/"max" baseline [1]. Proposition 1 in the paper shows this estimator stays unbiased for the Eq. (1) objective, because unbiasedness only requires the baseline to be statistically independent of the sampled response, which a separately greedy-decoded sample satisfies [1].

**Update rule**: $\theta_{k+1} \leftarrow \theta_k + \eta_k \tilde g(\theta_k)$ — one plain gradient-ascent step, no importance-sampling ratio between an old and new policy and no clipping, because the update uses the current policy's own log-probabilities directly [1]. Algorithm 1 in the paper writes this as six lines of pseudocode: sample a response and a greedy response, compute `rew = rm(prompt, seq) − rm(prompt, seq_max)`, compute `logp = lm.inference(prompt, seq)`, and minimize `loss = −(logp.sum(dim=−1) * rew).mean()` [1].

**Worked example**: for one prompt, suppose the reward model scores the sampled response at $r=0.8$ and the greedy response at $r_{\max}=0.5$; the per-prompt advantage is $0.8-0.5=0.3$, and every token of the sampled response is weighted by that same $+0.3$ in the reward-weighted log-likelihood loss (the paper's formula sums log-probabilities over the whole sequence, so the $0.3$ scales one scalar per response, not per token individually) [1]. If instead the sampled response scores below the greedy one, e.g. $r=0.2, r_{\max}=0.5$, the advantage is $-0.3$ and the update pushes probability mass away from that sampled response.

The paper defines only this single (outcome, per-response) form of the estimator — there is no separate process-supervision variant as in some contemporaries [1]. verl's shipped `compute_remax_outcome_advantage` matches this outcome-only form: it turns the token-level rewards into a return via a reverse cumulative sum and then subtracts the greedy-response reward (`reward_baselines`) from every response token, `advantages = returns - reward_baselines.unsqueeze(-1) * response_mask` [6].

## Cost

**Theory, from the method's own math**: per prompt, generation cost is two full response rollouts instead of one — the sampled response and the greedy baseline response — while training touches only the policy network, with no value-network forward/backward pass at all [1]. The paper writes this trade-off explicitly as $T_G + T_B = t_{gene} + 2\,t_{back}$ for PPO (one generation, but both policy and value model trained) versus $T_G + T_B = 2\,t_{gene} + t_{back}$ for ReMax (two generations, only the policy trained) [1]. Memory: ReMax removes one whole trained model relative to PPO — the paper's own footnote states that for a 7B model, the value model and its training components (activations, gradients, optimizer states) consume 46% of GPU memory versus 4% for the (inference-only) reward model [1]. A naive reading of "two generations" might suggest ReMax is always slower than PPO; the paper's own measurements (below) show the opposite once the value model's backward pass is removed, because in its experiments generation was faster than backward computation [1].

**In practice, per framework**:

- verl [4][6][10][19]: `algorithm.adv_estimator=remax` is not the default (`gae` is) and must be set explicitly; the paper-mandated KL term is toggled independently via `actor_rollout_ref.actor.use_kl_loss` and `algorithm.use_kl_in_reward`, and per verl's own comment, "Reference model will be enabled when actor.use_kl_loss or/and algorithm.use_kl_in_reward is/are True" — i.e. the frozen reference model is only loaded into memory when one of those flags is on, matching the method's own optional-KL definition [19]. verl's shared actor config applies its usual clipped-ratio PPO-style loss surface to every `adv_estimator` choice, including `clip_ratio: 0.2` by default, with no ReMax-specific override found in the actor config; this diverges from the paper's own Algorithm 1 loss, which has no clipping or importance ratio at all, so verl's shipped ReMax loss is a clipped-PPO-style loss run on ReMax's advantage rather than the paper's plain reward-weighted likelihood [20]. verl's own ray_trainer generates the sampled and greedy responses inside a single combined rollout request per training step specifically to avoid sending a second generation request after vLLM replicas have been put to sleep [21].
- No other framework ships ReMax as of this reading: trl's exported trainers (DPO, GRPO, KTO, reward, RLOO, SFT) do not include it [18].

## How to use it

- Prompts: any prompt-only dataset from the target task distribution; no response or preference annotation is needed, only a working scorer [1].
- Reward: one scalar per complete response, called on both the sampled response and a separate greedy decode of the same prompt; the paper defines the advantage as their difference, `rm(prompt, seq) − rm(prompt, seq_max)` [1]. verl computes this via its reward manager on both the sampled and the greedy-decoded outputs and stores the greedy reward as `reward_baselines` on the batch before calling the advantage function [6][21].
- Key knobs, with each source's own value ("not stated" = the source was checked and does not give it; "not checked" = this card did not verify that source's key):

| knob | verl default [19][22] | verl `remax_trainer` example [10] | paper (Llama-2-7B RL stage) [1] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| adv_estimator | `gae` (must be set to `remax`) | `remax` | n/a (ReMax is the algorithm) |
| launch entry point | `verl.trainer.main_ppo` (confirmed present [11]) | `run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh` launches via `verl.trainer.main_ppo_sync`, which does not exist on the current `main` branch and raises `ModuleNotFoundError` as written [10][12][15]; `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh` launches via the working `main_ppo` and is the README's first-listed script [16] | n/a |
| KL coefficient | `use_kl_in_reward: False`; `kl_ctrl.kl_coef: 0.001` when enabled | `run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh`: `use_kl_in_reward=True`, `kl_ctrl.kl_coef=0.0` (structurally on, numerically no penalty) [10]; `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh`: `use_kl_in_reward=True`, `kl_ctrl.kl_coef=0.001` (matches the verl default) [16] | 0.1 (same coefficient used for PPO in the same experiment) |
| clip_ratio | 0.2 | not checked (inherits actor default) | not applicable — Algorithm 1's loss has no clip |
| learning rate | `actor.optim.lr: 1e-6` | `1e-6` in both scripts (`actor_lr=1e-6`) [10][16] | `1e-6` (RL stage, cosine decay, no warm-up) |
| rollout group size (`rollout.n`) | 1 | 4 in both scripts (`ROLLOUT_N` default) [10][16] | not applicable — Algorithm 1 samples exactly one scored response plus one greedy response per prompt |
| sampling temperature | not checked | not checked | 1 (RL training), 0.7 (AlpacaEval evaluation generation only — a different, non-training setting) |

  Two readings of that table: the learning rate agrees across the paper and both verl examples at `1e-6`, well below a typical SFT rate, so carrying over an SFT learning rate is a live mistake here just as with other RL post-training methods. The KL coefficient shows the two example scripts disagree with each other: `run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh` leaves the KL-in-reward flag on but its coefficient at 0.0, applying no actual KL penalty despite superficially matching the paper's toggle, while `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh` — the working, README-recommended script — sets a nonzero `kl_ctrl.kl_coef=0.001`, still two orders of magnitude below the paper's own 0.1; a user who wants the paper's own regularization strength must set `kl_ctrl.kl_coef` explicitly regardless of which script they start from.
- Batch/rollout trade-off: raising `rollout.n` above 1 to sample multiple responses per prompt is not part of the method's own definition (Algorithm 1 samples exactly one scored response per prompt), and verl's issue tracker confirms this combination was previously broken: issue #3408 reported a tensor batch-size mismatch when running ReMax with `n>1`, and the maintainer response points to a merged fix (PR #3779) that changed how the sampled and greedy generations are combined so they no longer collide when multiple samples per prompt are requested [23][24].
- Install, launch flags beyond the algorithm-selecting keys above, and general framework operation belong to the verl framework card, not this one.

## While it runs

- Signals and their healthy shapes: this card found no ReMax-specific first-hand monitoring guidance (crucial-values list, logged metric names) published by verl or by the paper itself beyond what the training curves in the paper show — the paper's Figure 6 plots training gradient norm for DPO, PPO and ReMax on the same axes as a diagnostic, and its Figure 4 (OPT-1.3B, full-hh-rlhf) shows plain REINFORCE (no baseline) with markedly larger gradient-norm variance and a worse evaluation-reward curve than ReMax over the same training run, which is the paper's own evidence that the greedy baseline is what stabilizes training [1].
- Published reference runs: verl's own baseline table reports a GSM8K test score of 97 for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct trained with `adv_estimator=remax`, with a linked launch script and a linked Weights & Biases run log for that exact run [17].
- Degeneracies and defaults: the KL term is off by default in verl (`use_kl_in_reward: False`) [19], and the two shipped example scripts disagree once it is turned on — `run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh` leaves `kl_ctrl.kl_coef` at 0.0, so nothing pulls the policy toward a reference model in that configuration, while `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh` sets `kl_ctrl.kl_coef=0.001` [10][16] — both well below the paper's own experiments, which always ran KL at 0.1 for both PPO and ReMax [1]. Running ReMax with `rollout.n>1` used to raise an assertion error from a tensor-batch-size mismatch between the sampled and greedy generations, fixed by PR #3779 after issue #3408 [23][24]; a separate, still-unresolved issue reports a similar batch-mismatch error when combining ReMax with multi-turn rollouts, with no maintainer fix confirmed at the time of this reading [25]. One of the two shipped example scripts (`run_qwen2.5_math_7b_sync_fsdp.sh`) launches via a module, `verl.trainer.main_ppo_sync`, that no longer exists on the current `main` branch — created by PR #5401, later modified by PR #6340 to add ReMax support, and removed by PR #6710 [13][14][15] — so running that script as written silently fails to start rather than running ReMax at all; the other script, `run_qwen3_8b_fsdp.sh`, already launches via the working `main_ppo` and is the README's recommended default [10][12][16].
- Named successors: none identified from the sources checked for this card (verl's docs/algo directory and the paper's own citations) — a search of verl's `docs/algo/` file listing found dedicated pages for GRPO, DAPO, PPO and several others but none for ReMax or a documented ReMax variant [17].
- Known failure modes: the paper's own limitations statement, at the end of its main text, says ReMax "does not address all issues of RLHF," naming reward-function inference from human preferences and reward-bias mitigation as open problems it does not solve [1]. From verl's closed issues on the `verl-project/verl` repository (searched for "remax" in title/body; the search API returned 34 total results, of which 30 were retrieved in the one successfully paginated results page opened for this card — two earlier attempts at retrieving further pages returned "422 Validation Failed" and were not retried; of the 30 opened, 9 are non-PR issues): a maintainer (HollowMan6) confirmed the `rollout.n>1` batch-size bug above and pointed to its fix [23][24]; the same maintainer separately confirmed, in issue #3089, that `RayPPOTrainer.fit()` had at some point dropped the code block that generates the greedy baseline response and computes `reward_baselines`, so that particular version of the trainer silently could not run ReMax at all until that generation block was restored to match `recipe/entropy/entropy_ray_trainer.py`, closing the issue once the block reappeared, with the batch-size-mismatch part of the same report resolved by the same PR #3779 [26]; the multi-turn-rollout batch-mismatch issue received no maintainer reply confirming a cause at the time of this reading, so it is reported here only as an open, unconfirmed report, not a settled failure mode [25].
- What the gain is - and is not: the paper positions ReMax's contribution as computational and memory efficiency and training stability relative to plain REINFORCE and PPO, not as a claim of higher achievable reward ceiling; its own headline results (94.78% AlpacaEval, 7.739 MT-bench) are reported as matching or exceeding PPO/DPO-trained models of comparable or larger size at lower training cost, not as evidence that ReMax reaches capability unreachable by those methods [1].
